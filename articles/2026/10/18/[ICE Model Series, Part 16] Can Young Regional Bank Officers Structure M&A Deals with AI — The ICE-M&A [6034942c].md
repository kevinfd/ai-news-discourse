# [ICE Model Series, Part 16] Can Young Regional Bank Officers Structure M&A Deals with AI? — The "ICE-M&A Training Model" Practiced at Kofu Precision Industry

note · チラシの裏紙メモ（Note · 2026-10-18

[ICE Model Series, Part 16] Can Young Regional Bank Officers Structure M&A Deals with AI? — The "ICE-M&A Training Model" Practiced at Kofu Precision Industry
From corporate valuation to buyer search, price negotiation, and loan assessment. A practical simulation of business succession support where AI, ICE, and bank officers collaborate
Introduction: Avoiding Leaving M&A to "Experts Alone"
One of the vital roles of regional banks is to connect the businesses of local companies to the next generation.
However, M&A for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) is fraught with difficulties.
How should a company's value be assessed?
Where can potential buyers be found?
Is the seller's asking price reasonable?
If acquisition funds are loaned, can they be repaid?
To examine these questions, it is necessary to understand not only financial analysis but also the company's technology, business partners, the owner's intentions, and future business development.
Therefore, it is not easy for inexperienced bank officers to structure M&A deals on their own.
That is why I would like to propose an M&A training system for regional banks utilizing AI and the ICE model.
This is not a mechanism that lets AI decide on loan approvals or the success or failure of an M&A deal.
It is an auxiliary tool for bank officers to input company information and engage in dialogue with AI to examine corporate value and potential buyers.
In Part 15, we used a fictional company, "Kofu Precision Industry," to verify AI-driven cross-industry buyer searches, ICE scores, corporate value, and loan repayment capacity. In Part 16, we will use the same company to simulate training where young bank officers actually structure a deal. Note that this article is a design proposal for a training system and does not indicate results achieved through implementation and verification at actual regional banks.
Chapter 1: Why is the ICE-M&A Training Model Necessary for Regional Banks?
1. Corporate value cannot be determined by financial statements alone. When bank officers visit a company, financial statements are an important source of information. By checking sales, profits, borrowings, and equity, one can grasp the current financial state. However, that is not enough. For example, even if a precision machining company possesses unique processing technology, financial statements alone cannot determine which industries will require that technology in the future. Conversely, even if current profits are high, there is significant risk to future earnings if the company is dependent on specific business partners.
In the ICE model, in addition to a company's financial information, we systematically organize factors such as technical capability, competitiveness, growth potential, AI utilization potential, and speed of commercialization. However, such qualitative evaluations involve the officer's judgment. That is precisely why a mechanism is needed to record evaluation items and the reasons for scoring so that other officers can verify them.
2. Supplementing the lack of experience of young officers. In traditional M&A, the knowledge of experienced officers and specialized departments plays a major role. In the ICE-M&A training model, rather than replacing all that knowledge with AI, we standardize the order in which officers think.
Specifically, the flow is: Understand the seller company → Create an ICE score → Verify corporate value → Search for potential buyers → Verify synergies by buyer → Confirm loan repayment capacity → Decide whether to proceed with the deal. The important thing is that officers do not simply adopt the AI's answers as they are, but become able to explain why that evaluation was reached.
Corporate Understanding → ICE Score → Corporate Value → Buyer Search → Synergies → Loan Verification → Final Decision
Chapter 2: Basic Information on the Training Company 'Kofu Precision Industry'
We will use the fictional company 'Kofu Precision Industry' again this time.
Basic Financial Data
Item | Setting Value Annual Sales | 1.2 billion yen Net Income | 80 million yen Equity | 400 million yen ROE | 20% EBITDA | 160 million yen Annual FCF | 100 million yen Seller's Desired Share Price | 1 billion yen Assumed Net Interest-Bearing Debt | 200 million yen
*All financial figures are assumptions for training purposes. In practice, FCF and the source of funds for loan repayment are not necessarily the same.
Kofu Precision Industry is set up as a company with high-precision metal processing technology, supplying parts to semiconductor manufacturing equipment, medical devices, industrial robots, and automotive-related companies.
The management is elderly, and no successor has been decided.
They are looking for a buyer who can maintain employee employment and inherit the technology.
The seller's desired share price is 1 billion yen.
What the staff member should check first
Before entering company information into the AI, the bank staff member needs to confirm the following points.
What is the purpose of the sale?
The appropriate buyer differs depending on whether it is for the owner's retirement or a capital alliance for growth investment.
What is the subject of the transfer?
Valuation and contract terms change depending on whether it is all shares or a part of the business.
What are the conditions that must be protected?
Organize conditions other than price, such as employee employment, relationships with business partners, and the maintenance of the company name and factory.
At this stage, clearly distinguish between the facts confirmed by the staff member and the content inferred by the AI.
Chapter 3: Creating an ICE Score — What do the points mean?
In Part 15, we calculated Kofu Precision Industry's ICE score using three comparison methods.
Comparison Method | ICE Score | Based on one listed company in the same industry | 74.5 points | Listed companies in the same industry + industry average | 78.8 points | Composite criteria including different industries | 77.3 points
These scores are examples based on hypothetical benchmarks and scoring conditions.
They are not verified scores calculated from actual corporate data.
The most important question in the training
The staff member asks the AI the following: 'Why does the ICE score change depending on the comparison method? Please explain the differences in scoring items and comparison targets.' If the AI only answers with the score here, the evaluation is insufficient. In the training model, at least the following information must be displayed: 
Evaluation items and point allocation, comparison targets used, reasons for choosing the comparison targets, sources of input numerical values, grounds for qualitative judgment, and uncertainty due to lack of information. Furthermore, even if the evaluation of technical capabilities and customer base is high, if there is a shortage of successor engineers, the risk in business succession increases.
The ICE score is a tool for organizing these strengths and weaknesses.
It is not meant to be used for mechanical judgments such as 'buy because it is 80 points' or 'cannot provide a loan because it is 70 points.'
Also, the ICE score cannot be directly converted into a monetary amount.
The evaluation of corporate characteristics based on the score and the valuation of corporate value based on profit and cash flow should be considered separately.
Chapter 4: Verifying the distortion between the ICE theoretical value and the requested price of 1 billion yen
Next, the staff member verifies whether the seller's requested price of 1 billion yen is reasonable.
In the basic corporate valuation of the ICE model, the relationship between profit and the benchmark P/E ratio is used.
Theoretical Equity Value = Normalized Net Income × Benchmark P/E Ratio
Assuming Kofu Precision Industry's net income is 80 million yen and the assumed benchmark P/E ratio is 10x,
80 million yen × 10x = 800 million yen
This is the result.
In this case, the distortion from the requested price of 1 billion yen is,
Distortion = Requested Equity Price ÷ Theoretical Equity Value
1 billion yen ÷ 800 million yen = 1.25 times
.
The asking price is 25% higher than the reference theoretical value based on this assumption.
However, the base P/E ratio of 10x has no empirical backing and is an assumption for explanation purposes.
For private companies, it is also necessary to verify differences in business scale, stock liquidity, customer concentration, dependence on management, and financial risk.
Alternative verification using DCF
The reference stock value from the simplified DCF shown in Part 15 was approximately 737 million yen.
Valuation Method | Reference Stock Value | Deviation from Asking Price Earnings Multiplier Method using ICE Basic Formula | 800 million yen | 1.25 times Previous Simplified DCF Estimate | 737 million yen | Approx. 1.36 times Seller's Asking Price | 1 billion yen |
DCF is a different valuation method based on future cash flows.
Therefore, you must not explain the 737 million yen from the DCF as a price calculated from the ICE score.
Furthermore, if detailed conditions such as the DCF discount rate, growth rate, and forecast period are not sufficiently presented, the 737 million yen remains only a provisional reference value.
Judgment in training
Instead of justifying the 1 billion yen asking price as is, the staff member explains it as follows:
'Under current assumptions, the reference stock value is in the 700 million to 800 million yen range. However, the valuation may change due to buyer-specific synergies or detailed financial due diligence.'
If they can provide this explanation, the staff member will have a starting point for price negotiations.

Chapter 5: AI searches for buyer candidates in different industries
Once the verification of corporate value is complete, search for buyer candidates.
Here, we ask the AI the following questions.
"Please list buyer industries that can utilize Kofu Precision Industry's processing technology, customer base, and quality control capabilities, and explain the acquisition purpose and risks for each."
The following four industries are potential candidates that AI might suggest.
Semiconductor manufacturing equipment manufacturers
Stable procurement of high-precision parts and the internalization of processing technology could be acquisition objectives.
On the other hand, responding to fluctuations in semiconductor capital investment and quality requirements is a challenge.
Medical device manufacturers
There is a possibility that precision processing technology could be utilized for medical device parts.
However, compliance with quality assurance and regulations is required.
Industrial robot manufacturers
There is a possibility of incorporating manufacturing technology for precision drive parts and mechanical components.
However, it is necessary to confirm suitability for product specifications and mass production systems.
Automotive-related parts manufacturers
There is a possibility that processing technology could be utilized in the fields of electrification and high-precision parts.
However, price competition and the burden of capital investment become challenges.
A 'reason for suitability' is necessary when selecting candidate companies
What we want to note here is that AI simply listing industries does not mean that an actual buyer has been discovered.
To truly qualify as a buyer candidate, it is necessary to confirm at least acquisition needs, financial strength, business compatibility, and management policy.
Also, you must not input client information or non-public information held by the bank into external AI without confirming the terms of use.
In training, we use fictional data, and in actual practice, we assume information management and authorization settings.
Chapter 6: Calculating Corporate Value by Buyer
After searching for potential buyers, we verify how much added value will be generated after the acquisition.
In the previous assumption, the reference stock values for each buyer industry were as follows.
Buyer Industry | Reference Stock Value Semiconductor Manufacturing Equipment | 716 million yen Medical Equipment | 710 million yen Industrial Robots | 733 million yen Automotive Parts | 710 million yen
*All figures are estimates based on assumed synergies and integration risks for hypothetical buyer profiles.
In these results, none of the four industries reached the seller's asking price of 1 billion yen.
Even the highest reference value, in the industrial robot sector, is 733 million yen.
In other words, under these conditions, simply increasing the number of potential buyers will not solve the price issue.
How should the staff member respond?
There are three possible options.
1. Propose that the seller reconsider the price conditions.
2. Verify the added value for each buyer using more concrete business plans.
3. If the conditions cannot be met, postpone the sale for the time being.
What is important here is not to make the AI justify the desired price.
It is important to be able to explain the result that the price does not match to both the seller and the buyer.

Chapter 7: Can regional banks provide financing for acquisitions?
When considering M&A, one must not confuse corporate value with loan feasibility.
Even if the acquisition price is reasonable, if the loan cannot be repaid after the acquisition, financing becomes difficult.
Therefore, staff members use metrics such as DSCR to verify repayment capacity.
DSCR = Annual Repayment Source ÷ Annual Principal and Interest Repayment Amount
Assuming the annual repayment source is 100 million yen,
Annual Principal and Interest Repayment | DSCR 80 million yen | 1.25x 100 million yen | 1.00x 120 million yen | approx. 0.83x
This is the result.
If the annual repayment amount is 80 million yen, the repayment source covers the annual repayment amount by 1.25 times.
On the other hand, if the annual repayment amount becomes 120 million yen, the repayment source alone will be insufficient.
However, just because the DSCR is 1.25x does not mean a loan can necessarily be granted.
Banks also check existing debt, capital investment after acquisition, working capital, the status of collateral and guarantees, and the financial strength of the acquiring company.
Also, the cash flow of the selling company cannot always be used directly for the repayment of acquisition loans.
It is also necessary to confirm the relationship between the acquisition entity and the operating company, the method of fund transfer, and tax and legal constraints.

Chapter 8: How to design training for young officers
The ICE-M&A training model does not need to be a complex system from the start.
One way to start is with a small-scale prototype using fictional companies.
For example, training where you prepare data for 20 fictional companies, including Kofu Precision Industry, and consider combinations of sellers and buyers.
While interacting with AI, officers organize company characteristics, check ICE scores, select buyer candidates, and verify price and loan repayment capacity.
Training grading criteria
Rather than grading simply on whether they “chose the correct buyer,” the following five items are evaluated.
Evaluation Item | Points Understanding of the selling company | 20 points Explanation of the basis for the ICE score | 20 points Reasons for selecting buyer candidates | 20 points Verification of price and synergy | 20 points Loan risk and final decision | 20 points Total | 100 points
This scoring sheet is a proposal for training purposes and is separate from the official evaluation weights of the ICE models 1.0 through 11.0 themselves.
A mechanism is needed to ensure that staff members are not given full credit if they adopt AI responses uncritically.
Conversely, if they are able to exclude candidates suggested by AI based on sound reasoning, that judgment should be evaluated.
Functions required for a prototype
In the initial stage, it is worth considering narrowing the scope to the following functions:
- Input of fictional company information, display of ICE scores and scoring rationale, calculation of reference stock value and distortions, comparison of potential buyers, simple post-acquisition cash flow estimation, calculation of DSCR, and recording of decision rationale and output of training results.
One concept is to start with a low-cost prototype by utilizing existing general-purpose AI tools and keeping the prototype budget under 100,000 yen, but the scope achievable with that budget will vary depending on development methods, AI usage fees, security requirements, and so on. Therefore, it is realistic to design it initially as a training demo that does not handle confidential information of real companies. Furthermore, as for the future revenue model (training fees) for this system, the following three market price ranges are anticipated depending on the delivery format:
1. Group training/workshop format (spot): 500,000 to 1.5 million yen per session 
2. E-learning/SaaS format (monthly license): 100,000 to 300,000 yen per month 
3. Practical consulting training (hands-on): 3 million to 5 million yen per project. By clarifying the added value of "cultivating next-generation bankers who can master AI" rather than mere classroom learning, it becomes possible to set prices differently from conventional financial training.
These training costs can be considered a level where investment recovery is fully possible when considering the "improvement in deal closing rates" and "reduction in external consulting fees" resulting from the improvement of bank staff skills.
Chapter 9: The value of the ICE-M&A model lies in "transparency"
Strategic significance of introducing it as a training model: Introducing this system as a "training program" rather than just a "business efficiency tool" has significant advantages for bank management.
Lowering psychological hurdles: Even in organizations cautious about direct implementation in practice, rapid decision-making is possible under the name of "next-generation talent development" if it is an education budget. 
Differentiation from existing financial training: Unlike training that stops at "how to read financial statements," the point of thoroughly training the "verbalization of non-financial information (business viability)" using ICE scores is the uniqueness that regional banks are currently seeking most. 
Standardization of decision-making processes: Through dialogue with AI, the thought process of "what a bank should prioritize" can be shared by all young staff, leading to an improvement in the appraisal capabilities of the entire organization. 
Gradual transition to practice: An introduction scenario with reduced risk can be drawn, where the tool is gradually deployed to the field as an actual deal support tool after confirming its effectiveness in training. 
Direct response to management issues: As a concrete measure for "hands-on support" and "business viability assessment" required by the Financial Services Agency, it provides clear output (ICE scores and AI analysis results) that is easy to incorporate into management plans and DX strategies. 
Robust security design: While general-purpose AI on the internet is used at the training stage, the data handled is limited to "fictional data" only. By thoroughly implementing operations that do not use bank accounts or actual customer data at all, a safe educational environment that completely eliminates the risk of information leakage is realized.
M&A has the problem that the process of evaluating corporate value and selecting buyers can become opaque to the seller company.
Of course, the experience and negotiation skills of experts are important.
However, if the seller's management cannot understand "why this price" or "why this buyer," it becomes difficult for them to be convinced to hand over the business.
The ICE-M&A model aims to record at least the following content:
Why that ICE score was reached.
Why that company became a buyer candidate.
Why that stock value was calculated.
Why it was thought that the loan amount could be repaid.
Why it was ultimately decided to proceed with or pass on the deal.
By leaving such grounds, it becomes easier to hand over the considerations even if the person in charge changes.
Also, it becomes easier for supervisors and specialized departments to verify the work.
However, increased transparency and a higher M&A success rate are separate issues.
Whether actual results will be achieved requires verification after the training.
Chapter 10: Can AI develop staff?
In this training model, what I consider particularly important is human resource development.
AI can support the organization, comparison, and calculation of information.
However, drawing out the true intentions of the seller's management, confirming the buyer's management policy, and advancing negotiations to protect local employment are the roles of humans.
If bank staff can use AI to organize a large amount of information in a short time, they may be able to spend that time on dialogue with management and on-site verification.
However, there is also a danger that reliance on AI will weaken the staff's own judgment.
Therefore, in the training, it is important not just to read the AI's answers, but to emphasize the process where the staff member themselves argues back, changes conditions, and re-verifies the conclusion.
For example, even if AI determines that 'an industrial robot manufacturer is the most promising candidate,' the staff member should ask questions such as the following:
'Why is it not a semiconductor manufacturing equipment manufacturer?'
'How would the evaluation change if key engineers resigned?'
'If the seller lowers the asking price to 800 million yen, would the buyer's choice change?'
'If interest rates rise, what happens to the DSCR?'
The purpose of the training is to acquire the ability to verify AI's answers through such dialogues.
Summary: Making ICE-M&A a 'tool that bank staff can use'
In this 16th installment, I envisioned training where young regional bank staff structure M&A deals using Kofu Precision Industry as a case study.
There are five important points.
First, do not judge a company's value solely by financial figures.
It is necessary to organize aspects such as technology, customer base, competitiveness, and management structure.
Second, do not confuse the ICE score with stock value.
The score is an indicator that visualizes a company's characteristics, not the acquisition price itself.
Third, distinguish between finding potential buyers and establishing a price.
In this assumption, the reference values for the four different industry sectors were all below the desired price of 1 billion yen.
Fourth, distinguish between enterprise value and loan repayment capacity.
For loan decisions, it is necessary to verify the post-acquisition cash flow and the loan repayment plan.
Fifth, use AI as a tool to develop staff, not as a replacement for them.
I want to develop the ICE-M&A model not just as a mechanism to streamline M&A brokerage, but as an education and practical support system for regional bank staff to think about the future of companies.
AI explores.
ICE visualizes.
Bank staff verify and judge.
And, they must be able to explain the basis for that judgment to both the seller and the buyer.
This is the vision of the ICE-M&A model.
