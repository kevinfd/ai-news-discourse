# Colossal has take another big step toward bringing back the dodo

Fast Company · Rebecca Heilweil · 2026-10-14

Since its founding in 2021, Colossal Laboratories & Biosciences has sought to bring back extinct animals, including the dodo, the iconic flightless bird once endemic to Mauritius. It was documented by humans when the Portuguese first arrived on the island in the 16th century, but hasn’t been seen in hundreds of years. The columbid (a 3-foot-tall member of the Columbidae family, which includes modern pigeons and doves) struggled to survive the arrival of new invasive species and human hunting, and, as the saying goes, went “the way of the dodo.”
For that same reason, Colossal, the biotech/genetics company that already claims to have resurrected the dire wolf, wants to bring the dodo back. The company’s strategy relies on editing the genomes of other species (their living relatives) with the extant preserved DNA from the species they’re trying to regenerate. But the dodo is especially difficult: The company only has access to one dodo genomic sample and needs to apply its technology—previously applied to dire wolves conceived via surrogate—to a species gestated in an egg.
But Colossal announced on Wednesday that it has made progress toward that goal by using a new AI tool it built, called GenoMorph. The AI tool is trained on DNA from other species and can identify which sections of a genome are likely to be associated with different traits, like a beak or a feather. The scientists are using that technology to identify which regions of the available dodo DNA are responsible for the traits that make the dodo, well, dodo-ish. Thus far, they’ve whittled the list down to 2,300 DNA targets. (The company also says that, for the first time, it has used lipid nanoparticles to conduct genomic editing within an egg.)
This is all a reminder that what Colossal is doing isn’t complete genetic replication of the original dodo, but something more nuanced. The company’s strategy instead involves building what its leadership calls a “functional” dodo, filling in the genetic sequence of a living relative species, a type of pigeon, with selections of dodo DNA associated with specific dodo traits, like its iconic beak.
Whether or not you think this strategy constitutes actually bringing back the dodo hinges on how broadly you’re willing to define a species. Some scientists have strongly objected to Colossal’s framing of its work, even as the company announces quests to bring back the woolly mammoth (a heavy-coated cousin to the elephant), the thylacine (Tasmanian tiger), and the giant Moa (a flightless bird from New Zealand that stood up to 12 feet tall and weighed nearly 500 pounds). But Colossal’s chief science officer Beth Shapiro counters that the distinction between different species is often fuzzier than we think. “It’s a species definition that’s based on an arbitrary threshold of sequence similarity,” she says. “But that threshold is arbitrary. It’s going to be arbitrary no matter who you are.”
Fast Company sat down with Shapiro and company CEO Ben Lamm to discuss Colossal’s latest dodo updates, the definition of species, and the way their company uses AI.
