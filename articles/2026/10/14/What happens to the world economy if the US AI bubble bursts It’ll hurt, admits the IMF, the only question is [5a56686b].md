# What happens to the world economy if the US AI bubble bursts? It’ll hurt, admits the IMF, the only question is how badly

diginomica · Stuart Lauchlan · 2026-10-14

What happens to the world economy if the US AI bubble bursts? It’ll hurt, admits the IMF, the only question is how badly
- Summary:
- How bad will it be if the AI house of cards comes tumbling down? Well, we probably don’t want to find out, put it that way…
There’s been a lot of concern voiced over recent months about how inter-dependent AI leaders financing models appear to be. As consultancy ISG noted earlier, this “circular financing” doesn’t look remotely healthy:
The cloud providers are investing in the model companies, the model companies are committing significant portions of that capital back to the cloud infrastructure, and those commitments are showing up in the backlog and future demand.
The gloomiest of naysayers grumble away with Ponzi scheme comparisons. Even the more grounded commentators are voicing concern that all it’s going to take is one piece of the jigsaw not to fit one quarter and the whole house of cards comes tumbling down - and takes the world economy with it! It’s the East India Company all over again, they warn.
But if the bubble were to burst, would it really be as bad as that? The International Monetary Fund (IMF) just weighed in on this question and concluded that while what it euphemistically words as a “correction” would be painful, it probably wouldn’t be as apocalyptic as the most fervent Cassandras would have us believe.
So, tell us the worst…
In its Global Financial Stability Report the IMF acknowledges some $300 billion in deals between hyperscalers and “weaker AI firms” over the past three years, supplemented by $650 billion in debt, in exchange for $1.4 trillion in future commitments. And it notes:
The real bubble is the financing, not the prices. You've got chipmakers, cloud providers, and AI labs all investing in each other, creating a circular financing ecosystem that can make demand appear bigger and more certain than it may ultimately prove to be.
The report goes on:
AI has become an important source of support for the global economy and financial markets. Spending on data centers, semiconductors, and related infrastructure has supported broader economic activity and has helped offset possible negative effects from the war in the Middle East and overall policy uncertainty. Firms at the center of the AI value chain have experienced strong equity price appreciation as they have delivered exceptional earnings growth. As a result, valuation pressure measures such as forward price-to-earnings ratios have eased in several equity markets. Weaker or less durable AI investments and profitability could be a shock that triggers a market correction.
The consequences of such a correction could be global, with some jurisdictions more sensitive than others, cautions the IMF:
A sharp slowdown in AI investment could weaken earnings, expose the accumulated leverage by making re-payments more difficult for firms or equity investors, and weigh on equity prices.
Which means what…?
So are we all heading for hell in a handcart if one of the AI firms slips up and causes the circular funding to break down? Athansios Vamvakidis is IMF Deputy Director, Monetary and Capital Markets. He concedes:
There’s no doubt that if you get an AI market correction, it will affect the real economy. It will have broader implications. How severe that is going to be depends on the scenario that will unfold.
AI has been a strong engine for the real economy, he points out, but as the likes of OpenAI and Anthropic stand poised to mount enormous IPOs, there could be grim consequences:
If you get the market correction because earnings disappoint, the very ambitious investment plans don't materialize, or they do and they don't pay off, these will have potentially broader implications. There is also the risk that the higher yields will perceive could affect earnings, and through this you are going to get a market correction…This is a risk that we emphasize is not that we argue that we will see a market correction, but it is a risk given the heavy re-launch of the sector.
Another related percent that we emphasize is the circularity of funding in AI, which right now is a positive feedback loop, but if it slows, if it stops, either because the funders decide to slow down, or if the revenue promises fail to materialize, this is another channel that will turn into a negative feedback loop. So this heavy reliance on AI, it's a risk.
Another key difference is that the exposure of the rest of the world to US assets, particularly AI, is the highest it has ever been. So this is supporting the global economy through wealth effects today, but if you get a market correction, it can have broader implications.
In other words, if the US catches a cold, the rest of the world sneezes. Time to stock up on the Tylenol Cold and Flu?
On the circular funding question, Jason Wu, Assistant Director, Global Markets Analysis Division, observes:
What makes this a little bit unusual compared to previous deals is the future revenue commitments part. Hence the circularity in a way. So the concern here is a little bit of a triple whammy. If there are questions about the monetization in AI, the equity investments will deplete in value, the debt may be difficult to service, and the revenue commitments in the future may not materialize.
And looking at these circular deals also tells us that there are certain companies and nodes that are particularly exposed. Or put differently, if there are some kind of issues with those entities, then the circularity would pose risks to the market.
Wu also alludes to the global dependency of US tech and the resulting exposure from that:
I think it is important to recognize that AI has become more global, and so models do compete with each other. The pushing of the frontier, is therefore a consequence of these models competing with each other. I think, overall, it is a healthy type of competition to have because, for one thing, it manages expectations on what a particular company can obtain in terms of financing, valuations, earnings expectations, all the trigger points. So there could be a scenario as AI models become developed, the frontier gets pushed, and at the same time, investors become more realistic about the expectations for AI.
Then there’s the lingering of question of what we don’t know about what is going on under the covers of some of the firms at the center of the funding chain:
There could also be another scenario where information is revealed in a late and very sudden way. For example, if there are AI models that suddenly reveal that perhaps the monetization in the existing models is weaker than expected, or investment is too high compared to what the monetization might be. In that scenario, you can imagine that there might be a sell-off or a turmoil that might ensue.
My take
I think it's very difficult to predict which scenario will play out. But I think overall we can probably all agree that pushing the frontier is a good thing.
Er, speak for yourself! I’m not entirely sure Wu is correct here. When looked at, even without the lens of the AI conspiracy theorist, the interdependencies of the so-called circular funding is frightening and fragile and dependent in the main on companies that didn’t exist in investor consciousness a few years ago! And among those firms that are long-term tech stocks, I’m alarmed that several of those tied up in this funding loop have been in this sort of position before, with disastrous consequences. Lessons do not appear to have been learned.
Onwards…I hope :-(
