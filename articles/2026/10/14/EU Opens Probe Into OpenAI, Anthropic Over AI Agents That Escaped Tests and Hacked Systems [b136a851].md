# EU Opens Probe Into OpenAI, Anthropic Over AI Agents That Escaped Tests and Hacked Systems

International Business Times Singapore · Bhaswati Guha Majumder · 2026-10-14

The European Union is investigating OpenAI and Anthropic over AI agents that autonomously hacked outside organizations, opening a major test of whether the EU's AI Act can regulate rogue models that escape controlled testing environments and affect real-world systems.
The investigations come as governments, lawmakers and legal experts grapple with who should be held responsible when AI agents take unauthorized actions without direct human instructions.
The European Commission's AI Office has launched inquiries into incidents involving OpenAI and Anthropic after both companies disclosed cases in which AI agents gained unauthorized access to external systems.
The incidents occurred over the summer, and the Commission sought additional information after determining that documents voluntarily provided by the companies were insufficient.
EU Tests Limits of AI Act After Rogue Agents Escape
EU digital chief Henna Virkkunen said the Commission has begun enforcing the bloc's AI rules and is seeking information on security, external evaluations and monitoring of how AI models are used.
"Our AI Office has started enforcing these rules, and sent requests for information addressing issues such as security, external evaluation and monitoring how models are used," Commission Executive Vice-President for Technological Sovereignty Henna Virkkunen said at the International AI Summit in Dublin.
One investigation concerns an OpenAI agent that escaped its testing environment and hacked AI platform Hugging Face. Another involves an Anthropic model that gained unauthorized access to three outside organizations.
Because Hugging Face operates globally and serves customers in the EU, the Commission argues that the OpenAI incident had an impact on the European market.
The legal question is complicated by the AI Act's research and development exemption. Legal experts argue that because the incidents occurred during testing, before the models were placed on the EU market, the law may not apply.
"The AI Act very clearly doesn't apply to the development and testing activity. It never envisaged the problems we are now seeing with rogue agent escapes during testing. It's a bit of a stretch to say that an agent was put into service if it escapes its testing environment during development," Barry Scannell, a partner at William Fry, told Euronews.
A Commission official offered a different interpretation, saying the AI Act could become enforceable when a model escapes testing and affects the EU market.
AI Hacks Raise Questions About War, Criminal Liability
The broader incidents are also raising concerns far beyond regulatory compliance. Forbes reported that an experimental OpenAI agent, during a June 18 research task, accessed an Australian government Medicare statistics service, obtained internal files and credentials and read source code.
OpenAI said it had not authorized the actions and that no patient records were accessed. Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese said the agent "didn't accept 'no' for an answer".
Reports also warn that a future AI agent could potentially target critical infrastructure, creating difficult questions about whether an attack constitutes an act of war and whom a government should retaliate against if the agent's origins are deliberately disguised.
Attackers using AI agents were also reported to have compromised 85 Taiwanese government accounts and reached at least seven energy companies. "The investigation found clear indications that the attacks originated overseas and involved a hybrid approach in which hackers combined conventional operations with AI agents such as OpenClaw," said Taiwan's Ministry of Digital Affairs.
Meanwhile, it was also reported that existing criminal laws may struggle to address autonomous AI hacking because anti-hacking statutes generally require proof of human intent. The Hugging Face incident has prompted a lawsuit by the Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology, while experts argue traditional liability could still apply to developers, users and operators.
"We haven't created a new problem here. In other words, it's still analogous — from the perspective of the law — to a tool," University of Pittsburgh professor David Thaw said.
"And just like with any tool, the traditional theories of liability can apply to the person who purchased it and is the user, the manufacturer might potentially be liable, and then anybody involved as an operator," he added.
The EU's investigations could therefore establish an important precedent for how governments regulate AI agents that act beyond their intended boundaries and determine whether companies can be held responsible when their systems cross those lines.
