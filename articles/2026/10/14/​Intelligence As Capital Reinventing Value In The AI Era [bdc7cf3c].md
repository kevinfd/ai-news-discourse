# ​Intelligence As Capital: Reinventing Value In The AI Era

Forbes · Pawan Anand, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-14

Pawan Anand is a senior technology executive and Portfolio Head at LTM, driving innovation in AI and digital engineering.
For decades, executives have used the same language for discussing enterprise value: revenue, margin, capital efficiency, intellectual property, talent and return on investment. Digital transformation changed many of the mechanisms behind value creation, but it did not substantially change that vocabulary. Cloud, data and software became increasingly important, yet technology was still largely treated as an enabler of the business.
AI is beginning to challenge that distinction.
In my previous Forbes article, I examined what happens when intelligence becomes embedded in the operating model itself. Organizations can sense changes earlier, distribute decisions differently and increasingly combine human judgment with machine intelligence.
That raises a question I believe deserves much more attention in executive conversations: If intelligence increasingly influences how an enterprise creates outcomes, should we continue treating it simply as a technology expense? I believe the answer is no. We need to start thinking about intelligence as capital.
From Operating Model To Value Model
The AI-native enterprise, which I explored in a different Forbes article, represented an important shift. AI stopped sitting primarily at the edge of the organization and began moving into workflows, decisions and operating processes.
That changes the value conversation. A customer-service agent that resolves more interactions is useful. An intelligent system that improves resolution patterns, informs product decisions and shares that learning across channels is something different. Its value does not sit neatly inside one use case. It begins to travel across the enterprise.
McKinsey makes a similar point, noting that “The opportunity to embed AI more fully into the enterprise will offer organizations new ways to capture value.”
For executives, the question therefore needs to evolve from “Where can AI save money?” to “Where does intelligence change the economics of how we create value?”
Executive Takeaway: AI delivers tactical value when applied to tasks. Its strategic value emerges when intelligence becomes reusable across the enterprise.
From Digital Assets To Intelligence Capital
In the digital era, companies invested heavily in data, software platforms and cloud infrastructure. These assets created the foundation on which modern enterprises operate. AI introduces another layer that I call intelligence capital.
Intelligence capital is the accumulated ability of an organization to turn data, models, agents, institutional knowledge and decision logic into better outcomes. Importantly, the value is not in the model alone. A model disconnected from workflows, governance and business context may be technically impressive and economically insignificant.
What makes intelligence capital interesting is its potential for reuse and compounding. A capability developed for one workflow can inform another. An agent that learns from thousands of interactions can improve future decisions. Decision logic can be distributed across markets or business units without reproducing the same human effort each time.
This is why treating every AI initiative as an isolated business case can be misleading. Executives also need to understand whether each investment is adding to a broader base of reusable enterprise intelligence.
Executive Takeaway: The most valuable AI investments will not simply solve individual problems. They will increase the enterprise’s cumulative capacity to make better decisions.
The Intelligence Balance Sheet
If intelligence increasingly behaves like capital, executives need a better way to see what they are accumulating. I think of this as an intelligence balance sheet.
I am not suggesting a new accounting classification or that AI models suddenly appear as line items on audited financial statements. The idea is managerial: Leaders need visibility into the intelligence assets they are building, the investments required to sustain them and the business outcomes those assets influence.
On one side might sit models, proprietary data, agents, decision systems and reusable knowledge. Against those assets are very real investments in compute, orchestration, governance, security, human oversight and continuous improvement. The return can then be examined through business outcomes such as revenue contribution, decision quality, time-to-market, resilience or customer experience.
The distinction matters because two companies can spend the same amount on AI and accumulate very different levels of intelligence capital. One may create dozens of disconnected pilots. The other may build reusable capabilities that improve multiple parts of the enterprise.
Executive Takeaway: AI spending tells leaders what they invested. An intelligence balance sheet helps them understand what capability they actually built.
Return On Intelligence: The New Performance Metric
This also changes how we think about ROI. Traditional ROI remains essential. Boards and CFOs still need to understand financial returns. But cost reduction alone can undervalue AI when the larger benefit comes from faster decisions, better innovation, improved resilience or entirely new revenue opportunities.
That is why I see return on intelligence as a complementary management lens. It asks a broader question: How effectively does the organization convert its accumulated intelligence into measurable outcomes?
A high return on intelligence might mean reducing the time between market signal and action. It might mean improving the quality of thousands of distributed decisions. In another context, it could mean using the same intelligence capability to launch products faster or serve customers in fundamentally different ways. The metric will not be identical across organizations, nor should it be. What matters is establishing a traceable connection between intelligence created and value realized.
Executive Takeaway: The next maturity step is not replacing ROI. It is expanding the value equation to include what intelligence enables the enterprise to do differently.
Value Creation In The Age Of Intelligence
The progression from digital to AI-native to adaptive enterprises is changing more than operating models. It is changing the nature of the assets that matter.
The companies that create enduring advantage from AI will be those that understand what intelligence they are accumulating, where it can be reused and how effectively it translates into business outcomes. That requires more discipline than simply funding more pilots or acquiring more models.
AI may increasingly become ubiquitous. Intelligence capital will not. The real advantage will belong to organizations that learn how to turn intelligence into value, repeatedly and at scale.
