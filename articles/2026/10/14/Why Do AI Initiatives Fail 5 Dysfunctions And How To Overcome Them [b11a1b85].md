# Why Do AI Initiatives Fail? 5 Dysfunctions And How To Overcome Them

Forbes · Mike de Vere · 2026-10-14

Mike de Vere is CEO of Zest AI.
AI failure has become an industry norm. Ninety-five percent of AI initiatives fail, and companies are now refocusing on human capital after rounds of AI-driven layoffs. The banking industry is no different: 67% of financial institutions are implementing AI, but only 16% know why.
Still, leaders today want to know where to get the most value out of AI. But before you begin creating product road maps and requesting demos, you’ll need to create a framework for AI initiatives. And it starts with what not to do.
The Five Dysfunctions Of A Team
As a CEO of a growing organization for eight years, I’ve built my leadership approach around Patrick Lencioni’s The Five Dysfunctions of a Team. His thesis: Teams don’t fail from a lack of talent. They fail from a stack of five behaviors, each one built on the one below it, like a pyramid:
1. Absence of trust
2. Fear of conflict
3. Lack of commitment
4. Avoidance of accountability
5. Inattention to results
Everything flows from trust. If your team doesn’t have trust, it can’t have healthy conflict, commitment, accountability or attention to results. I’ve noticed that AI strategies fail the same way—without a solid foundation or reason why AI is there, those efforts will fall apart. 
If trust is the foundation of teamwork, then purpose is the foundation of AI strategy. Of course, trust is a foundational building block in any AI deployment. But before you begin even looking for a model you can trust, you still need to ask: Why AI and why here? 
Here’s the pyramid I keep seeing inside companies right now, and how to fix it. 
The Five Dysfunctions Of AI Initiatives
1. AI Without A Purpose
Most AI strategies start with the wrong question. “How can we use AI?” sounds ambitious, but the right question is narrower and harder to answer: “What outcome are we trying to change?” Revenue, losses, cycle time, customer experience—something concrete has to sit on the other side of the investment. Without purpose, AI becomes a solution looking for a problem. 
Instead, find your “why”: Audit where your organization is falling behind, and why. Is it because we lack data, it takes too long to analyze, or both? What are the biggest pain points for my team, and why do they exist? 
2. The Hammer-And-Nail Problem
Once companies invest in AI, suddenly every problem looks like an AI problem. Some processes should be automated, some augmented, some left deliberately human. Mature AI leadership means knowing the difference.
Instead, view AI as a scalpel: Purpose-built AI tools can offer deep expertise in an area that affects your business, instead of a general-purpose large language model. Once you’ve diagnosed the problems, you narrow your search for a solution.
3. Impact Without Accountability
Organizations celebrate models, pilots and deployments instead of measuring whether AI changed revenue, losses, efficiency, customer outcomes or employee productivity. “We deployed AI” is not a business result.
Instead, measure everything: Quarterly business reviews, keeping track of model results and refitting when necessary are all ways to hold AI initiatives accountable. 
4. Taking The Human Out Of The Loop
It’s a paradoxical argument: The best use of AI may be to create more human organizations. AI should absorb information-processing and repetitive work so people have greater capacity for judgment, empathy and relationships.
Instead, keep humans in the loop: This approach involves human oversight of AI technology, where AI does the heavy lifting of aggregating data and providing analysis, but humans review and oversee the final product. It creates efficiency and a system for accountability, especially in AI-driven decisions that involve risk and compliance. 
5. The Goalie Paradox
Companies say they want innovation while organizational incentives punish anyone who takes a shot and misses. Like judging a goalie exclusively on goals allowed, this creates defensive organizations optimized for avoiding visible mistakes rather than achieving exceptional outcomes. AI exposes this cultural problem because experimentation inherently involves uncertainty.
Instead, change the scoreboard: If an AI initiative does fail, don’t point fingers. View it as another step closer to solving the problems you laid out in step 1, and try again. Reward the swing, not just the outcome.
Getting AI Adoption Right
None of these five dysfunctions are about the AI itself, and that’s the point. The model can be excellent and an initiative can still fail, because the organization around it never created its purpose, established guardrails, nor held it accountable. The 5% of companies that beat the odds with a successful AI initiative won’t have the biggest budget or place massive bets on AI, but they will be able to name their own dysfunctions and navigate through them.
