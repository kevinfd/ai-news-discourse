# Making Generative AI a 'Built-in Mechanism' in Manufacturing: What I Actually Did for Implementation, Training, and Adoption

note · 現場の思考エンジン · 2026-10-14

Making Generative AI a 'Built-in Mechanism' in Manufacturing: What I Actually Did for Implementation, Training, and Adoption
When I first tried to spread generative AI within the company, the first thing I prepared was not training materials.
It was using it for my own work and achieving results. I used generative AI to aggregate equipment data, reducing a task that previously took about 5 hours to around 30 minutes. I hadn't mastered advanced programming. I would get errors, consult the AI, fix them, and try again. It was a process of repetition.
I thought, 'This can be used on the shop floor.' So, I proposed it to the company and started by testing it with a small group.
However, things change when you actually increase the number of people involved.
Some people say, 'I don't know what to ask.' Some people try it once and never use it again. On the other hand, some people apply it to tasks I didn't even teach them.
Furthermore, there were situations where you shouldn't trust AI responses too much. When it comes to equipment or quality, you cannot make decisions based solely on plausible-sounding text.
Just teaching people about its convenience won't ensure it continues to be used safely within the company.
What we tried was selecting tasks, conducting small-scale proofs, revising training, setting safety boundaries with relevant departments, demonstrating effectiveness, and increasing the number of people who could teach others. This led to expanding from a trial of several dozen people to a rollout for 100 people.
However, there is another issue I noticed here.
Even if the number of users increases, if questions are concentrated on me, it is not yet an organizational mechanism. Increasing the number of users and creating a state where it is used even without me were two different things.
I wrote this article for people like this
Field leaders, managers, and promotion staff who are trying to introduce and deploy generative AI in the manufacturing industry. I have structured it so it can also be used in workplaces where tools have already been introduced but are only used by a few people.
This article does not cover rankings of recommended AI or collections of prompts.
It covers the 'process' and 'decision-making models' you can use when moving forward with discussions in your company tomorrow.
After purchase, I have prepared a roadmap from introduction to adoption, along with 8 practical tools that you can copy and rewrite for your own company.
- An 'AI Use Case Selection Sheet' to decide which tasks to start with
- A 'Small-Scale Proof Checklist' to design small-scale trials
- A '60-Minute Training Template' to create your first success story
- A 'Before/After Effectiveness Measurement Sheet' to track time, costs, and usage
- A 'Safety Judgment Table' to determine the scope of AI usage
- An 'Outline for Internal Explanatory Materials' to explain to management, IT, and the shop floor
- A 'Promoter Dependency Check' to review the concentration of consultations on the promotion staff
- A 'Generative AI Tool Selection Check' for choosing corporate AI
All of these are based on my trial and error, and have been restructured from the perspective of what I would check if I were starting over again today. I do not intend to write this as a universal success formula that I haven't experienced myself.
In particular, those who are about to introduce generative AI should start with the 'AI Use Case Selection Sheet' and the 'Small-Scale Proof Checklist'.
Those who have already introduced it but haven't seen widespread usage can start with the '60-Minute Training Template' and the 'Promoter Dependency Check'.
For example, in small-scale proofs, you should decide in advance not only 'how many people used it,' but also the conditions for continuing, the conditions for changing the approach, and the conditions for stopping temporarily.
In the main text, I have summarized the checklist items for discussing such decisions within the company, as well as my own way of thinking about what I would check.
