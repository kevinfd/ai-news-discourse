# Former ARPA-H director Renee Wegrzyn wants to solve science’s ‘magic hands’ problem

Fast Company · Robert Safian · 2026-10-14

Renee Wegrzyn spent her career building the future of American science—including the Advanced Research Projects Agency for Health, or ARPA-H, the White House agency created to accelerate health breakthroughs.
Then the Trump administration showed her the door. Wegrzyn cofounded Transfyr to tackle what she calls one of the most overlooked issues in science: the magic hands problem. She explains why fixing this unglamorous bottleneck is the prerequisite for everything AI promises to deliver in medicine, and why she can feel the next Sputnik moment coming—even if she can’t quite name it yet.
This is an abridged transcript of an interview from Rapid Response, hosted by Robert Safian, former editor-in-chief of Fast Company. From the team behind the Masters of Scale podcast, Rapid Response features candid conversations with today’s top business leaders navigating real-time challenges. Subscribe to Rapid Response wherever you get your podcasts to ensure you never miss an episode.
You were at ARPA-H, the inaugural director of a White House effort to accelerate health research. You were amped up about the possibilities, but with the Trump administration, you had to pivot. Was it hard not to take it personally that they wanted different leadership?
I’ll tell you, I’m still amped up about it. It’s still an amazing idea. Obviously, DARPA [the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency] has been around for almost 80 years. I hope ARPA-H will have the same longevity and continue to live on today.
I’m a builder. I love to take things forward and really show what’s possible. So was it hard when I left? Of course it was hard. I had a really unusual appointment: I was a presidential appointee, but I was term-limited, so I served through the transition and had a few weeks in the new administration. I served in “Trump one,” by the way, as well.
So it was a great opportunity to make sure they really understood why we built the agency. We needed an ARPA-H because there was something missing in the way we were doing science broadly, which was: How do you move really quickly? How do you get focused on a problem versus building a general knowledge set? I was able to show them what we were building and convince them that it was worth keeping around. I couldn’t be more proud of the agency and its perseverance.
