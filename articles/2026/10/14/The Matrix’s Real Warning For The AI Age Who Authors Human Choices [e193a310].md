# The Matrix’s Real Warning For The AI Age: Who Authors Human Choices?

Forbes · Regan Peng, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-14

Regan Peng is an AI founder and former senior tech executive, focused on personal AI, privacy-preserving systems, and edge AI.
When The Matrix arrived in 1999, many thought its central question was epistemological: “Is the world around us real?” More than a quarter-century later, its more pressing question may be simpler: “Am I still choosing how I live?”
In the trilogy, the machines’ first simulation offered humanity paradise. People rejected it. A second version reproduced the violence and imperfection of human history, and it also failed. The durable Matrix introduced choice—even if that choice existed only near the unconscious level. The system did not become stable by making its reality more convincing. It became stable by giving people the feeling that they were choosing it.
That is what I see as the film’s deepest insight. Humans do not live by pleasure alone, nor do we accept a world merely because it resembles reality. We need to experience ourselves as a source of action. A difficult life can still carry meaning when we can say, “I chose.” A perfect world can become intolerable when nothing within it truly begins with us.
From Artificial Intelligence To Synthetic Intention
AI is now crossing a threshold. It is moving from tools that answer questions toward agents that can pursue goals and act on our behalf. As these systems begin to frame problems, infer preferences, rank alternatives and execute decisions, the essential question changes. It is no longer only, “What can the machine know or do?” It becomes, “Who authored the objective?”
We have spent years worrying about synthetic media, but I’ve found the more consequential frontier may be synthetic intention. Having spent years building large-scale data and AI systems, I have learned that the most powerful systems do not eliminate human choice. They shape the default, compress the alternatives and make one path feel inevitable.
AI does not need to order us to reduce our agency. It can do so by making decisions easier to accept. The recommendation arrives before we have fully considered what we want. The summary arrives before we read the underlying material. The draft arrives before we have formed our own view. The next action appears before we have weighed the alternatives. We may still press “approve,” but the system may have already shaped much of the decision.
A 2025 study of 319 knowledge workers found that greater confidence in generative AI was associated with less critical thinking, while AI use shifted much of that thinking toward verification, integration and task stewardship. That shift is not inherently harmful, but it exposes a crucial distinction: A person can remain in the loop without remaining the author.
Why Agency Is More Than Choice
For centuries, we treated intelligence as the signature of our species. AI is forcing the harder realization that intelligence may be reproducible. But being able to make a choice is not the same as deciding what matters.
Intelligence helps us work within a given set of goals and constraints. Agency gives us a role in deciding those goals and constraints in the first place. Intelligence can help determine how to reach a destination. Agency asks whether that destination is worth pursuing. Intelligence predicts the most probable next step. Agency gives us the ability to reconsider that prediction. Intelligence can optimize among existing options. Agency allows us to introduce a different option.
This is why agency is inseparable from responsibility. To be an agent is not merely to cause an outcome, but to recognize an action as one’s own and to answer for it. When judgment is outsourced but responsibility remains human, we create an ethical fiction: People are held accountable for decisions they no longer meaningfully authored.
Neo’s defining act is therefore not simply rebellion against the system. Near the end of the trilogy, he is forced to choose between two paths presented by the Architect, the designer of the Matrix. One path would preserve the system and humanity within its controlled cycle, while the other risks that stability. Neo chooses the second path, a decision which is shaped by his commitment to Trinity, the person he loves, rather than by the outcome the system considers most rational.
The point is not that Neo makes a perfectly logical choice. It is that the system cannot determine which values should matter most to him. It can calculate consequences and present trade-offs, but it cannot decide what he considers worth protecting. That is where human agency becomes visible: not simply in choosing among the options a system provides, but in deciding which values should guide the choice in the first place.
The Fourth Matrix
The first Matrix tried to govern humanity through pleasure. The second tried to govern through realism and suffering. The third governed through the experience of choice.
The AI age now presents the possibility of a fourth paradigm: systems that do not merely design what people see, but increasingly shape what they want, which questions they ask and which futures they can imagine. Our task should be to build the opposite: AI that enlarges the field of human possibility without quietly seizing authorship of human purpose.
As intelligence becomes abundant, agency becomes scarce. The most valuable human capacity may no longer be producing answers, but originating intentions: deciding what matters, choosing against prediction and accepting responsibility for what follows.
The defining question of the AI age is not whether machines will become more human. It is whether humans will remain the authors of their own lives.
