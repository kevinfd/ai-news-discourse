# Your phone may reveal doctor, church and pharmacy visits

Fox News · Kurt Knutsson, CyberGuy Report · 2026-10-14

Nobody needs to follow you to know where you went this week. The phone in your pocket can already keep a record of your movements. And parts of that information can end up in the hands of other companies.
Your pharmacy. Your cardiologist. The church you attend on Sunday morning. The support group you'd rather nobody knew about. None of it necessarily comes from hacking. It can come from ordinary apps you installed yourself and the permissions you gave them. Here's how your movements can get out, who buys location data and the settings that can limit how much gets shared.

WHAT YOUR INTERNET PROVIDER, WEBSITES AND ADVERTISERS SEE
Your phone can carry an advertising identifier
Smartphones can use advertising identifiers that help advertisers and developers measure ads and activity. On Apple devices, the system advertising identifier is known as the IDFA. Android devices can use Google's advertising ID.
Advertising identifiers can help connect activity across apps when apps and advertising systems are permitted to use them. Combined with location data and other signals, those identifiers can help companies build a much more detailed trail of where a device has been.
And a trail stops being anonymous fast. A device that sleeps at your address every night and turns up at a dialysis center every Tuesday and Thursday isn't much of a mystery to anyone who can connect those locations with other identifying information.
The apps hand it off, and it keeps moving
An app asks for your location. Often for a fair reason, like providing a local forecast or showing the nearest pharmacy. What many people don't realize is that apps can include advertising and analytics software from outside companies. Depending on the app, its permissions and its privacy practices, that technology may collect or receive information about your device and location. Location information can also become part of the digital advertising ecosystem, where information about a device can pass among multiple companies. Once location data leaves your phone, controlling where it goes next becomes much harder.
Federal regulators have already gone after this
This isn't a theory about what could happen. The government spent years fighting one such case in court. On May 4, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission announced a settlement that bars the data broker Kochava and its subsidiary, Collective Data Solutions, from selling, sharing or disclosing sensitive location data without people's affirmative express consent, subject to the terms of the order. The FTC alleged Kochava sold location data from hundreds of millions of mobile devices that could track people to health facilities, places of worship and other sensitive locations. Kochava did not admit wrongdoing. Kochava wasn't alone. In December 2024, the FTC took action against two more location data companies, Gravy Analytics and Mobilewalla, over allegations involving the sale of sensitive location data.
Then one of them got robbed
In January 2025, Gravy Analytics was breached. A hacker posted what they claimed was 17 terabytes of stolen data on a Russian cybercrime forum. Security researchers examining a sample found more than 30 million location points, including phones at the White House, the Kremlin and military bases around the world. The data reportedly came from everyday apps, including games and dating apps. Think about what that means. A company collects movements. Regulators take action over how sensitive location data gets handled. Then criminals allegedly obtain a massive collection of data anyway.
What your movements actually tell a stranger
Strip out the technical language. A few weeks of location data can potentially reveal:
- Which clinic you go to and how often you go back
- Which house of worship you attend and whether you still do
- Whether anyone else appears to live at your address
- What hours your home may sit empty
- Which relatives you visit and on what day
Those last few can turn a privacy problem into a safety concern. A device that regularly leaves a home at the same time and returns two hours later can reveal patterns about when someone may be away. Location information can also make a scam call more convincing. "I'm calling from the billing office at the clinic on Oak Street about your visit last week" is much harder to dismiss when the clinic and timing happen to be correct. And with Medicare Open Enrollment beginning October 15, a caller who seems to know your doctor or pharmacy may sound more convincing than a random scammer.
'But I never share my location'
If you've ever tapped "Allow" on a weather app, a store app or a game, you may have shared it. You might have granted that permission years ago and forgotten about it. Even apps without precise location permission may be able to estimate your general area through information such as your internet connection. You can't make yourself invisible, but you can make your movements harder to track.
What you can do about it
You can't stop your phone from knowing where you are and still use every location-based feature. But you can limit which apps get that information and reduce some of the identifiers used for advertising and tracking. None of this takes technical skill, and you can tackle one part at a time.
Start here: limit advertising tracking
Advertising identifiers aren't the only way companies can recognize a device, but limiting them can remove one tool used to connect activity for advertising purposes.
On iPhone
First, control whether apps can request permission to track you across other companies' apps and websites:
- Open Settings
- Tap Privacy & Security
- Tap Tracking
- Turn off Allow Apps to Request to Track
When this setting is off, apps that request permission to track are treated as though you selected Ask App Not to Track. They cannot access Apple's system advertising identifier, known as the IDFA.
Apple also has a separate setting for ads that Apple itself delivers:
- Open Settings
- Tap Privacy & Security
- Tap Apple Advertising
- Turn off Personalized Ads
This second setting limits how Apple personalizes the ads it delivers in services such as the App Store, Apple News and Stocks. It is separate from the app-tracking control above.

On Samsung Galaxy
To check your advertising ID controls:
- Open Settings
- Tap Security and privacy
- Scroll down and tap More privacy settings
- Open Ads
- Tap Delete advertising ID if that option appears
- Confirm your choice
Depending on your Galaxy model and software configuration, you may also find Google's advertising controls through Settings > Google > All services > Ads.
Deleting the advertising ID prevents apps from accessing that particular Google advertising identifier. It does not prevent every form of advertising, analytics or device identification.
On Google Pixel
- Open Settings
- Tap Security & Privacy
- Tap Privacy controls
- Tap Ads
- Tap Delete advertising ID
- Confirm your choice
Google says deleting the advertising ID removes that identifier. Apps that try to access it receive a string of zeros instead. Apps can still have their own privacy and advertising settings, so this does not eliminate every form of tracking.
Next: tighten location permissions
Now look at which apps can see where you are and whether they really need that access.
On iPhone
- Open Settings
- Tap Privacy & Security
- Tap Location Services
- Scroll through the list of apps
- Tap an app you want to review
- Choose the appropriate access, such as Never, Ask Next Time or When I Share or While Using the App
- If the app doesn't need your exact location, turn off Precise Location
You don't have to turn Location Services off completely. Doing so can interfere with useful features. Instead, focus on apps that have more access than they need.
On Samsung Galaxy
- Open Settings
- Tap Location
- Tap App permissions or App location permissions
- Review the apps that can access your location
- Tap an app you want to change
- Select the appropriate option, such as Don't allow, Ask every time or Allow only while using the app
- If the app doesn't need your exact location, turn off Use precise location
Pay particular attention to apps allowed to access your location all the time. Weather, shopping and similar apps often don't need continuous background access.
YOUR HOME IS EMPTY. DO BURGLARS ALREADY KNOW?
On Google Pixel
- Open Settings
- Tap Location
- Tap App location permissions
- Review the apps under categories such as Allowed all the time, Allowed only while in use, Ask every time or allow when I share and Not allowed
- Tap an app you want to change
- Choose the level of location access it actually needs
- Turn off Use precise location when an approximate location is enough
A weather app, for example, usually doesn't need to know exactly where you're standing. Navigation is one situation where precise location makes much more sense.
Then: clear out what you're not using
Delete dormant apps. Every unused app is another set of permissions and data practices you may have forgotten about. If you haven't opened an app since last year and no longer need it, consider deleting it.
Finally: do it for your parents too
If you have older relatives, their phones are worth checking next. They may be carrying years of old app permissions, and scammers frequently target older adults. Sit down with them for 10 minutes the next time you visit. Review location permissions, advertising controls and apps they no longer use.
The half your phone settings can't reach
Changing these settings can reduce future collection and sharing, but it doesn't erase information that companies may have already collected. And here's the piece many people miss. A location trail by itself may consist largely of coordinates and device information. It becomes much more revealing when someone can connect those movements with a name, home address, phone number or relatives. Some of that identifying information can come from people-search and data broker sites that publish or sell personal details. Kurt's key takeaways
Your phone can reveal far more about your life than you may realize. Location data can expose visits to doctors, pharmacies, places of worship and other sensitive locations, especially when it gets combined with advertising identifiers and other information about you. The FTC's actions against location data brokers show this is not a hypothetical privacy problem. And the Gravy Analytics breach showed what can happen when enormous collections of location information fall into the wrong hands. I would start by reviewing which apps have access to your location and removing access from anything that doesn't truly need it. Then limit app tracking on your iPhone or delete your advertising ID on Android if that option is available. These changes won't make you invisible, but they can reduce the amount of information available to build a detailed trail of where you go. And don't forget the phones belonging to parents or older relatives. Years of forgotten app permissions can leave far more information exposed than they realize.
