# Every AI Agent Needs A Human Owner Before It Signs Off On Anything

Forbes · Morey Haber, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-14

Morey J. Haber, Chief Security Advisor at BeyondTrust, is an identity and technical evangelist with over 25 years of IT industry experience.
Artificial intelligence (AI) can now write a contract, analyze a financial statement, generate ready-to-compile code, recommend business decisions, negotiate with other AI agents and increasingly execute entire workflows without human intervention. Agentic AI takes this evolution even further. It allows systems to process a task, determine the steps required to accomplish it, access applications and data, invoke third-party tools, make business decisions and take automated actions.
We are quickly moving from AI that tells us what we should do to AI that can actually do it for us. However, 78% of executives still lack confidence that they could pass an AI governance audit within 90 days, a security gap that only widens as agents gain more autonomy.
Yet there is one deceptively simple thing it still cannot do: sign its own name. That is not about handwriting or electronic signatures. It is about ownership and accountability. When an AI system makes a consequential decision, someone, presumably a human, still needs to own the outcome.
The Accountability Gap
This distinction between autonomy, ownership and accountability may become one of the most important governance challenges of agentic AI. Organizations are beginning to create AI agents with their own identities and entitlements, and some have broad access to sensitive systems and information.
Some agents may operate independently for hours, days or potentially indefinitely based on a prompted task. Technically, these agents increasingly resemble digital workers but still need owners for accountability. Organizationally, however, they are something very different than just having a management structure. An autonomous AI agent introduces ambiguity because the entity making the decision may not be the entity accountable for it.
A Case Study: The $500,000 Purchase Order
Consider an AI procurement agent authorized to purchase products and services for a company (and yes, this really does exist). The agent identifies a requirement, evaluates suppliers, negotiates pricing, selects a vendor and generates a $500,000 purchase order.
The technology may have operated exactly as designed, but who actually authorized the purchase? AI cannot resolve that question because it cannot accept accountability. It can exercise authority, but it cannot own it, a difference that matters because AI cannot be held accountable the way humans can.
Mapping Identity To Ownership
This is why every autonomous AI identity should ultimately map to an accountable human or organizational owner. Think of it as a human approval for a minor under your care.
AI Agent > Identity > Privileges > Owner (Parent or Guardian) > Accountability > Acceptable Use Policy
Giving an AI agent a unique identity is necessary, but identity alone does not establish responsibility. Without this chain, organizations risk creating thousands of digital workers capable of accessing sensitive information and executing business processes while effectively belonging to no one. We have already learned versions of this lesson through orphaned accounts, abandoned service accounts and unmanaged machine identities.
Agentic AI magnifies the problem: Unlike static service accounts, these identities can actively make decisions, communicate with other systems and pursue objectives. An orphaned agent with privileges is a fundamentally different category of risk than an orphaned service account.
Rethinking Privileged Access For AI
The same principle should reshape how organizations think about privileged access and AI accountability. Traditional privileged access management asks fundamental questions:
• Who has privileged access?
• What can they access?
• When can they access it?
• What did they do with that access?
• Was the access and response appropriate?
Agentic AI introduces additional questions:
• Who authorized the agent to act?
• Did the behavior conform to established policies?
Setting Autonomy Thresholds
There should also be clearly defined thresholds where AI autonomy ends and human accountability begins. Based on the previous example, an AI procurement agent might be permitted to reorder $500 worth of office supplies without intervention. A $500,000 purchase order should probably require a human signature.
Similar boundaries should exist for deleting production data, changing security controls, creating privileged accounts, transferring money, modifying payroll, accessing highly sensitive records, deploying software or granting another AI agent additional privileges for access. These approval points become the digital equivalent of a signature line—something AI cannot do, nor should be allowed to.
When AI Agents Talk To Each Other
This scenario becomes even more complicated when AI agents interact with other AI agents. In this environment, consider what happens when one agent requests information from another, which then invokes a third agent that accesses a privileged system, triggering a fourth automated workflow, and potentially so on. The original human instruction may eventually be several degrees removed from the final AI action.
Without strong identity governance and auditability, organizations could find themselves investigating incidents where everyone can explain what the AI did, but nobody can definitively explain who authorized it to perform in an obtuse manner. “The AI did it” cannot become an acceptable answer to a customer, regulator, auditor, board or legal position in a court of law.
Why Capability Is Not Accountability
We understandably anthropomorphize AI. We call these systems agents, give them names, assign them identities, provide credentials and increasingly delegate responsibilities previously reserved for employees. But capability should never be confused with accountability.
An AI system may recommend an action, execute it, document its reasoning and produce a convincing explanation afterward. None of those capabilities means it can accept responsibility for the consequences.
AI security will not be defined solely by how intelligent our agents become, but by how responsibly we govern the authority we give them. Every AI identity needs an owner, every privilege needs a purpose, every consequential action needs attribution and every autonomous workflow needs guardrails.
Who Holds The Pen
AI may eventually write better than we do, code faster than we do, analyze more information than we ever could and execute complex business processes without asking permission at every step. However, when the document reaches the bottom and there is a line marked “Authorized Signature,” someone still needs to be accountable for signing it, and AI cannot sign its own name. A human still has to hold the pen.
