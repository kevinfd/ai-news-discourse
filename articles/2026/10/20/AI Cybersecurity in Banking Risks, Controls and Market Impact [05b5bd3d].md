# AI Cybersecurity in Banking: Risks, Controls and Market Impact

AI cybersecurity in banking covers two connected tasks: using artificial intelligence to detect digital threats, and protecting financial systems from attacks that exploit AI. Banks must defend customer identities, payment instructions, confidential data, and increasingly automated operations.
World’s identity-verification launch on 18 October 2026 illustrates one approach, but confirming that someone is human cannot secure a financial transaction on its own. Strong authentication, controlled AI access and operational resilience remain central to managing cyber risk.
- AI cybersecurity involves both AI-powered protection and securing banks against AI-assisted attacks.
- The FBI recorded 22,364 AI-related cybercrime complaints and $893.3 million in reported adjusted losses in 2025, which is not a measure of global bank losses.
- AI agents require defined permissions and transaction controls, and biometric verification does not establish payment authority.
- Cyber incidents can interrupt financial services and market liquidity, particularly where institutions depend on shared technology providers.
Traditional cybersecurity protects networks, account credentials and data. AI widens that exposure because attackers can automate parts of an intrusion, while banks increasingly rely on models for fraud detection, customer support, and operational decisions, meaning the systems doing the defending now need defending themselves.
Confidentiality of financial information, integrity of records and payment instructions, and availability of critical services remain the foundation. Controls must also record who, or which authorised software agent, took an action so investigators can trace and review questionable decisions afterwards.
Impersonation is the most visible threat, but banks also face automated probing for software vulnerabilities and attacks aimed at the models they operate. Malicious instructions hidden inside a document or message can redirect an AI assistant, and poisoned training data can distort what a model decides.
The FBI’s 2025 Internet Crime Report recorded 22,364 complaints tagged as AI-related, with $893.3 million in adjusted reported losses. The classification spans many crime types and cannot be read as losses suffered by banks alone.
EBC has separately examined AI deepfakes and digital banking fraud, which covers the customer-facing side of the problem. The wider cybersecurity task also includes protecting the systems sitting behind those interactions.
Machine-learning models can flag unusual logins, unfamiliar devices, atypical transfer patterns and suspicious network activity. Risk scores help security teams prioritise investigations, and higher-risk activity can trigger an additional authentication step or a temporary hold for review.
These tools do not make flawless decisions. Poor-quality data causes models to miss attacks or block legitimate customers, and accuracy degrades as customer behaviour changes, so institutions should track false alerts, confirmed incidents, detection speed and recovery times while keeping human review for consequential decisions.
AI-assisted monitoring complements routine security work rather than replacing it. Software updates, segregated access, tested backups and incident-response exercises still carry most of the load.
Biometric checks answer only the first of the decisions behind a payment, and the other two depend on records and approvals the bank controls.
NIST’s digital identity guidelines state that biometrics are not secrets and should be combined with a physical authenticator. Phishing-resistant passkeys, confirmation of the beneficiary and amount, and tightly controlled account recovery strengthen the remainder of the payment process.
AI agents introduce a separate permission problem. An agent reading an invoice should not gain authority to change bank details or release funds, and in February 2026 NIST identified agent identity, authorisation, auditability, and prompt-injection defences as areas needing attention.
In practice that means restricted tool access, complete action logs and human approval for sensitive transfers.
World announced a banking and finance identity suite on 18 October 2026, covering an identity wallet, video meetings and AI agents. Proof of Human uses an Orb enrollment to establish uniqueness, Proof of Presence checks a live selfie, and Proof of Attributes can confirm selected facts from government credentials without disclosing the document itself.
World ID for Zoom compares an enrolled image, a live selfie and a frame of meeting video before displaying a Human Badge. It is available through the Zoom Marketplace for eligible enterprise customers, while the agent-delegation tools remain in beta.
Design partners have been named, but no bank has been identified as operating the full suite in production. Tools like this can add assurance about human presence, but a badge does not verify account ownership or approve a transfer, and their accuracy, privacy safeguards, and running costs still require independent evaluation.
Cyberattacks become market events when they interrupt payment processing, settlement, pricing or access to funding. Delays at a major intermediary raise uncertainty about obligations and liquidity, and an outage at shared infrastructure can spread disruption across several institutions at once.
The IMF’s October 2026 Global Financial Stability Report identified increasingly sophisticated cyber threats as a growing systemic concern, and the Federal Reserve’s May 2026 assessment warned that financial-sector incidents could impair market liquidity, weaken confidence and contribute to forced asset sales.
The Financial Stability Board has separately flagged dependence on a small number of technology providers as a vulnerability in its own right.
In foreign exchange, disrupted pricing or funding channels could widen spreads or thin liquidity for a period. These are transmission channels rather than predictions for currencies, bank shares or bond yields, and the effect depends on how long an incident lasts, what alternatives exist and how quickly the affected firms recover.
Supervisors now ask not only whether an AI system detects fraud, but whether it can be audited, restored and operated safely under stress. The EU’s Digital Operational Resilience Act requires covered financial entities to manage technology risk and report major incidents.
European supervisors’ June 2026 overview recorded 3,383 major technology incidents, roughly a third of which had cross-border effects, while only 10% were classified as cybersecurity incidents. Operational resilience therefore has to cover ordinary technology failures as well as deliberate attacks.
Biometric and AI systems raise further questions about consent, data retention, cross-border processing and reliance on vendors. Independent testing, documented fallback procedures and clear accountability matter most where a security control could lock legitimate customers out of their own accounts.
Banks should weigh technology costs against outcomes: prevented account takeovers, fewer unauthorised transfers, acceptable false-rejection rates and faster recovery after an incident. Evidence published from live deployments carries more weight than enrollment totals or launch announcements.
Traders should confirm broker communications through pre-verified channels, protect login credentials, and review any changes to funding details before sending money. EBC clients can check suspected impersonation or unfamiliar links through the EBC Official Verification Centre.
AI cybersecurity in banking extends well beyond detecting convincing fakes. It combines threat monitoring, secure identities, carefully restricted AI agents and the ability to keep essential financial services running during an incident.
World ID illustrates one emerging verification method, not a complete banking defence. For institutions and financial markets, meaningful progress will show up as measurable reductions in fraud, contained disruptions, and reliable recovery.
