# ‘You Can See Everything’ perfectly captures what a decade of post-truth has done to us

Fast Company · Joe Berkowitz · 2026-10-20

A tech-world documentary may seem an unlikely candidate for one of those movies people claim simply must be experienced in a theater. You Can See Everything makes a strong case all the same. There are moments in Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim’s beguiling profile of the disgraced Theranos CEO Elizabeth Holmes that inspire gasps, convulsive laughter, and involuntary hand gestures. One such moment was especially resonant in my theater, though. (And be warned: There are very mild spoilers ahead.)
To put it as vaguely as possible, the moment involves Fielder attempting to pin down Holmes about her history of dishonesty. (As the trailer suggests, there’s a lot of that going on throughout the entire, plus-size runtime.) In this particular instance, Holmes says something so hard to believe, it can only be taken with a Texas-sized grain of salt. Since Fielder is a master of deadpan who has made a career out of soft-spoken understatement, his naked astonishment registers as volcanic: “Liz, what?!”
It’s a very funny moment, but it’s more than that. There’s something cathartic at play, too. A decade into the post-truth era, as powerful people stand 10-toes-down on flagrantly false narratives, it’s the kind of deeply human reaction to dishonesty many Americans have been craving. In his rejection of the line he’s being asked to swallow, Fielder jars viewers out of whatever boiled-frog comfort we may have found in a timeline riddled with unreality, forcing us to see it with fresh eyes.
The post-truth era meets the AI era
You Can See Everything finds Holmes and her husband, Billy Evans, having invited Fielder to observe and interview the Theranos founder in 2023, during the days leading up to her 11-year prison sentence for defrauding investors. Fielder is a canny but bold choice for this mission, as he is a comedian best known for making one TV show about business scams (Nathan For You) and another about his endless fascination with authenticity (The Rehearsal). It’s never explicitly stated how this arrangement came about, but theories abound online.
For his part, Fielder does not seem to view the project as an opportunity to determine whether Holmes was dishonest while leading Theranos. (We’re well past that, as anyone who’s read John Carreyrou’s explosive exposé, Bad Blood, which Fielder has, can vouch.) Instead, his movie suggests that some dishonest people are simply sociopathic, while others are less connected to reality on a neurochemical level, and others still might be somehow both, with Fielder hoping to assign a definitive category to Holmes. His dogged refusal to accept Holmes and Evans’s framing is a clarifying encapsulation of what it’s been like to read the news on any given day in recent years.
Although the rise of partisan media outlets and Twitter echo chambers had been fracturing public belief systems long before 2016, Trump’s election is what thrust us into the post-truth era. Every president lies, but the breadth and scale of Trump’s lies was previously been unheard of in American politics. By making false claims about eminently observable reality, Trump revealed that lies don’t carry consequences if enough powerful people are willing to ignore them. His emergence as the leader and mascot of the United States announced that being factually accurate was no longer as important as simply appearing supremely convinced of one’s own veracity.
This performance of total confidence—as in the “con” in “con man” or “con woman”—has now become standard in just about all forms of mass communication, from wellness influencers with a questionable grasp on nootropics to reality show contestants who definitely aren’t there to develop a personal brand. The just-trust-me shtick seems especially prevalent in today’s tech world, though, which often rewards people for confidently describing an AI future that does not yet exist.
