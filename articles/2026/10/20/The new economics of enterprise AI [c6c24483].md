# The new economics of enterprise AI

MIT Technology Review · David Sydow · 2026-10-20

Sponsored
The new economics of enterprise AI
As enterprise AI shifts from isolated experiments to becoming foundational infrastructure, companies need to focus on efficiency, not just consumption.
Provided byHPE
Imagine your company just discovered electricity. At first only a few machines run on it, and nobody worries about efficiency — consumption is low and the value is obvious. But once electricity becomes infrastructure, every process depends on it, and the question changes from "can we use it?" to "how efficiently are we using it?"
Enterprise AI is going through the same shift. What began as isolated chatbot and copilot experiments is becoming a foundational layer of the business, with models, agents, and data increasingly interconnected in production workloads. The important question stops being what a token or GPU-hour costs. It becomes how efficiently the AI environment converts paid capacity into useful business outcomes.
That's the metric worth tracking: effective cost per useful outcome — the total platform, model, operations, and capacity cost required to complete work the business can actually use, whether a resolved request or a completed workflow.
Three ways to run AI
Utilization is central to the economics. Directing more work to paid-for capacity lowers effective unit cost; idle GPUs, fragmented workloads, and oversized models all raise the cost of every useful result. Enterprises generally choose among three models:
A bespoke stack — procuring infrastructure and controls in-house. This approach is flexible, but it creates a continuing burden, since teams must revalidate the environment as models and policies change.
On-demand consumption — hyperscalers reduce the assembly burden and suit low or uncertain demand, but charges rise with every prompt or agent action.
An integrated private AI platform — owned or dedicated capacity paired with a validated stack. The advantage isn't free inference; it's control over efficiency levers like scheduling, model selection, GPU partitioning, and cache management.
The right model depends on the workload. APIs can be rational for experimentation or variable demand. As workloads become sustained and business-critical, enterprises need to weigh not just what they consume, but how efficiently they operate the system.
Ownership is an operating discipline
Buying a Formula 1 race car and parking it in your garage means owning one of the most sophisticated vehicles ever built — but ownership alone doesn't make you competitive; you still need drivers, mechanics, and track time to justify it. AI infrastructure works the same way.
One of the biggest misconceptions in enterprise AI is that owning infrastructure automatically improves economics. Owning GPUs creates the opportunity for better economics, but only if the organization has sufficient demand and operational discipline to keep resources productive.
The infrastructure that returns the best ROI isn't a reference architecture or a bespoke build-out — it's a fully integrated, qualified stack that lets teams route tasks to the least costly model that meets quality requirements and reuse context through techniques such as KV-cache management, reducing wasted capacity while maintaining responsiveness.
Without these practices, committed infrastructure can become stranded capital. With them, rising demand improves economics as fixed costs are amortized across more useful work — increasingly important in agentic systems, where economics are shaped less by any single prompt than by the efficiency of the complete environment.

Measure what matters
Token consumption remains useful, but it isn't a complete measure of AI economics. A fuller enterprise view tracks cost per business-relevant outcome, accelerator utilization, and model choice relative to task complexity.
Enterprises already operating private AI should ask how mature their utilization is, and how workload volume will change over the next 12 to 18 months. Those earlier in the journey should build these practices in from the first deployment, so strategy anticipates growth before demand becomes a bottleneck.
For low-volume, unpredictable demand, an API or hyperscaler may remain sensible. But as AI becomes a continuous production capability, enterprises have a growing incentive to control utilization, amortize fixed costs, and manage the environment as a long-term operating asset. The strategic question isn't whether enterprises will pay for AI — it's whether they will understand, and ultimately own, the economics behind it.
