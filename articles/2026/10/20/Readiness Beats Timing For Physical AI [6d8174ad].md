# Readiness Beats Timing For Physical AI

Forbes · Rodrigo Madanes, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-20

Rodrigo Madanes, Global Next Frontier Technology & AI Leader at EY.
Physical AI has the potential to reshape any industry that manufactures, moves, inspects, maintains, builds, serves or provides direct care. By combining intelligent software, sensors, simulation, robotics and autonomous systems, it could improve worker safety, relieve labor shortages, raise productivity and change the economics of physical work.
But it will not arrive everywhere at once. Robotics is already operating at scale in some structured environments, while remaining expensive, brittle or difficult to integrate in others.
That unevenness should shape how executives respond, but not deter them. The question is not whether robotics is ready as a technology category as a whole or when it will have its “ChatGPT moment”; it is whether physical AI is becoming ready enough to address a specific task, workflow or operating problem within a business.
Leaders therefore need to understand where adoption is likely to move fastest, how to reduce risk and what capabilities are required to deploy physical AI safely and at scale. Those capabilities extend well beyond the robot itself and include data, trust, value and adoption.
The winners will not necessarily be the companies that predict exactly when robotics takes off. They will be the ones that identify where it can create value now and build the organizational readiness to scale as the technology matures.
Robotics will not arrive evenly.
According to the International Federation of Robotics, 542,000 industrial robots were installed globally in 2024, marking the fourth consecutive year above 500,000 annual installations, while the global operational stock reached nearly 4.7 million. Capital is moving in the same direction: robotics startups raised $15 billion globally in 2025, and had already raised $18.8 billion by June 2026, surpassing the previous 2021 peak.
Yet those encouraging numbers do not mean every sector is equally ready. Robotics adoption is still concentrated where tasks are structured, repeatable, measurable and commercially proven.
Manufacturing, warehousing and logistics are further along because many of these environments have already been highly structured—a good foundation for the current capabilities of physical AI. The priority in these sectors is not simply to buy more robots, but to connect robotics more closely to business operations, workforce planning, data systems and end-to-end performance.
Other sectors face a different challenge. Shipbuilding, construction, energy, infrastructure and others operate in complex, high-risk and unstructured physical environments. Robotics is already creating value in areas such as surgery, rehabilitation, diagnostics, laboratory automation and medical assistance, but broader adoption will depend on clinical workflows, regulation, reimbursement, liability, patient trust and the relationship between care teams and technology.
For example, Fincantieri, one of the world’s largest shipbuilding groups, worked with EY and the EY-NVIDIA Alliance to explore how humanoid robotics and physical AI could improve safety and productivity in shipbuilding. The initiative used a phased, simulation-first approach, with virtual environments built using NVIDIA Omniverse and NVIDIA Isaac technologies before controlled real-world testing.
This approach can serve as a model for evaluating physical AI opportunities without committing immediately to full-scale deployment. Leaders can use simulations to test specific use cases against their own operating environments, safety requirements and business objectives, then move the most promising applications into controlled pilots before determining where broader deployment makes sense.
In these environments, the opportunity may be large, but adoption will be uneven. That does not mean leaders should wait. It means they should start where the environment, task and risk profile are ready enough. Fincantieri’s approach is not based on the assumption that humanoid robots are ready to transform every shipyard tomorrow.
Physical AI will not be plug-and-play for most organizations. A robot may be the most visible part of the system, but the value it delivers depends on the stack around it: data infrastructure, sensors, connectivity, simulation, cybersecurity, governance, workflow integration, workforce readiness and ecosystem partnerships. Without that stack, even advanced robotics can remain isolated from the business processes and performance outcomes it is meant to improve.
There are four pillars of physical AI readiness.
Buying a robot is easy compared with building the enterprise capability to deploy intelligent physical systems safely, responsibly and at scale. Four pillars matter most: data, trust, value and adoption.
1. Data is the foundation.
Physical AI depends on reliable, connected and well-governed operational data. That includes asset data, process data, safety data, workforce data, maintenance data and performance data. Without that foundation, robotics remains disconnected from the business.
2. Trust is the permission to operate.
Physical AI operates near people, equipment and critical assets. Leaders need safety cases, accountability models, compliance processes, cybersecurity, auditability and workforce engagement from the start. Trust cannot be retrofitted after deployment.
3. Value is the reason to act.
The best early use cases should be tied to measurable outcomes: safety, productivity, downtime, quality, resilience, capacity or cost. Robotics initiatives fail when they are framed as technology showcases. They scale when they solve operational problems that matter.
4. Adoption is the path to scale.
Companies need a disciplined route from simulation to pilots to scaled deployment. That means testing use cases safely, learning from controlled environments, engaging the workforce early and building the partnerships required to scale.
That last point is critical. Physical AI will rarely scale inside a company’s four walls alone. In physical AI, that ecosystem may include technology providers, data platforms, infrastructure partners, regulators, universities, workforce organizations and industry peers. The company may be ready to move, but the system around the company also has to be ready enough to support it.
Readiness beats timing.
None of the four pillars of preparation—data, trust, value and adoption—requires a company to bet the business on robotics tomorrow. The point is to create readiness over time: the ability to understand where physical AI can add value, test it safely, build confidence with the workforce and scale responsibly when the opportunity becomes clearer.
Investors often say that time in the market beats timing the market. The same logic applies to physical AI: I believe the winners in robotics will be those that invest in readiness before the tipping point arrives—not just in robots, but in the capability to deploy physical AI safely, responsibly and at scale.
