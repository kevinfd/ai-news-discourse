# Beyond Traditional QA: Building Quality Engineering for Generative AI and Intelligent Applications

Tech Times · Carl Williams · 2026-10-16

Generative AI is rapidly moving from experimental environments into enterprise applications. Organizations are using large language models and AI-powered workflows to interpret documents, generate content, automate processes, support decisions, and interact with increasingly complex business systems. But the ability to generate an answer is not the same as the ability to generate a trustworthy answer.
For conventional software, testing has traditionally centered on predefined requirements and expected outcomes. Generative AI introduces a different challenge. The same input can produce different outputs, retrieved information can influence the response, and changes to models, prompts, or knowledge sources can alter system behavior without a conventional code change.
This has created a new engineering problem: how do organizations continuously validate AI systems that are probabilistic, context-dependent, and increasingly embedded in business-critical workflows?
Santosh Kumar Vududala, a GenAI Automation Architect and quality engineering professional, has focused on this problem through AI-driven test automation, AI assurance, data validation, intelligent workflow testing, and quality engineering for enterprise AI applications. His work illustrates a broader transition taking place in software engineering—from testing whether an application functions according to predefined rules to establishing whether intelligent systems remain reliable, explainable, and appropriately controlled as they evolve.
AI Quality Engineering: Extending Traditional Testing
Traditional QA remains an essential foundation for software development. Functional testing, regression testing, integration testing, API validation, and user-interface testing continue to be important for AI-powered applications.
The challenge is that they are no longer sufficient by themselves.
A generative AI application may technically execute successfully while producing an inaccurate, incomplete, irrelevant, or unsupported response. A RAG application may return a fluent answer based on poor retrieval. An AI-powered workflow may complete successfully while making an incorrect interpretation of the underlying business information.
Vududala's approach treats these situations as quality-engineering problems rather than simply model problems.
His documented work includes an AI-driven automation framework designed to interpret requirements, generate user stories, and create comprehensive test scenarios covering positive and negative conditions. The approach extends automation beyond executing predefined scripts and toward using AI to assist in the creation and analysis of the testing process itself. This creates a more continuous relationship between requirements and validation. Instead of testing only after software has been implemented, AI-assisted quality engineering can help identify potential scenarios earlier, expand test coverage, and provide engineers with additional ways to examine how an intelligent application may behave. The objective is not to replace the quality engineer. It is to give the quality engineer a more capable set of tools.
Building AI-Driven Test Automation
One of the emerging areas in quality engineering is using generative AI to automate parts of the testing lifecycle. This can include interpreting business requirements, identifying possible test conditions, generating test scenarios, creating positive and negative cases, and assisting with validation activities.
Traditional test automation generally follows instructions that have already been defined. AI-driven automation can help transform less structured information into potential testing requirements. That creates opportunities to connect business analysis, software development, and quality engineering more closely.
Vududala's work has incorporated this model into enterprise automation, with intelligent agents supporting activities that traditionally required manual interpretation. According to documented professional feedback, the framework extends beyond conventional QA into areas such as requirements analysis, test design, and AI-enabled development workflows.
However, automation does not eliminate the need for human judgment. AI-generated test cases still need to be reviewed against the actual business requirements. A technically valid test can still be the wrong test if it does not represent the intended business behavior. That makes human oversight a central part of effective AI quality engineering.
Testing the Full RAG and AI Application Pipeline
Retrieval-Augmented Generation has become an important architecture for enterprise AI because organizations frequently need models to work with proprietary or domain-specific information.
But RAG also introduces additional quality dimensions.
A simplified AI application may involve a sequence such as:
User request → retrieval → context → prompt → model → response → business validation
Each stage can introduce a different type of failure.
The retrieval layer may select irrelevant information. The context may be incomplete. The prompt may fail to communicate the intended instruction. The model may generate information that is not supported by the retrieved context. The final response may technically sound reasonable while failing a business rule. For quality engineers, this means testing cannot stop at the final response. The components surrounding the model also require validation.
Vududala's quality-engineering work incorporates data validation, AI-driven workflows, API and application testing, and automated quality controls. His documented technical responsibilities include validating AI-driven workflows and establishing quality practices across enterprise systems built on modern AI and cloud technologies.
This broader perspective is increasingly important as organizations move from isolated AI demonstrations toward integrated enterprise applications.
AI Assurance in Regulated Environments
The stakes become higher when AI is introduced into regulated industries.
In such environments, organizations need more than functional success. They need confidence that systems behave within defined boundaries, that important outputs can be validated, and that appropriate controls exist when automated processing encounters uncertainty.
Vududala's work has involved AI-driven document intelligence, automated workflows, data validation, and quality engineering within a regulated financial environment. Professional recommendations describing his work emphasize AI assurance, automated controls, auditability, and responsible adoption of automation in environments where accuracy and regulatory requirements are significant considerations.
This changes how quality engineering is approached. An AI system should not simply be evaluated on whether it produces an answer; engineers also need to consider whether the answer is supported by available information, whether the system follows the intended workflow, whether sensitive information is handled appropriately, whether exceptions are identified, whether decisions can be reviewed, and whether sufficient evidence exists to understand how the system behaved. This is where AI assurance begins to extend beyond conventional software QA, and quality becomes part of governance.
Human-in-the-Loop Validation and Continuous Testing
One of the most important principles in enterprise AI is recognizing where human judgment remains necessary. AI can accelerate document processing, generate testing scenarios, identify potential defects, and support workflow automation. But organizations still need mechanisms for reviewing ambiguous or high-risk situations.
Vududala's documented approach incorporates human-in-the-loop review of AI-generated outputs. The objective is to use automation to increase engineering productivity while retaining human oversight for validation and important decisions. This also connects directly to continuous testing.
AI applications are not static systems: A model can change, a prompt can change, a retrieval source can change, application logic can change, and new types of input can appear. A quality strategy that works only at the time of initial deployment can therefore become outdated.
Continuous AI testing requires reusable evaluation scenarios that can be executed when important components change. Regression testing must expand beyond traditional application behavior to include AI-specific characteristics such as response quality, relevance, grounding, consistency, and adherence to business requirements. The goal is to identify behavioral changes before they become production problems. That represents a fundamental shift from testing before deployment to continuous evaluation throughout the AI lifecycle.
The Future of Quality Engineering for AI
As AI systems become more capable, the role of the quality engineer is likely to become broader rather than smaller. The quality engineer of the future may need to understand traditional automation, data engineering, AI models, retrieval systems, application architecture, security, governance, and business processes simultaneously.
Vududala's career reflects this convergence. His documented responsibilities extend beyond traditional QA into AI solution design, requirements analysis, automation architecture, release governance, and enterprise quality strategy. His broader professional contribution also includes peer-reviewed research, conference participation, speaking, peer review, and mentoring.
The next stage of AI adoption will therefore require more than increasingly capable models. Organizations will need engineering disciplines capable of answering a more fundamental question: Can we demonstrate that an intelligent system continues to behave appropriately as the technology, data, and business environment change? That is the emerging role of AI quality engineering. It moves testing from a final checkpoint to a continuous engineering discipline that combines automation with human judgment, technical validation with governance, and model evaluation with end-to-end system assurance.
As enterprise AI becomes increasingly embedded in critical workflows, the ability to demonstrate reliability may become just as important as the ability to demonstrate intelligence.
