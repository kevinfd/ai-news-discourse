# AI is flooding creative markets, but is it pushing humans out?

Earth.com · Eric Ralls · 2026-10-16

AI is flooding creative markets, but is it pushing humans out?
AI-generated books and images may compete with human creators, but new research questions whether that competition violates copyright law.
Artificial intelligence can produce a book in minutes, doing work that might take a human author months or years. If enough of those books flood the market, will readers still buy the ones written by people? And if human authors can no longer compete, will they eventually stop writing?
A federal judge gave that worry a name last year, market dilution, and suggested authors could win copyright cases against AI companies if they could show it happening. Three copyright scholars examined that argument and found that market dilution has no clear definition, while existing studies offer conflicting evidence about whether AI is actually harming human creators.
Pamela Samuelson of Berkeley Law wrote the argument with Abhishek Nagaraj of Berkeley’s Haas School of Business and Christopher Jon Sprigman of NYU School of Law. It’s a policy essay, not an experiment, and it takes a side. Courts, the authors argued, shouldn’t accept dilution claims without proof tied to a specific AI system and a specific market.
The stakes reach past novelists. More than 100 lawsuits in the United States are testing whether training AI on copyrighted books and images is legal. The scientific labs that build and use these models have a stake in the answer.
How AI market dilution entered copyright law
Richard Kadrey and 12 other book authors sued Meta for training its Llama models on their books. In June 2025, the judge ruled for Meta. The training was fair use, the rule that lets people use copyrighted work without permission for purposes such as research and commentary.
But the judge added a warning. AI could produce so many works so cheaply that it floods the market and weakens the incentive for humans to create. Romance novels and gardening books might suffer, he suggested, while memoirs and famous authors might not. The judge wrote that if Kadrey had provided evidence of that harm, Meta would have lost its fair use defense.
The same idea appears in other lawsuits and in a U.S. Copyright Office report from May 2025, which the authors said mentioned it briefly and offered no evidence.
Competing was never copying
Copyright, the authors wrote, has always asked one question about harm. Does the copy take sales from the specific work it copied? A song that lifts a melody can infringe. A book that uses the same ideas and competes for the same readers, without copying protected expression, has not traditionally been considered copyright infringement.
Market dilution flips that. It says AI outputs can harm authors as a class simply by competing with them, even when no output copies anything from any one book. The authors called that a conflict with a central norm of copyright law, and they said neither the judge nor the Copyright Office has defined what dilution means.
The authors’ first objection is the word “AI.” Many new works are hybrids, written by people with AI help.
Whether a work counts as human-authored, they wrote, depends on what role the AI played and how much human effort went in. The share of words a machine produced is not the whole test. Counting every hybrid as dilution would let a drop of AI erase the human author.
Stock photo research shows mixed results
The essay takes a closer look at the studies cited by supporters of the market dilution theory. When one large stock photo marketplace began allowing AI-made images, uploads rose 136% and the number of contributing artists rose 47%.
Uploads of non-AI photos fell 15%, and the number of artists posting them fell 29%. On those figures alone, AI looks like it crowded people out.
Other findings complicate that picture. Sales rose 82%, and of 1.7 million new images, almost half came from sellers who were already on the platform. The site also labeled any image made with AI assistance as AI, so that category likely held many hybrid works.
AI-generated books raise more questions
A second study of self-published genre fiction on Amazon found that books with at least 25% machine-written text cut into revenue per book. The study reported nothing on fully AI-written books. And of the 824 sellers who released one of those books, 374 had published a human-written one before.
A third study of millions of Amazon books from 2022 to 2025 found that the catalog nearly tripled in size, yet the number and quality of wholly human-written books held steady. Another experiment found readers preferred AI passages to human ones, but only when the model had been trained on everything the imitated author ever wrote. The scholars said that is unlikely to be an important market.
Asked by Earth.com about the essay’s main point, Samuelson said the research so far is inconclusive. “The article explains why empirical studies thus far are equivocal and do not adequately take account of hybrid works which are human authored but AI assisted,” she told Earth.com.
Lawmakers could address broader market harm
Suppose dilution turns out to be real in some corner of the market. Even then, the authors argued, a lawsuit between one author and one AI developer is the wrong place to fix it. The harm would fall on a whole community of writers or photographers, and a judge deciding one case can’t measure a market-wide effect or design a remedy for it.
Legislatures could. In Europe, some scholars have proposed making AI developers pay into a fund for human authors. The payment could be a condition of a license to train on copyrighted works, or a levy on commercial AI systems. The authors said a collective harm would need a collective answer.
Two of the three authors disclosed ties to the AI side of the dispute. Nagaraj has served as an economics expert witness for defendants in AI copyright cases, and Sprigman has consulted for an industry trade association on copyright and AI.
Researchers need better evidence of AI harm
Whether market dilution exists is unknown, and the essay says so. The studies that exist infer AI use from the finished text with imperfect detection tools. Those tools can’t tell a human idea written up by a machine from a machine draft polished by a human.
The authors laid out what a convincing study would need. It would start with a clear definition of dilution and a way to separate hybrid works from fully automated ones. It would also distinguish newcomers from established creators who use AI to compete with their own earlier work.
Tracking authors as they enter and leave creative markets, paired with surveys of how they work, could answer the question without any company’s private data, they wrote.
Until that evidence arrives, the authors wrote, a ruling that AI developers are diluting markets would raise the legal risk for everyone who builds or uses these models. That includes the open-source and specialized models that scientists depend on.
The full study was published in the journal Science.
