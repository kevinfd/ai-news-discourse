# IMF chief urges cooperation on AI and war risks, as US hails economic momentum

Channel NewsAsia · CNA · 2026-10-16

IMF chief urges cooperation on AI and war risks, as US hails economic momentum
BANGKOK, Oct 16 : The head of the IMF on Friday appealed to global leaders to cooperate in the face of multiple economic risks, including rapid advances in AI and a prolonged war in the Middle East, as Washington hailed what it sees as US-led economic momentum.
Their contrasting views echoed throughout this year's annual meetings of the International Monetary Fund and World Bank in Bangkok, as finance officials and central bankers from around the world took stock of a global economy rocked by high inflation and rising interest rates that have kept growth sluggish and increased inequality.
The IMF on Tuesday slightly raised its global growth forecast to 3.1 per cent in 2026, well below the historical average, as it flagged significant growth and financial stability risks from a potential ending of the AI investment boom, growing debt levels and the ongoing Middle East conflict.
Absent the US-Israeli-led war against Iran and Tehran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz that triggered a massive global energy shock, the IMF has said it would have expected AI to have fueled much stronger growth.
On Friday, IMF chief Kristalina Georgieva said the sheer speed of technological change added to the long list of challenges, including disruptions to the global trading system, high food and energy prices and lingering wars, all of which threatened to widen the gulf between advanced economies and the world's poor.
Bond markets, she said, were clearly sensing the risks, with US, German and Japanese 10-year sovereign yields reaching their highest levels in decades.
"The need of the hour is for urgent and practical international cooperation," Georgieva, the first IMF chief from an emerging market economy, said at a plenary session on Friday. She told a subsequent panel that she spent sleepless nights worrying about how to regulate AI and ensure that poorer countries weren't left behind by its breakneck-speed development.
US Treasury Under Secretary Erin Browne, in a call with reporters, pushed back against those warnings, hailing record highs on the US stock market, strong US economic output and improvements in Japan's economy.
"The IMF needs to take a more balanced approach to assessing the global economic outlook," she said, adding: "The global economy has demonstrated significant resilience with strong US growth helping to support broader economic and financial stability."
She said that the Fund should give equal consideration to "opportunities for positive economic shocks, particularly those driven by American innovation and super intelligence, energy abundance, and productivity-enhancing reforms."
US President Donald Trump's policies on tax, trade, energy and deregulation were paving the way for stronger investment, higher productivity and durable economic growth, she said.
AI JITTERS HIT STOCKS
Emerging market stocks have been volatile in recent weeks, roiled by expectations of higher US interest rates that have sent borrowing costs sharply higher. Major markets have also been hit by the growing concerns over the AI investment cycle.
South Korean shares posted their biggest weekly fall in 18 years as investors questioned the sustainability of the AI boom, while Japan's stock market lost 5.4 per cent over the week.
Eli Remolona, governor of the Philippines' Bangko Sentral ng Pilipanas, said in an IMF panel discussion earlier in the week that he was concerned Asia had not learned the lessons of its destructive financial crisis in the late 1990s.
Before the Asian financial crisis broke out in 1997, economies drew huge capital inflows that prompted the World Bank to describe it as the "East Asian miracle," Remolona said.
"Investment poured into Asia, and then you had the crisis because it wasn't really a miracle. I think we have to remind ourselves about that. We have to guard against that kind of crisis," he said on Wednesday. "We have not really been making a lot of effort looking at that kind of crisis."
Illustrating the vulnerability to sudden shifts, Indonesian stocks fell 11.6 per cent on Friday, their steepest one-day fall in 28 years, after global index provider MSCI said the Indonesian market was no longer worthy of "emerging market" status.
Officials at the IMF and World Bank this week said they are seeing elevated demand for grant and loan programs.
IMF strategy chief Christian Mummsen said the IMF's low-interest lending to low-income countries had quadrupled since the COVID pandemic, and demand was expected to remain strong. About 60 per cent of the IMF's financing since COVID had gone to low-income countries, he said.
Developing countries are increasingly outspoken about their concerns. Barbados this week joined a group of countries calling for the creation of an international panel to address inequality, citing geopolitical tensions, climate change and rapid technological advances that could further divide wealthy and poorer nations.
