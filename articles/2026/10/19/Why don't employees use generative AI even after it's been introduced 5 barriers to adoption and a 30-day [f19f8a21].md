# Why don't employees use generative AI even after it's been introduced? 5 barriers to adoption and a 30-day plan to get the team moving⭐

note · ISO · 2026-10-19

Why don't employees use generative AI even after it's been introduced? 5 barriers to adoption and a 30-day plan to get the team moving⭐
“We’ve set up an environment where all employees can use it, but only a few actually do.”
“It was exciting during the training, but by the following week, everyone was back to their old ways of working.”
For those in HR, DX promotion, and corporate planning, this situation is frustrating. Once you’ve spent the money to introduce a tool, you want to see its use spread. However, the more you call for its use, the wider the gap in enthusiasm between management and the front lines can become.
To put it simply, it is important not to attribute the reason generative AI isn't being used solely to employee motivation or IT skills.
I believe that usage only becomes part of the daily routine when the “reason to use it for work,” “rules for trying it out safely,” and “mechanisms that make people want to try it” are all in place.
In this article, I will organize the five reasons why generative AI fails to take root and practical measures that HR, DX, and corporate planning teams can work on together.
Even if adoption progresses, it doesn't necessarily mean it is being “mastered.”
According to the IPA’s “DX Trends 2026,” the percentage of domestic companies that responded they have “introduced” generative AI increased from 22.6% in the 2024 survey to 44.0% in the 2025 survey.
On the other hand, the following items are cited as challenges in the introduction and operation of AI, including generative AI.
- “Lack of specialized personnel” 50.1%
- “Lack of understanding regarding the effects and risks of generative AI” 45.8%
- “Difficulty in creating rules and standards to manage appropriate use” 39.7%
Source: IPA “DX Trends 2026”
This is a survey of companies and is not a figure that directly measures the “reasons why employees don't use it.” Even so, it provides a clue as to why simply providing the tool is not enough.
Reason 1: “What to use it for” is left up to the employees
Even if told to “please use generative AI for your favorite tasks,” employees busy with their daily work may not even have the time to think about how to use it.
Instead of starting with major business reforms, I recommend choosing one small task that occurs every week.
For example, for HR, it could be drafting training announcements; for general affairs, drafting headings for internal FAQs; and for corporate planning, identifying points to be discussed in meetings.
In all cases, start with a draft that is checked by a human, rather than leaving the final output to the AI.
Focus on “which part of my usual work will become easier” rather than “what can generative AI do.”
I believe this order is easier for teams to understand.
Reason 2: Anxiety about 'what if I make a mistake' remains
What if it outputs incorrect information? What if I accidentally input confidential company information?
Simply telling employees who have these concerns that they should use it because it's 'convenient' will not make them feel secure.
The Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry have published the 'AI Business Guidelines (Version 1.2),' encouraging businesses that use AI to take measures based on risks. Additionally, the IPA has released educational materials for users to learn the basics of security.
Reference: AI Business Guidelines / IPA 'Security Tips for AI Users'
What teams need is not just thick rulebooks.
They need guidance they can act on immediately, such as 'Which tools are permitted by the company,' 'What information must not be input,' and 'Who should verify the output.'
In particular, it is a prerequisite to check the terms of service and internal company regulations, and not to input personal or confidential information lightly.
Reason 3: Training was held once and then finished
Even if they can operate it during training, they might get stuck the next day when they are alone, wondering 'what kind of instructions should I write?' Such a situation is plausible.
Therefore, instead of just training materials, why not prepare 'examples by task that can be tried as-is' first?
For example, instructions like the following:
'Draft a notice for internal training in a structure that conveys the target audience, date and time, and application method. Please use placeholders for proper nouns and dates.'
With examples like these, it becomes easier to try it out without inputting personal information.
It is not just about teaching how to operate it, but also sharing how to fix it when the output is inaccurate.
I believe this repetition is what supports adoption.
Reason 4: The manager's way of working has not changed
'Use generative AI,' says the manager, but they don't actually welcome drafts created using AI. Or, using it is perceived as cutting corners.
In such an environment, wouldn't employees feel it is safer not to use it?
What managers should show first is not a flashy success story.
It is a familiar use case, such as,
"I used AI to organize the agenda for this meeting, and then I made some revisions."
At the same time, acknowledge that there are tasks where choosing not to use it is the right decision.
In situations that affect individual rights or treatment, such as hiring decisions or personnel evaluations, humans must take responsibility for judgments rather than taking generative AI output at face value.
Reason 5: Using "number of uses" as the only metric for success
The number of users and login frequency can be indicators of the adoption status.
However, even if it is used every day, it cannot be called a success if it actually increases the amount of revision work.
What you should look at is whether "work time has decreased," "content quality has been maintained," and "the burden on the person in charge has been lightened."
For example, in the case of a document draft, compare the conventional creation time with the time spent creating, checking, and revising when using AI.
If time is saved but errors increase, review how instructions are given or which tasks are being targeted.
I believe it is more important to use the numbers as material for improving work rather than for evaluating employees.
A small adoption plan to try in the first 30 days
There is no need to roll it out to the entire company all at once. You can verify it on a small scale as follows:
Week 1: Select tasks
Recruit collaborators from departments like HR, General Affairs, or Corporate Planning, and select recurring tasks one by one. Also, confirm the security of the information being handled.
Week 2: Standardize usage
Summarize examples of prompts tailored to the tasks, information that is prohibited from being entered, and points that need to be checked into a single document.
Week 3: Try it in practice
Have volunteers use it for a portion of their actual work and record any difficulties or rework. If it is difficult to use, there is no need to force its continuation.
Week 4: Reviewing the results
Check the work time, quality, and whether it was used with peace of mind, then decide which tasks to continue and which to review.
At this stage, it is easier to divide roles by having HR handle learning and evaluation systems, the DX department handle tools and security, and corporate planning handle objectives and performance indicators.
The important thing is not to decide only within the departments in charge, but to include the voices of the employees who actually use it.
Frequently Asked Question: Should use be mandatory for all employees?
In my opinion, there is no need to make it uniformly mandatory from the initial stage.
This is because the effects and risks differ depending on the task.
First, build a track record with tasks where the value of using it is easy to understand, and share the results and points of caution. After that, incorporating it into necessary work is a more reasonable way to proceed, isn't it?
Finally, a word
The adoption of generative AI does not start with "making" employees use it, but with "creating a state" where employees can use it for their work.
When it is not being used despite being introduced, before thinking about additional training or usage promotion campaigns, try asking the front lines, "What should I use it for?", "Can I use it with peace of mind?", and "Do I feel glad I used it?"
You should be able to find your next move in those answers⭐
