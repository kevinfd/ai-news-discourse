# New studies explore AI expectations, jobs impact and gender gap

Music Ally · Stuart Dredge · 2026-10-19

Beyond the AI-music debates that have been gripping our industry, there’s a lot to think about in terms of the other ways AI will affect our jobs and wider society. Several studies published in recent days offer new perspectives on that.
Start with a pair of reports from Anthropic. One identifies an “AI expectations gap” between what US adults expect AI to be able to do in the coming years, and what Anthropic’s own employees (who should know, right?) think.
“Anthropic staff expect capabilities to advance faster, AI to be used more widely and with less human involvement, and the time it saves on a task to be much larger,” is the summary.
“When it comes to their own job, over half of Anthropic staff expect AI to be able to do at least 80% of their current work by 2030, compared with 5% of US workers,” is a more-specific (and concerning) difference.
Talking of employment, Anthropic’s second study looks at how AI has changed its own hiring. “Anthropic has been hiring for a smaller share of junior technical positions. This occurred as hours worked by AI agents relative to employees rapidly expanded,” it revealed.
The study also suggested that “this acceleration makes human attention the bottleneck for agent output”. In other words, humans’ jobs are increasingly about overseeing what the AI agents have done – but they’re doing so much, the humans can’t keep up.
But about those junior jobs. While focused on coding in Anthropic’s case, this is a live debate in the music and creative industries too.
While some companies are cutting junior positions, some commentators are warning that this is storing up trouble for the future in terms of where their next wave of senior professionals come from, and how they learn judgement and taste.
Finally today, this study from the Pew Research Center is worth a look. It explores “the gender gap in AI” by examining whether men and women use this technology differently.
While they’re equally likely to be using AI, “men report using chatbots more regularly than women and are more likely to turn to them for a variety of things, including work,” noted Pew. “Women, on the other hand, are more skeptical about AI, including how it will impact their own lives.”
