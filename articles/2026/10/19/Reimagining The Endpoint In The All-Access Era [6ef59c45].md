# Reimagining The Endpoint In The All-Access Era

Forbes · Kevin Greenway, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-19

Kevin Greenway, CTO at 10ZiG, has 25+ years in IT and leads technology and product strategy, with a focus on VDI and End User Computing.
Before the advent of AI and the pandemic catapulted us into remote work, the endpoint was the center of our work universe. People accessed applications they needed from a few devices, with one typically dominating. Today, the endpoint no longer rules how we work or access data or programs. Workers can retrieve AI-generated models from the cloud. Their user profile gives them access to work tools, freeing them from dependence on one PC and enabling the location and device flexibility they now expect.
Virtualization and cloud computing are driving the all-access era, making application delivery flexible for worker needs, devices and locations. Data now resides in the cloud, on-premises data centers and, less often, on the device itself.
Workers enjoy the freedom of choice, but that freedom comes with new challenges.
Endpoint Sticking Points
Businesses now manage a more complex computing environment from the worker, staff and security perspective. Employees still value flexibility in remote or hybrid work arrangements, according to McKinsey’s “HR Monitor 2026” survey, as greater employee control over the time and place of work is a key retention lever.
Employees’ desire for device flexibility is driving the new approach to endpoints, spurring the BYOD trend. This shift has also changed IT staff’s responsibilities to manage a growing number of devices workers use, driving significant growth in the unified endpoint management (UEM) software market. A 2025 Straits Research report estimated the UEM market “will grow from USD 8.95 billion in 2026 to USD 59.23 billion by 2034.”
Managing hundreds or thousands of endpoints competes for IT resources with agentic AI and other AI initiatives. Add shadow AI and shadow IT risks, and businesses face even greater resource constraints in defending against cyberattacks. A 2025 Malwarebytes threat study cited fully or partially unprotected endpoints and servers as a persistent favorite of attackers. Calling them “blind spots,” they enable attackers to work unobserved by countermeasures such as endpoint detection and response.
PC Complexity
PCs dominate the global market, making them a prime target. Threat defense includes staying up to date on the latest security software, managing thousands of PCs with variations of stored data and files, plus staying current on patch updates. It’s a significant workload for short-staffed IT teams.
PCs locally store sensitive data, files and applications, resulting in a promising attack surface for compromising a corporate network. If that PC happens to be someone’s shadow IT unprotected device, the threat increases.
Apart from security defense, other dynamics make PCs a complex investment. A June 2026 IDC study showed that PCs have a three-year life cycle, after which the cost of running and maintaining them spikes. Security and software solution price increases, while IT staff time requirements, unplanned downtime and user productivity losses contribute to the costs.
IDC noted that organizations refreshing PCs after three years reduced their five-year, per-device total cost of ownership (TCO) by 14%, or $1,993 per PC, compared to organizations that hold the same device for five years, even after accounting for the full cost of purchasing and deploying a new PC at the beginning of year four.
When organizations replace a business PC, the base price is just the start. Ongoing costs include managing the PC and its applications, supporting the latest Windows OS, addressing performance and compatibility issues and ensuring old devices are disconnected from the network. Each refresh can also cause downtime as workers adjust to a new device. Energy is another consideration, as fully loaded PCs can consume significant power.
A January 2026 IDC report called the PC market “volatile” and didn’t expect price relief in the near future. Vendors are focusing on midrange and premium-priced devices to offset rising component costs such as memory chips. These pressures are affecting demand. A June 2026 IDC forecast estimated an 11.3% decline in global PC sales in 2026, with average selling prices around $1,100 and only a slight decline by 2028.
Thin Client Alternatives
Modern thin clients can reduce operational complexity while improving defense against attackers. They don’t store applications or data, thereby greatly reducing the attack surface. Following zero-trust principles, they control administrative access, limiting user-caused vulnerability.
These computers boot from a known “good” state managed by a Linux operating system. This prevents any permanent changes to the device and is particularly important in occupations such as healthcare where shift workers share endpoints.
They’re also compatible with VDI, DaaS, cloud or on-premises application delivery, allowing for device flexibility.
Thin clients have a longer hardware shelf life. There’s no continuous refresh since Windows, patching and OS updates are centrally administered, ensuring a consistent computing environment. This reduces staff time, help desk calls and downtime.
They support sustainability with a smaller footprint and lower energy consumption. Breaking the refresh cycle means they stay out of landfills.
Evaluating Thin Clients
Thin clients are purpose-built to provide secure, managed connectivity to remotely delivered applications and desktops. They’re not intended to replicate a traditional PC by providing large amounts of local processing power, RAM or storage.
When considering thin clients, it’s important to validate whether an organization’s required applications and data can be delivered remotely. If they’re installed locally on the thin client instead, CPU, memory and storage requirements increase, and some of its fundamental benefits begin to diminish.
It’s best to evaluate thin clients’ TCO rather than hardware purchase price. They reduce ongoing operational and support costs through centralized management, simplified endpoints and reduced local maintenance while deployed as a longer-term five-year to seven-year investment.
From a broader perspective, vendors and customers need to focus less on old notions of thin clients and more on how the modern workspace has made them a more viable endpoint option.
Endpoint’s Future
How workers use and perceive endpoints will continue to evolve as technologies, such as AI, reshape the way we work. Businesses can use this shift to reassess their endpoint strategy and determine whether a different mix of PCs and thin clients could better meet their cost, security and operational needs.
