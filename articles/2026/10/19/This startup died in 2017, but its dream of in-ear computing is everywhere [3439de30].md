# This startup died in 2017, but its dream of in-ear computing is everywhere

Fast Company · Harry McCracken · 2026-10-19

Few elements of Silicon Valley’s culture are more cherished than its embrace of failure. Nobody looks askance at you for starting a company that doesn’t make it. Indeed, it’s widely regarded as a badge of honor, proving your willingness to make bold, risky bets and leaving you a smarter, more battle-tested veteran.
And yet failed startups tend to be forgotten the moment their doom is sealed. Aside from a few flops so epic they become legends—hello, Webvan and Humane—defunct businesses quickly stop ringing a bell. Everyone involved moves on to chasing the next big thing.
That has been true of many intriguing upstarts I’ve profiled over the years. The one that set out to be the Uber of shipping. The one that partnered with Steph Curry to build a tiny, minimally distracting smartphone. The one that aspired to build an AI-powered computer for the ears well before AI became the industry’s obsession.
But wait. Though that last startup, Doppler Labs, ceased to be back in 2017, its vision didn’t die along with it. Doppler’s flagship product was Here One, a pair of wireless earbuds designed to do far more than stream music and make phone calls. The company developed technology to perform feats such as intelligently mixing streamed audio with live ambient sound. It hoped to help people with hearing loss get a better listening experience than that offered by traditional hearing aids, at a much lower price. It was working on live translation that would let people speaking different languages chat in real time.
When Doppler was founded in 2013, these goals were exceptionally forward-looking. At the time, nobody had even figured out how to incorporate Bluetooth into earbuds that worked reliably and offered acceptable battery life. Those were table stakes that Doppler had to achieve along with its loftier vision.
As you already know, a key moment in the history of wireless earbuds—no, the key moment—happened when Apple released the first AirPods. That happened in December 2016, months before Here One hit stores. Apple didn’t position AirPods as tiny computers. Instead, it was happy to have people think of them as wireless headphones—just remarkably convenient ones with high-quality audio and long battery life.
When the AirPods Pro followed in 2019, however, Apple watchers did describe them as a computer for your ears. As AirPods became one of Apple’s most important product lines—the company shipped 16.8 million units in the second quarter that year alone, according to research firm Omdia—they grew increasingly capable. Eventually, they checked off many of the boxes in Doppler’s original vision, from mixing streaming and ambient sound to real-time translation.
