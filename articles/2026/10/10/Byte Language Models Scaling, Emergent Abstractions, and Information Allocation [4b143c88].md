# Byte Language Models: Scaling, Emergent Abstractions, and Information Allocation

Lobsters · arxiv.org via Yogthos · 2026-10-10

Byte Language Models: Scaling, Emergent Abstractions, and Information Allocation
Abstract
Tokenizer-free language models remove the inductive bias of fixed tokenizers by modeling text directly as bytes, but the resulting longer sequences substantially increase computation and eliminate explicit text abstractions. We ask whether this additional computation can be useful, and whether standard Transformers can learn the abstractions that tokenization provides. We study these questions on Transformers without specialized tokenization-related architectures. With token-superposition training and hash embeddings, byte Transformers consistently outperform subword Transformers as model size scales. We further find that byte Transformers build local text abstractions as external tokenizers: a set of segmentation-like positions are used to collect local context representations, and restricting up to \(25\%\) of intermediate layers to these local representations preserves downstream performance. Finally, these learned structures induce highly non-uniform generation difficulty, with uncertainty concentrated near local structure boundaries; exploiting them for speculative decoding yields \(3.4\times\) more accepted tokens than in subword Transformers.
1 Introduction
Tokenizer-free language models, or byte language models, aim to remove externally imposed tokenizers by modeling text directly as byte sequences (Xue et al., 2022; YU et al., 2023; Slagle, 2024; Tsiamas et al., 2025; Shah et al., 2026). This formulation provides a universal input space and a new joint learning setting. However, it also introduces two main challenges. First, byte sequences are longer than subword sequences. If a typical subword contains K (usually around 4) bytes, a byte model must process K times more sequence positions. Second, prior studies suggest that effective language modeling benefits from prior abstractions over raw text. Directly modeling bytes can therefore underperform tokenized models, despite having a more general input representation.
Recent works (BLT Pagnoni et al., 2025, H-Net Hwang et al., 2026, ByteFlow Deng et al., 2026) develop new network architectures with built-in tokenization mechanisms. For example, in BLT, local encoder layers first encode byte streams into variable-sized chunks, then global layers operate on the chunks, and local decoder layers decode chunks back to bytes. While these architectures demonstrate the promise of joint tokenization, they often need specialized training and optimization strategies, making it less clear how much of the observed gains are from the joint formulation. In this work, we retain the flat Transformer architecture (without the explicit local-global-local hierarchy) and investigate the two challenges underlying byte-level language modeling: Can the additional computation caused by longer sequences be beneficial, and how does a Transformer recover useful text abstractions directly from bytes?
We study the scaling behavior of byte Transformers, and the main findings are: 1) with two modeling ingredients (token-superposition training (Peng et al., 2026) and hash embeddings), byte Transformers consistently outperform subword Transformers when scaling the number of parameters (Figure 1a). 2) Byte models remain effective as training data continues to scale. Given a fixed FLOPs budget (Figure 1c), the optimal proportion between data and parameters is around 1.5 to 3 times that of subword models. These results motivate a different perspective on sequence length: rather than treating longer sequences as a defect, they can provide additional computation under the same parameter budget, which is a useful scaling axis for language models. Further evaluations on downstream text-only and vision–language tasks show that byte Transformers significantly improve performance on tasks that require fine-grained perception (around \(40\%\) relative improvements over subword Transformers on CUTE scores, \(20\%\) on OCRBench (Liu et al., 2024)).
We next investigate how a byte Transformer develops the abstractions that tokenization provides explicitly. By studying an input-copying task on byte models, we find that copying actions need to attend only to certain segmentation positions (not necessarily whitespace), where information from local text pieces can be read out. Based on this finding, we further constrain a range of intermediate layers (up to \(25\%\) of all layers) to access only these locally aggregated representations, resembling a global modeling stage over learned local structures. The resulting model retains downstream performance without additional training. This suggests that the apparent hierarchy of local abstraction and global reasoning is not merely imposed by tokenizer-free architectures such as BLT and H-Net, but can also emerge implicitly in the byte Transformer.
Finally, we study whether these learned local structures can be exploited during inference. By plotting the per-byte loss distribution, we find that byte Transformers have much higher variance than subword models: many byte positions have near-zero loss (in bits per byte), while substantially larger losses concentrate near the boundaries of the learned local structures. This observation suggests that the extra generation steps are not uniformly expensive in terms of model uncertainty. We therefore apply speculative decoding to construct local structures during inference (Wang et al., 2024; Kallini et al., 2026), and show that a drafting model on byte Transformers can produce \(3.4\times\) more accepted tokens than on subword Transformers.
To summarize, our results support a simple view of tokenizer-free language modeling: longer byte sequences provide useful additional computation. At the same time, the model can learn useful local abstractions internally rather than relying on a fixed tokenizer. This perspective suggests sequence length, learned abstraction, and computation allocation as closely connected dimensions for future language-model design.
2 Scaling Byte Transformer Language Models
We first ask how the additional tokens in byte sequences affect model quality under parameter and compute constraints. We then examine which task capabilities benefit from byte resolution and how shared depth and sparse capacity change these trade-offs.
2.1 Building Byte Models
We first establish a controlled comparison between byte- and subword-level Transformers. Both models are trained from scratch with a mixed attention model. 11 1 We generally follow the setting of Qwen3.5 (Qwen Team, 2026), which interleaves Gated DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2025) and Gated Softmax Attention (Qiu et al., 2025) in a \(3:1\) ratio. We occasionally call the architecture flat Transformer, in contrast to architectures with an explicit local-global-local hierarchy. The byte model uses a vocabulary of size \(256\), while the subword model uses a \(49,152\) token vocabulary from the cosmo2-tokenizer (Allal et al., 2025). We use “token” to refer to both byte and subword, depending on the model in context.
Directly applying the standard training recipe to bytes is suboptimal. Byte sequences contain substantially more tokens, while each byte token carries less local semantic context than a subword token. We find the following two ingredients are effective for training byte models.
First, we use token-superposition training (TST; Peng et al., 2026) to improve learning efficiency from the longer byte sequences. TST averages four consecutive token embeddings and predicts the corresponding next four-token bag. Applying TST during the first 30% of training provides \(4\times\) token exposure in this stage, while reverting to standard next-token prediction for the remaining 70% results in \(1.9\times\) total text exposure under the same number of training tokens (see Appendix A.2 for details).
Second, we augment byte embeddings with hash embeddings as (Pagnoni et al., 2025). The model learns representations for \(n\)-grams of bytes and combines them with byte embeddings, allowing each byte token to incorporate information from nearby multi-byte patterns (Appendix A.3). Similarly, we increase the convolution width of gated DeltaNet from \(4\) (for subword models) to \(16\) (for byte models), to build a broader local receptive field.
Unless otherwise specified, byte models apply TST and hash embeddings (denoted by Byte), while subword models don’t (denoted by Subword). We later ablate these components to separate the contribution of the training recipe from that of input granularity.

2.4 Controlling Computation with Shared Depth and Sparse Experts
The preceding results reveal a compute–quality trade-off between Byte and Subword. We therefore examine two complementary ways to shift this trade-off (Figure 2): increasing Subword computation through shared depth, and reducing Byte’s activated computation at a given parameter capacity with sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE). Using the main model suite, we evaluate four-pass Subword models and Byte MoE models under the training schedules of their dense counterparts; architecture details are provided in Appendix A.
Increasing computation through shared depth.
We follow the setting of LoopFormer (Jeddi et al., 2026) to increase Subword computation with four shared backbone passes. It closes most of the BPB gap to Byte in both the parameter and compute views, showing that repeated depth is an effective way to scale computation for subword models. The looped Subword models also achieve consistently higher QA RC than the standard dense Byte models across the evaluated scales.
Reducing activated computation with sparse experts.
Sparse MoE (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) consistently improves Byte BPB in both the parameter and compute views, showing that sparse activation is an effective way to reduce computation while retaining larger model capacity. Notably, the 1B Byte MoE achieves lower BPB than the 3B dense Subword model while reaching comparable QA RC.
Together, these results show that the byte–subword modeling gap depends strongly on how computation is allocated. Increasing computation through shared depth allows Subword models to recover much of the BPB gap, while sparse activation improves the compute efficiency of Byte models at comparable parameter capacity.
3 Emergent Local Abstractions in Byte Transformers
Tokenization provides explicit local abstractions that can organize text into useful computational units. In this section, we investigate whether similar structures can emerge implicitly in byte Transformers without an explicit tokenizer or hierarchical architecture. We study this question through two complementary experiments. First, we examine how byte models organize information during prediction using an input-copying task. Second, we evaluate whether the representations revealed by this analysis can support alternative computation patterns through a frozen layer intervention.
3.1 Copying as a probe of local information structure
To probe how text semantics are abstracted and accessed in models, we study the copying task by prompting the model with “Your task is to copy the string: \(X\). Now copy it: \(X\)”, where \(X\) is a short string composed of multiple meaningful English words. We evaluate Byte-1B by analyzing attention patterns during the copying process, focusing on how different layers access the source text when generating the copied sequence (see Appendix E.1 for details).
As shown in Figure 3, Byte-1B exhibits a clear attention aggregation pattern during copying. In particular, attention in intermediate layers becomes concentrated on a small set of source positions that are repeatedly accessed by multiple consecutive target bytes. This aggregation occurs even without any whitespace in inputs, indicating that it does not simply arise from explicit separators. 55 5 Whitespaces may provide shortcuts during the aggregation: model can learn to utilize repeatedly appearing tokens, ablation of separators is in Figure 9. The repeated reuse of these positions suggests that the byte model internally develops a mechanism for consolidating local information into representative states, providing an implicit structure over the byte sequence.
3.2 Testing local abstractions with a frozen layer intervention
The copying task reveals that multiple byte predictions can repeatedly access a small set of locally aggregated source representations. However, attention patterns alone do not establish whether these representations are sufficient for subsequent computation. We therefore introduce a frozen layer intervention that forces a contiguous range of intermediate layers to operate on a shorter sequence of locally aggregated representations, while preserving full byte-resolution states before and after the intervention. No model parameters are updated.
Concretely, following the external tokenizer applied in BLT (Pagnoni et al., 2025), we partition the text sequence into local spans using a frozen, architecturally matched 200M Byte model and compress non-image text tokens (for vision–language tasks) by approximately \(4\times\). We treat the selected intermediate layers as a temporary global stage (like the global model in BLT): each local span is mean-pooled into a single representation, processed at the compressed resolution, and then propagated back to the original byte-resolution sequence. This intervention introduces a coarse-grained computation stage into the pretrained Byte-1B model without modifying its parameters or retraining it. More details and results are in Appendix E.2.
We apply the same intervention to Byte-1B and Subword-1B across several downstream tasks, sweeping the start and end layers of the compressed global stage \(G\). As shown in Figure 4, Byte exhibits a clear recovery in performance when the global stage is placed sufficiently late in the network, with the trend becoming apparent for intervals starting around layer 17. More importantly, a stable compressible region emerges in the later layers: for \(G=[21,27]\), 7 of the model’s 28 layers (25%) operate only on the compressed local representations, while downstream performance remains close to the uncompressed baseline across tasks. In contrast, applying the same intervention to the subword model causes substantial degradation across nearly all layer intervals. This contrast suggests that the robustness to an intermediate compressed global stage is a property of the byte model rather than a generic consequence of pooling hidden states.
4 Information Allocation Along Text Sequences
Locally aggregated representations can support part of Byte’s computation on a shorter sequence (Section 3), while the model still predicts every byte separately. These additional modeling steps raise the question of where the extra computation (Section 2.2) is useful and where it could be reduced. For a given text source, deterministic, reversible tokenizations preserve total entropy (Proposition 1), so finer granularity distributes the same source information over more tokens. We therefore examine how BPB varies across these tokens, how token boundaries explain the distribution gap, and whether small drafts can handle the low-BPB continuations.

4.2 Drafting Low-BPB Continuations
Maintaining a fixed byte granularity allows more computation for high-BPB tokens, but also adds modeling steps for the many low-BPB continuations (Figure 5; Table 2). We therefore examine whether speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023) can reduce this overhead.
We use existing 200M and 50M models as drafts and same-family 1B models as targets for both Byte and Subword. We do not train specialized drafts. On the validation dataset, we compare accepted bytes per target forward pass in an oracle simulation on the given evaluation text. Appendix G specifies the protocol.
Higher byte acceptance rates compensate for shorter token spans.
At both draft sizes, Byte accepts about \(3.4\times\) as many tokens per target forward pass as Subword. Accepted bytes per forward are nearly identical with 200M drafts and slightly higher for Byte with 50M drafts (Table 3). The 200M drafts yield longer accepted text per forward in both families.
These acceptance lengths suggest that many additional byte tokens can be verified together, reducing the need for separate sequential target calls. The comparable text coverage of the two families at both draft sizes suggests that joint verification could offset much of the extra sequential overhead introduced by byte granularity.
5 Related Work
Tokenizer-free language models.
Tokenizer-free language models operate directly on fine-grained inputs such as bytes or characters, avoiding a separately constructed subword vocabulary. ByT5 (Xue et al., 2022) studies byte-to-byte pretraining, while MambaByte (Wang et al., 2024) models byte sequences with selective state-space models. Other works (Clark et al., 2022; YU et al., 2023; Pagnoni et al., 2025; Hwang et al., 2026), introduce hierarchical mechanisms that compress or aggregate fine-grained inputs for coarser-grained computation. In contrast, we study byte language modeling with Transformers with hybrid attention that preserves byte resolution throughout the network.
Compute scaling and allocation.
Model scale and computation can be varied independently through tokenization and architectural design. Compute Optimal Tokenization (Limisiewicz et al., 2026) studies how token granularity changes the relationship between raw data, sequence length, and compute. Repeated-depth models such as LoopFormer (Jeddi et al., 2026) increase computation while sharing parameters, whereas sparse MoE models such as DeepSeek-V3 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) increase parameter capacity without proportionally increasing activated computation. Building on these directions, we use shared depth to increase Subword computation and sparse experts to reduce Byte activated computation at a given parameter capacity, examining how computation shapes the byte–subword modeling trade-off.
6 Discussion and Conclusion
Our results provide a complementary perspective to recent byte-level approaches, which introduce explicit hierarchical or learned tokenization mechanisms to control the cost of long byte sequences. Using a flat Transformer, we show that this sequence-length overhead can instead provide useful computation: byte models benefit from scaling and favor substantially more training data per parameter under a fixed FLOP budget. Moreover, although we do not impose a hierarchy, byte Transformers develop segmentation-like positions that aggregate local text information, and intermediate layers can exploit these representations without loss in downstream performance. This connects flat byte Transformers to hierarchical architectures and suggests that local abstraction and global modeling can emerge implicitly from byte-level training. These learned structures also have inference-time consequences, as prediction difficulty concentrates near their boundaries and can be exploited by speculative decoding. Together, our results suggest that sequence length, learned abstraction, and computation should be viewed as coupled design dimensions, motivating future work on adaptive computation and learned computational granularity for byte language models.
Limitations
Our study is limited to vanilla Transformer backbones and the evaluated training and task scales; whether the observed scaling behavior and emergent local structures generalize to other architectures, larger models, and broader modalities remains open.

Appendix A Architecture and Objectives
A.1 Tokenizers
Our byte tokenizer encodes text directly as UTF-8 without learned merges or Unicode normalization. To support document/chat control and padding within a 256-entry vocabulary, we use 0xC0, 0xC1, and 0xF5–0xFF as special-token IDs. These 13 bytes values are forbidden in UTF-8 and never occur in valid UTF-8 text (Yergeau, 2003).
For subword models, we use HuggingFaceTB/cosmo2-tokenizer (Allal et al., 2025)88 8 https://huggingface.co/HuggingFaceTB/cosmo2-tokenizer, a pretrained byte-level BPE tokenizer with 49,152 vocabulary entries. On the pretraining dataset, it averages approximately 4.56 UTF-8 bytes per token. We use this ratio in the compute and data exposure calculations in Section 2.2 and Appendices C.
A.2 Token-Superposition Training (TST)
TST (Peng et al., 2026) lets each model input represent several tokens. We divide a token sequence \(\mathbf{T}\) into non-overlapping bags of \(K=4\) consecutive tokens, forming the sequence \(\mathbf{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{|\mathbf{T}|/K})\). Let \(T_{i,j}\) be the \(j\)th token in bag \(B_{i}\). Each bag is represented by the average of its token embeddings, including causal hash features when enabled.
The causal model and its single output head, with parameters \(\theta\), use the preceding bags \(\mathbf{B}_{<i}\) to produce one shared token distribution \(p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{B}_{<i})\) for all tokens in the next bag \(B_{i}\). The loss averages token cross-entropy over all target bags (Peng et al., 2026, Eq. (3)):
The first 30% of pretraining updates use TST; the remaining 70% use ordinary next-token training on the same parameters. During TST, each four-token bag counts as one training token. With a fixed number of training tokens per update, this schedule provides \(0.3\times 4+0.7\times 1=1.9\) times the text exposure of ordinary next-token training at the same training-token count.

G.2 Oracle simulation and acceptance metrics
To isolate draft–target agreement from the strategy used to construct draft candidates, we use an oracle simulation on the given evaluation text. It directly measures consecutive agreements, setting aside choices such as draft-tree expansion, pruning, and stopping heuristics.
Both models condition on the preceding tokens of this text at each scored token (teacher forcing). A draft token is counted as accepted when the two models select the same token, including when it differs from the reference token. The acceptance rate is the fraction of scored tokens accepted. The following step always uses the reference continuation as context. Each verification step ends at the first rejection or document boundary and counts as one simulated target forward pass, with no proposal-length cap.
The mean accepted draft length is the number of accepted draft tokens per simulated target forward pass. To compare text coverage across granularities, we also report accepted bytes per forward, summing the decoded byte lengths of the accepted draft tokens. Both averages include verification steps with no accepted tokens. Special tokens contribute no bytes; correction and bonus outputs are excluded.
Related work uses similar diagnostics of draft–target agreement. Mamou et al. (2024) let the draft continue until the first rejection, identifying the maximum accepted draft length at each iteration without choosing a fixed lookahead or a stopping heuristic. EvoESAP (Liu et al., 2026) likewise evaluates acceptance on fixed reference prefixes using teacher forcing. It averages the expected single-token acceptance computed from the overlap between draft and target next-token distributions. Our diagnostic measures consecutive agreements and their byte coverage.

Implications for speculative decoding.
The drafting experiment provides a proof of concept for computation allocation at inference. Longer byte agreement runs indicate that small drafts can match their targets across many consecutive byte tokens in reference text. These runs provide a teacher-forced diagnostic of drafting opportunities. Generated histories may differ from the reference text, so these lengths do not provide a general upper bound on accepted draft length during generation. In speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023), accepted tokens become part of the subsequent context, and each round includes correction or bonus outputs. Establishing practical speedups therefore requires decoding along generated histories with finite proposals and measuring the costs of drafting and target verification.
