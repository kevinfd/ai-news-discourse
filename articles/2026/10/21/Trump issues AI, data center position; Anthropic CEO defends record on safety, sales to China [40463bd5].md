Meta hit with $25 billion judgment as New Mexico AG puts Big Tech on notice
Meta was ordered to pay New Mexico $25 billion Wednesday after a judge imposed the penalty stemming from a jury's finding that the company misled consumers, prompting the state's attorney general to put Silicon Valley on notice.
“This judgment should be a signal and unambiguous message to anyone in Silicon Valley,” Attorney General Raúl Torrez said.
The case grew out of the Cambridge Analytica scandal involving Facebook users’ personal information. Torrez said more than 350,000 New Mexicans had their data sold or brokered without their knowledge.
Jurors found more than 43 million violations of state consumer protection laws, exposing Meta to roughly $220 billion in potential penalties before the judge set the award.
Meta Chief Legal Officer C.J. Mahoney blasted the decision and vowed to appeal. “This ruling, and the Attorney General's approach to this enforcement, makes it all but impossible to do business in the state of New Mexico,” Mahoney said.
“At its core, this lawsuit seeks to impose a tax on speech to enforce new rules for what people can say online. We are confident we will prevail on appeal,” he added.
Meta argued in court filings that it does not sell user data and that the state failed to prove consumers were actually misled, Reuters reported. FOX Business has reached out to the company for comment.
Torrez said he expects Meta to appeal. Money collected would support public school construction and other capital projects, he said.
The judgment follows nearly $1 billion in penalties and payments in a separate New Mexico case over young users’ safety on Meta’s platforms.
Torrez also warned AI companies, revealing an investigation into what he called “loss of control events” involving OpenAI and others.
“Our users and consumers deserve honesty in the marketplace,” he said.

What is PHP? The behind-the-scenes code powering much of the web
PHP is a programming language that powers websites behind the scenes, assembling pages on a server before they appear in a visitor’s browser.
It runs on nearly 70% of websites whose programming language can be identified, according to web technology tracker W3Techs.
PHP also powers WordPress, the platform used by about 40% of all websites, including blogs, business pages, government portals, online shops and news sites.
Because PHP works behind the scenes, most website visitors never interact with it directly. The software is free and open source, maintained by a community of developers.

FBI, NSA urge organizations to patch critical PHP flaws or take systems offline
The FBI and NSA are warning organizations to urgently address two critical vulnerabilities in PHP that could let attackers remotely run code and potentially compromise sensitive data and networks.
The FBI, in an X post, urged organizations to update affected PHP installations immediately, following guidance from PHP’s developers. If an update cannot be made right away, the FBI recommends taking the affected system offline until it is fully protected.
The flaws affect PHP (Hypertext Preprocessor), programming language used to build and run websites. PHP developers have released a fix, while cybersecurity agencies have urged site operators to update or temporarily take vulnerable websites offline.
A web server can use PHP to assemble a page’s content and send it to a visitor’s browser. It powers widely used platforms such as WordPress, along with many other websites and online services.
The NSA issued a similar warning, saying the vulnerabilities could provide a path to a server’s file system or database. It advised organizations using PHP to be prepared to install an update and said it would provide more specific guidance.
"Effective Immediately, all organizations using PHP for their systems are advised to be prepared for an update addressing two critical vulnerabilities. PHP is widely used for web applications. These vulnerabilities often provide a direct path to the server's file system or database. If an immediate update is not feasible, it is recommend that organizations take the system offline until it can be fully mitigated. NSA will follow up shortly with more specific guidance."
The FBI also urged organizations to inventory where PHP is running, prioritizing internet-facing systems and products that include PHP, and to monitor web servers for suspicious activity. Signs of possible exploitation could include PHP processes unexpectedly launching command shells or downloading files.
Organizations that find signs of a compromise should isolate affected systems, preserve web server and PHP logs, and report the incident to the FBI or the Internet Crime Complaint Center, the agency said.

The effort responds to the battlefield impact of low-cost drones and seeks to help U.S. forces adapt faster, the letter said.
“Urgency does not excuse imprecision,” West and Strasiser wrote, pledging to evaluate which technologies should scale and cut those that do not meet operational needs.

He said Anthropic remains committed to proposing options for regulating the pace of AI research by Jan. 15, 2027, or explaining why it will not do so.
