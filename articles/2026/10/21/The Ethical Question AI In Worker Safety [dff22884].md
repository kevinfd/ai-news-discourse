# The Ethical Question: AI In Worker Safety

Forbes · Julia Penfield, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-21

Julia Penfield, Ph.D., Chief AI Officer at VelocityEHS, develops AI systems that identify workplace risks before serious incidents occur.
For over two decades, safety professionals have worked to reduce the most serious workplace incidents. We’ve made progress on lower-severity injuries, but the number that matters most has barely moved: In the United States, a worker dies from workplace conditions roughly every two hours. That rate has been essentially flat for 15 years. Despite collective effort, this is still unacceptable.
The plateau isn’t a mystery. Preventing serious injuries is fundamentally limited by time and human resources. Before a job is performed, someone must understand it, identify its hazards and plan controls to reduce or eliminate risk. If a task is repetitive, they must judge whether the workstation will wear the worker down through recurrent strain. When something goes wrong, someone must find the root causes and corrective actions to prevent recurrence. In a plant with thousands of chemicals or hundreds of contractors, someone must manage it all.
In a perfect world, we’d have unlimited experts, time and budget to do these things well. But we don’t.
New EHS 360 Benchmark Report data found that most EHS professionals say limited budgets and staffing constraints undermine their ability to do their jobs effectively. So, the work goes undone, and workers get hurt.
I’ve seen what this costs up close. Before I worked in software, I spent two years at a hydroelectric power plant in British Columbia, 600 feet underground, surrounded by heavy machinery and stored energy that can change a life in an instant. It’s where I learned what safety actually means to the people in steel-toed boots, and it became part of who I am.
It taught me something that still shapes how I build. You cannot close this gap by asking people to work harder. You need to provide them with better tools.
AI Can Make Expertise Scalable
The opportunity isn’t to make processes slightly faster. It’s to take a scarce expert’s judgment and put it everywhere it’s needed.
Imagine giving every electrician, mechanic and operator a tool that delivers professional safety guidance before they start a job. Not a generic checklist, but specific hazards and controls for the work in front of them. A worker might be highly experienced yet unfamiliar with a particular machine or newly moved to a line where their instincts don’t fully apply. Or they’re mentally tired and miss a hazard and the controls that need to be in place for it before starting the job. Those gaps are where people get hurt, and where AI can help. It doesn’t replace the safety professional; it extends their reach.
The same logic applies to the safety professional’s desk: AI can automate manual work, surface root causes and flag corrective actions, freeing safety teams to focus on judgment where it matters most.
This is where the human and business cases converge. Consider musculoskeletal disorders, among the most expensive categories of workplace injury in the U.S. They accumulate silently: A worker reaches and lifts the same way over time until a shoulder or wrist gives out, and the recovery is long and costly for everyone involved. Preventing these disorders requires accurate ergonomics assessments, yet only a few hundred certified ergonomists in the U.S. can cover the jobs that need them.
AI can extend that reach by assessing a task from a short video and identifying the redesign that could prevent injury. Fewer injuries, lower costs and workers who finish their careers whole.
Safety Demands A Higher Bar For AI
None of this works if we treat safety like every other AI application. In most software, an occasional wrong answer is a nuisance; you fix the summary and move on. In safety, a model that identifies hazards correctly 99% of the time and misses 1% has just let a worker walk into something with irreversible consequences. That is a completely different standard, and it’s why the rush toward general-purpose, off-the-shelf models in safety-critical settings concerns me.
A model may be capable without being appropriate for safety. Getting this right requires purpose-built models trained on appropriate safety data, EHS professionals involved throughout development, clearly defined performance standards, rigorous testing and benchmarking, and an understanding of the technology’s limitations.
That rigor may mean moving more slowly during development. I’m comfortable with that trade-off. Some places call for moving fast and learning from mistakes. Worker safety isn’t one of them.
But slower development does not mean slower safety work. Once validated, the right model can help EHS professionals perform high-quality work faster and at a scale difficult to achieve manually. The investment happens up front, so speed and quality don’t have to compete once the technology is in use.
Where Responsibility Still Lies
Human judgment remains essential. AI should make a recommendation; a qualified professional should be able to review it, question it, modify it or reject it before acting on it.
And when something goes wrong, “the AI made the decision” cannot be where responsibility ends. The people building these systems need to understand and communicate their limitations, and the people using them need to know when human expertise must take over. AI can expand a team’s bandwidth and support better decisions, but judgment must remain human.
Getting this right pays off in the business, too. Built correctly, AI can break the traditional trade-off between speed and quality: The upfront investment in the right models buys both. That alone is worth pursuing.
But that isn’t why I do this. We have spent 20 years accepting a number we never should have, and a real solution is finally within reach. To me, the choice isn’t even close.
People debate whether it’s ethical to hand something as high-stakes as worker safety to AI. I’ve come to believe the opposite: When a responsible, proven tool exists and we choose not to use it, that is the ethical failure. Either we accept that a worker will keep dying every two hours, or we build the alternative. I know which one I’m choosing.
