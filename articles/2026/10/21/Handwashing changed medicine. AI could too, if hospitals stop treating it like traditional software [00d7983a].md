# Handwashing changed medicine. AI could too, if hospitals stop treating it like traditional software

Fast Company · Stephen Straus · 2026-10-21

In 1847, Ignaz Semmelweis asked the doctors in the Vienna maternity clinic where he worked to wash their hands in a chlorine solution before examining patients. Deaths on the ward fell from about 18% to about 2% within two months. In healthcare, small changes in practice can have disproportionately large effects, because the healthcare system affects everyone and the stakes are literally life and death. We think AI has the potential to revolutionize healthcare, contributing to a dizzying array of changes. But institutions are failing to adjust to meet the moment, despite widespread interest in change from patients and providers.
Americans are racing ahead with using AI for medical advice. Roughly a third of Americans have used AI for health information, and one in four did so in the past month, through chatbots like ChatGPT. Who uses these tools the most? Younger people, and people who might not have access to a doctor, such as those who are uninsured or who can’t afford a visit. This is a form of unmet demand—clearly people want more access to healthcare. And while it’s laudable that people are finding creative ways to meet their needs, doing so cuts both ways, because when a chatbot is wrong, the consequences tend to fall on the people least able to absorb them.
Doctors, too, are adopting AI, and mostly on their own. OpenEvidence, a free clinical reference that doctors can use without institutional support, skyrocketed from about 3 million physician consultations a month to about 18 million a month a year later, in December 2025. Like patients, doctors are meeting the demand themselves. While individual experimentation is normal and to be expected, we will eventually need to see effective, system-wide adoption of AI in healthcare if we are to create significant benefits for society.
Lest you think we are unfairly singling out healthcare, we should point out that most organizations are struggling with AI: For instance, across industries, 88% of AI pilots never make it to production, and a recent working paper from the National Bureau of Economic Research found that only about one in 10 companies report any productivity gains from AI over the last three years.
While this gap is huge and at odds with the rush of spending and media attention surrounding AI, one of the main impediments to fixing it is something leaders can address today. We say this with some confidence. Over the past nine years our firm has worked on more than 230 AI projects across 30 industries, and 96% of the models we’ve been hired to take to production have shipped, including in healthcare. We think we know what’s going wrong, and what to do about it.
Don’t make the mistake of treating AI like it’s traditional software
So, what’s going on? While healthcare is complex and high-stakes—and its data is fragmented across institutions that are reluctant, and often legally unable, to share it with one another—we think one of the basic challenges holding back progress is that leaders have the wrong mental model for AI. They are treating it like traditional software, when it is not. Having the wrong mental model can be a critical blocker to progress; after all, Semmelweis’s colleagues rejected handwashing for decades, even though there was ample evidence to make a change, because the evidence didn’t fit how they understood disease.
How does AI differ from traditional software? To name a few ways: Traditional software is written, whereas AI is trained, and in some cases even “grown.” Software is deterministic: if this, then that; while AI is by and large probabilistic, and dependent on its data. Software is built linearly, but AI is built in loops, and most of the work happens before anything resembling a product exists. Managing AI like software means specifying the answer before you’ve checked whether the data can actually produce such an answer.
