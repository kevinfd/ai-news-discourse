Honestly, didn't you think this 'prep work' time was a waste? 
Grok Bot has come to almost eliminate that waste.

The general reaction in the world is focused on 'the limit has been raised, that's amazing.' 
But I see the biggest change here not as convenience, but as the shift in where the real work happens. 
If the effort of splitting files disappears, the difference will be whether you have decided in advance 'what to ask when passing the whole thing' and 'what not to pass.' 
I'm going to talk about this today.
Now that you can pass everything to Grok Bot, those who decide how to ask and what not to pass are the strongest
First, let's grasp what happened. 
On October 20, 2026, Elon Musk posted an announcement about Grok Bot upgrades. 
It has about 4,670 likes and about 1.49 million views. 
The post with the detailed video has also grown to about 7,870 likes and about 1.33 million views.
The content consists of figures that multiple users are citing and sharing on their screens as the xAI Grok Bot update history (0.71.2).
- Normal files up to 250MB
- Videos up to 1GB
- Up to 20 attachments per message (previously 6)
I will write this without sugarcoating it. 
I could not automatically read the official update history page itself. 
These figures are based on Elon's post and posts citing the update history. 
Detailed restrictions for each plan or device are not written in the cited range. 
So, please check the limits on your own screen before using it.
With that said, I have one thing to say. 
The moment the limit was raised, the value of the work of 'preparing files into a form the AI can accept' became almost zero. 
The remaining value is what you ask after passing it, and what you don't pass in the first place. 
For those who decide these two things in advance, document work will truly become lighter.
Even in Japanese posts, there are introductions saying, 'Normal files are up to 250MB, videos up to 1GB. The range of what you can do has expanded.' 
Looking at the reactions, many people are focusing on 'what can now be sent.' 
But what I'm concerned about is what comes after that. 
When the things you can send increase, the things you need to think about before sending also increase. 
If you skip this and rush to 'just send everything,' you will end up with accidents and mediocre answers behind the convenience.
Now that the effort of cutting has disappeared, the difference lies in whether you have decided in advance 'what to ask' and 'what not to pass.'
The waste in document work was the prep work of splitting and compressing
Try to recall your document work up until now. 
The PDF you want the AI to read is too heavy, so you split it page by page. 
The meeting recording is too large, so you cut it out with video editing software. 
You don't have enough attachments, so you send them in several batches.
These are all 'preparations to pass to the AI.' 
It is not the 'reading, summarizing, and judging' you really want to do. 
Moreover, when you pass them separately, the AI cannot see the whole thing at once, so it misses the connection between the beginning and the end. 
Even if the numbers in Part 3 contradict Part 1, you wouldn't notice if you passed them separately.
In third-party explanatory articles, the file limit for the previous Grok app was reported to be around 150MB. 
From there to 250MB. Videos to 1GB. Attachments from 6 to 20. 
Users are saying, 'I can send heavy PDFs, audio, and raw videos without compressing them,' and 'I can send 20 files at once, making work faster.' 
In the last 72 hours, I haven't seen any posts of dissatisfaction within my reach.
Moreover, even if this prep work is only a few minutes at a time, it is a task you repeat every week. 
Every time a document arrives, you cut it, shrink it, and resend it. 
Because the effort for each time is small, it remained without anyone saying, 'Let's stop.' 
The smaller, more repetitive, and unquestioned a task is, the greater the impact when it disappears.
I see this as one entire task on the ToDo side having disappeared. 
In Uniko terms, work is divided into 'the 30% that decides what to do (the plan side)' and 'the 70% that executes how to do it (the ToDo side).' 
Splitting and compressing are entirely on the ToDo side. 
If that part disappears, human time must be redirected to the plan side.
Preparation was not the work itself. If it has disappeared, redirect that time to designing 'what to ask.'
Precisely because you can pass everything, decide first what not to pass.
This is seriously important. 
An increased limit is not all good news. 
Being able to pass everything also means you can accidentally pass everything.
Until now, thanks to the effort of splitting, we naturally chose 'where to pass.' 
It's heavy, so I'll cut it. When cutting, I look at the contents. When I look, I realize, 'I shouldn't release this part.' 
The tedious preparation actually acted as a brake.
That brake is disappearing. 
Therefore, you need to draw a line in advance. 
For me, I would at least place these three on the 'do not pass' side.
- Customer lists or contact lists
- Original copies of contracts (those containing the other party's name, amounts, and conditions)
- Recordings of confidential meetings
Of course, the correct answer changes depending on the company and the contract. 
The important thing is to 'decide in advance' rather than 'hesitate on the spot.' 
If you judge while hesitating, the number of decisions increases, draining your remaining lives (the number of decisions a human can make in a day). 
If you decide once and write it down on paper, you won't have to hesitate afterward.
When drawing the line, I only use three questions.
- Does the file contain individual names or contact information?
- Is it decided by contract or agreement that it must not be taken outside the company?
- If it were to get out, would even one person be troubled?
If even one applies, I place it on the 'do not pass' side. 
When you absolutely must use it, create a separate version with names and numbers redacted before passing it. 
This is where you should not spare the effort. 
Since the preparation has disappeared, use your time only for the preparation of protecting information. 
I believe this is the perfect balance for the era where you can pass everything.
There is one more thing to note. 
The limit for xAI's Files API (the mechanism for developers to pass files via programs) is separate from the limit for this app. 
If you are using it via the API, please check that limit separately.
Preparation was also a brake. If it has disappeared, draw the line of what not to pass yourself in advance.

Create a template for what to ask after passing it
Once you can draw the line on what not to share, the next step is "how to ask." 
Even if you can hand over everything at once, just saying "summarize this" will only get you a mediocre answer. 
As the amount of material increases, the difference in how you ask becomes the direct difference in the results.
For me, I decide how to ask for each type of material I use frequently. 
For example, it looks like this.
- One meeting recording → Have it extract only "what was decided" and "who will do what by when"
- 20 proposal documents → Have it look for "places where numbers or statements contradict each other across documents"
- Internal manual PDF → Have it create "questions and answers that new hires are likely to ask"
The point is to narrow your objective down to one thing. 
If you get greedy and ask to "summarize everything, point out problems, and provide improvement plans," everything will end up shallow. 
If you can send 20 items at once, narrow your request down to one thing. 
This balance is the most effective.
In particular, being able to hand over 20 items at once is powerful for "cross-referencing" tasks. 
Previously, because we had to split them into groups of six, humans had to be the ones to find contradictions between document A and document M. 
Now, you can line them all up and ask at once. 
This is incredible. 
It means the most grueling part of document checking has shifted into an area where AI excels.
There is one more phrase I want you to always include in how you ask. 
It is the phrase: "Please also include which document and which page (or around what minute) that answer is written on." 
AI sometimes writes things that sound plausible but aren't in the documents. 
If you have it provide the source location, humans only need to open that specific part to verify it. 
Since you no longer need to re-read everything, verification time is also drastically reduced.
Once you find a way of asking that works, save it as a memo. 
The next time you get the same type of material, just paste that memo as is. 
How you ask is an asset you can reuse. 
Since you can reuse what you thought of once dozens of times, it is also the most effective way to save your "remaining lives" (the number of times you have to make human judgments).
If you increase the material, decrease the questions. The more you narrow down what you ask to one thing, the more effective handing over everything becomes.

Finally, I will talk about something subtle but important. 
To reiterate, the numbers this time are based on Elon's posts and the update history citations. 
There is a strong possibility that the limits differ depending on the plan, device, and region.
Therefore, do not try it with heavy, real-world documents right away. 
My recommendation is to try it once with "documents that are okay to release" that are about the same weight as your real work. 
For example, publicly available company documents or practice videos with no sensitive content. 
Can it be sent? Does it read to the end? How is the quality of the answer? 
Verify it on your own screen once before incorporating it into your work flow. 
This is the safest and, in the end, the fastest way.
I will write down one more habit of mine. 
I try not to use files that are right at the limit for my regular work. 
When at the limit, it can stop midway if the connection is unstable, or it might take a long time to load. 
I usually use sizes that have some leeway, and only use files at the limit in situations where "it wouldn't be a problem if it stopped." 
Just doing this significantly reduces the risk of your work flow coming to a halt.
Additionally, it is cited that the same update included improvements to PC notifications, pre-input forms, and Slack integration. 
Grok Bot has become faster and can accept more volume in the last few days. 
Tools change almost every week. 
That is precisely why people who have their own "template" for "what to hand over, what to ask, and what not to hand over" are stronger than those who just memorize the tool's numbers. 
Templates can be reused even when tools change.
Numbers change. What doesn't change is your own template for handing over, asking, and not handing over.
Use the time you saved to design what to ask and what to protect.
To summarize what we have discussed so far, it comes down to this.
The preparation work of splitting and compressing files has disappeared.
Instead, three things remain: 'what to ask,' 'what not to hand over,' and 'how to verify the answer.'
All three of these are the job of the planner.
What I have been saying all along is that in the AI era, the value remains with the side that decides 'what to do.'
This update is simply a case of that happening in the world of files as well.
Every time manual labor disappears, what remains for humans is the work of making decisions.
This is no longer a prediction; it is a certain future that is happening right in front of us almost every week.
So, there is no need to be afraid.
If anything, it is an opportunity.
A note on how to ask, and a list of what not to hand over.
Anyone who has these two things can immediately master new limits, no matter how quickly the tools change.
Try spending one hour today writing those two things down.
Once the preparation work is gone, all that remains is the work of making decisions. For those who have prepared for this, document work will become lighter.
Things to do today
- Write down three things you will 'never hand over to AI' in your work and post them where you can see them
- For each type of material you handle frequently (meeting recordings, proposal documents, manuals, etc.), decide on one thing to ask and make a note of it
- Use a heavy file that is fine to share to verify your own screen's limit once
