# The Chestnut Grove Caught in the Middle of A Years-Long Data Center Fight

404 Media · Samantha Cole · 2026-10-21

Near the bank of the Ohio River, a historic American chestnut grove in Mason County, West Virginia has become the epicenter of a fight against a planned 2,250 acre data center campus. Plans for the UK-based startup Nscale’s Monarch Compute Campus, with clients including Anthropic, Nvidia and Microsoft, encompass the former Clements State Nursery and the American chestnut trees still growing there.
For thousands of years, the American chestnut tree was a staple of life. Its natural range spanned hundreds of millions of acres from Maine to Mississippi, centuries before those names existed. As many as four billion trees provided a hearty nut crop for humans and provided miraculously rot-resistant lumber for railroad ties and housing. It sustained black bears as they prepared to hibernate, fed white-tailed deer and turkey, and shed nutrient-dense leaves. The largest American chestnut trees once grew 100 feet tall and 13 feet wide and represented one-fourth of all hardwoods; a squirrel could scamper along their branches from New England to Georgia without touching the ground, as an old saying goes.
In 1904, a forester at the Bronx zoo noticed something alarming among their American chestnuts: The trees were dying en masse, struck by a mysterious blight. Within 40 years, that blight — thought to be brought to America on Asian chestnut trees — wiped out the tree’s population to near-extinction. Since then, conservationists have worked to restore American Chestnut trees and breed blight-resistant specimens. Typically, threats to groves include pests, climate change, and the fungus that destroyed their ancestors. Today, however, a new threat has entered the picture: data centers.
Across the country, communities are pushing back against massive hyperscale data center projects, protesting against the noise pollution, environmental impacts, and blow to property values near these computer warehouses. In many places, elected officials are forging ahead to allow these multi-billion dollar deals in direct defiance of their constituents' wishes. Earlier this year, an Oklahoma farmer was arrested at a town hall meeting for speaking over his time against a 255-acre data center. Data centers are easy to build but much harder and slower to power, and their workarounds are egregious; For example, Elon Musk’s xAI Colossus 2 data center in Tennessee skips the local grid and runs its own natural gas turbines, worsening pollution and disproportionately impacting Black neighborhoods. But this tiny plot of American chestnut trees, just a little over an acre of land, has become a site of resistance in West Virginia.
I first heard about the Clements chestnut grove after a 404 Media reader emailed me about it earlier this month, saying that the trees faced “potential destruction after November 1, 2026.” Social media posts called the threat “urgent,” and claimed bulldozers are lined up, with headlines declaring the grove is “about to be destroyed.” In the last few weeks, the plight of a grove of fragile chestnut trees trembling in the shadow of a data center has gone viral. Goldberry Grove, a local regenerative chestnut orchard, posted “They’re bulldozing the American chestnuts after November 1st,” sparking more outrage.
In 2023, Governor Jim Justice announced a multi-billion dollar deal with Fidelis New Energy for a lifecycle carbon neutral hydrogen production facility, including the “Mountaineer GigaSystem” and the Monarch Compute Campus. Nscale acquired the American Intelligence & Power Corporation, sponsored by Fidelis New Energy and including the Monarch campus, in March.
“The citizens of Mason County are elated with the announcement of Fidelis New Energy selecting Mason County, West Virginia, for its multibillion-dollar state of the art carbon neutral, clean hydrogen complex,” John Musgrave, the Executive Director of the Mason County Development Authority said in Justice’s 2023 press release about the new data center plans.
With construction of the campus underway today, citizens do not seem elated. In September, heavy storms reportedly caused neighborhoods nearby to flood, as runoff from the construction site sent water and debris into adjacent properties as a section of the site’s fencing gave out. The Department of Environmental Protections (DEP) ordered Nscale “to immediately repair and strengthen the stormwater controls that failed, install additional erosion and sediment controls where needed, and take whatever actions are necessary to prevent similar releases from occurring in the future.”
Nscale told local news outlet WOWK 13 that it would pay for the damage. Separately, the company is trying to buy out homeowners, but the people living there are resistant. “What they’re saying is one thing, but they’ve got to prove it,” one homeowner told WOWK 13. “I’m staying. Fight for every one of these people.”
The fate of the trees has been a conversation among conservationists in the area for years. The American Chestnut Foundation (TACF) raised the issue in its May 2024 monthly newsletter: “Several communications have been made with [West Virginia Secretary of Commerce James Bailey] and he is aware of the chestnut trees at Clements. He assured the WV chapter that the trees will remain and be taken care of by Fidelis.”
The newsletter bulletin continued: “Mr. Bailey stated, ‘We value the chestnuts and will ensure that they are cared for. The state maintains ownership and control of the property, but if that ever changes, then the trees will be taken care of.’” The TACF noted that the future of the grove was “unclear.” Three years later, the picture is sharpening, as chestnut experts and the data center developers clash.
Sixty-two years ago, the first seedlings shipped out from West Virginia’s newly-designated Clements State Tree Nursery. The nursery was once owned by Mark Twain’s grandparents. The West Virginia Division of Natural Resources bought it in 1961 and the Division of Forestry operated it for decades onward. The nursery sold seedlings of several varieties of trees and kept 15 to 20 chestnut trees that showed promising traits of blight resistance. In 2021, the Charleston Gazette-Mail reported that the DNR quietly ceased operating the nursery, citing funding issues. “It was never set up to make money, but it was also never set up to lose the kind of money it’s been losing in the past 10 years,” then-Division of Forestry Director Tom Cover told the Gazette-Mail at the time.
Today, all of the American chestnut trees remaining at the former nursery site are infected with the blight fungus, Mark Double, a past president of the West Virginia Chapter of TACF told me. Many have died over the years, he said, but most of the trees still have sufficient resistance to have survived for 35 years, in spite of multiple infections. “The WV chapter of The American Chestnut Foundation has been harvesting nuts from this orchard for more than a decade,” Double told me over email.
Nscale’s Monarch campus is one of two data center projects planned for the area. Project Tamarack, which is owned by Starwood Digital Ventures, is planned for 10 miles south of the Monarch campus. West Virginia Governor Patrick Morrisey announced the project early last month. Morrisey promises 1,000 temporary jobs but just 300 permanent positions as a result of the project. Research shows that data centers rarely live up to promises about bolstering local economies. “Data centers do not bring high-paying tech jobs to local communities because they operate as infrastructure projects rather than traditional jobcreating businesses,” University of Michigan researchers wrote in a 2025 brief. “Although the construction of data centers can create many jobs, those are short lived.”
Residents were stunned to hear that another data center was coming to their community, telling local news outlet WSAZ that they felt blindsided. Despite not being located on the former nursery’s site, the fate of the American chestnuts have forced Starwood to address it, too.
I emailed Starwood on Oct. 8 asking if its campus threatened the trees. “I checked, and that grove isn’t on Starwood’s land,” a spokesperson told me. Sometime after Sept. 24, according to archives of the site, Project Tamarack added a line to its FAQ section about the grove: “The former Clements State Tree Nursery is a separate property and is not part of the Project Tamarack development site. Project Tamarack has no ownership, control, or management responsibility for the property. Questions regarding the chestnut grove, the former nursery, or the property’s management should be directed to the West Virginia Division of Forestry, which operated the facility prior to its closure in 2021.”
Nscale also claims that the trees aren’t on land it owns, either — yet. That section of land is technically still under a “plan to purchase agreement.”
“We understand the deep community connection to the former Clements State Tree Nursery site, and we look forward to working closely with the Morrisey Administration—including the Department of Agriculture and Division of Forestry—and The American Chestnut Foundation regarding the American Chestnut trees while responsibly developing the Monarch Compute Campus,” a spokesperson for Nscale told 404 Media. They continued:
“We want to be clear — there was never a plan to bulldoze the nursery or cut down the trees. Nscale does not currently own the former Clements State Tree Nursery but we are under a plan for purchase agreement. Once we own the land, we are focused on how to best care for the trees which, according to a recent assessment by an arborist, suffer from blight and adverse health conditions. We have always planned to incorporate the American Chestnut trees into the Monarch Compute Campus. As construction progresses, we will work closely with professional arborists and experts to determine how best to preserve and integrate them, covering all costs.”
Nscale Senior Vice President Pete Hollis told West Virginia Public Broadcasting: “There’s never been a plan to and there will never be a plan to bulldoze or cut down the trees.”
On October 10, Morrisey announced that the trees would eventually be moved, framing this as a way to appease everyone. “Under the preservation plan, the existing trees will be relocated to a more suitable environment within the property, potentially as part of an arboretum or integrated landscaping. That work is scheduled for late 2027, during the trees' dormant season, under professional arborist supervision,” the governor’s office wrote in a press release.
Amy Metheny, a forest pathologist for the West Virginia American Chestnut Foundation, told WCHS-TV that relocating the trees would inevitably kill them. “It’s my professional opinion that if the trees were moved they would die within two to five years,” she said.
I asked Morrisey’s office if they’re aware that experts say the trees will die if moved. A spokesperson for Morrisey’s office replied:
“We are aware of the concerns about transplanting and have discussed them directly with a member of the West Virginia chapter of The American Chestnut Foundation. The West Virginia Department of Agriculture’s assessment found that the trees are expected to continue dying under current site conditions. It also found that relocating chestnut clumps with proper care may improve their health, although some losses are expected.
“Mature chestnut trees from the same original source as the Clements planting are growing in and producing nuts in Tyler County. Approximately 500 nuts from those trees are expected to be used for additional planting to support continued restoration efforts.
“The company has said relocation remains its plan and that it will cover the cost. The property is subject to a legally binding purchase option signed in 2023 by the previous administration. We are working with the company and conservation experts to help secure the best possible preservation outcome. Specific questions about those relocation plans should be directed to the company.”
Much of the information floating around about the future of the grove — including the claims of looming bulldozers, the timeline for the actual threat to the trees, the “not it” statements between both data center projects, and the governor’s promises — has been a game of rumors and conflicting information. A representative for Goldberry Grove declined to provide an on-the-record source for their claims of a November 1 deadline, or the bulldozer claims.
But the attention turned to an otherwise largely overlooked and tragically rare tree species has stirred up the kind of public action that data center developers around the country have had to contend with when they want to throw miles of humming warehouses up in communities. On October 20, the West Virginia DEP hosted an in-person public comment session about Monarch’s air quality analysis and permitting, with a virtual meeting to come on October 29.
The campus’s environmental impact could extend beyond the handful of trees at Clements, according to documents filed with the DEP. The Charleston Gazette-Mail reported, based on those documents: “Construction activities would result in permanent impacts to over 8,400 linear feet of streams and over 1.7 acres of wetlands on one permit and over 5,900 linear feet of streams and more than 26 acres of wetlands on the other, according to the applications.”
In addition to being overwhelmingly unpopular with the communities where they’re built, and despite the trillions of dollars dumped into their development, data center projects and the industry at large around the world are starting to flounder. Australia’s NextDC AI data center operator’s shares dropped 40 percent on Wednesday after the company cited power delays and local pushback. Last week, Microsoft pulled out of its deal with Nscale’s Essex-based campus, following reports that the project might not be able to receive a power supply until 2030. Local community opposition to data centers has spurred legislation that’s slowed or halted these projects around the country, with some, including Microsoft in Wisconsin, canceling projects outright following petitions and protests.
