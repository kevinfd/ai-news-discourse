# Beyond The Hybrid Cloud: An AI-Based Approach For Smart Finserv Institutions

Forbes · Charles Crouchman, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-21

Charles Crouchman is the Chief Product Officer of Redwood Software, a leading orchestration platform for the autonomous enterprise.
Within financial services (finserv), legacy automation remains a vexing issue. It could be worse; the IRS has run its “Studebaker System” mainframe with patches, prop-ups and bolt-ons since 1962, when John F. Kennedy occupied the White House.
But the situation isn’t much better for banks. An astonishing six in 10 use code written before 2000. Legacy banking core systems often rely on back-end tech up to 40 years old, mainframes that run outdated programming languages, the Kansas City Federal Reserve notes.
Banks must commit to a smarter, safer and more efficient way to do things, and the Kansas City Fed argues as much: “Next-generation systems have many advantages over legacy systems [...] Their cloud-native infrastructure typically has lower operating and maintenance costs.” Hybrid cloud systems are one option for helping financial services organizations move toward an age of autonomous enterprise.
There’s an important caveat, though. Even hybrid systems must move forward.
Cloud Solutions, Remaining Problems
Many financial institutions opted for a “rip-and-replace” strategy to push their outdated systems forward. But it proved expensive, and as tech progress gathered steam and speed, many of the replacements themselves were on a path to obsolescence. As recently as 2017 and with semi-annual updates, these systems were “improved but still faced with integration challenges with newer technologies,” according to the fintech consultancy CCG Catalyst.
By the mid-2010s, hybrid cloud architectures were becoming a practical alternative for financial institutions looking to modernize without abandoning existing infrastructure. But some software providers promised more than they could deliver—and none could solve the issue of fragmented automation.
“Islands of automation” made integrating new services difficult. The crazy quilt of legacy schedulers and scripts with open-source and hybrid cloud services increased operational risk, slowed modernization and even presented talent resourcing challenges.
CTOs, CIOs and data engineering directors soon recognized the need to fully leverage cloud-native capabilities. Enterprises shifted from “lift and shift” to a refactoring approach. This makes it possible to reduce total cost of ownership (TCO), improve scalability and accelerate service innovation. It also introduces significant orchestration complexity, which becomes visible only when something breaks.
Commonly, we see a transaction or data workflow that begins on a legacy scheduler, depends on a file transfer or database process, triggers an application in the cloud and then feeds another downstream service. While each component can appear healthy in conventional monitoring, a missed handoff or timing change between them can cause the end-to-end process to fail.
Running legacy and modern workloads in parallel creates a “dependency fog” that obscures risk and muddies execution. It becomes apparent when a routine change causes an unexpected failure somewhere else in the workflow.
Organizations can clear that fog by mapping critical workflows end to end: identifying where data originates, every system it touches, what triggers the next step and which downstream processes depend on its successful completion. Pay particular attention to manual handoffs, undocumented scripts and processes that cross legacy, cloud and third-party environments.
Where Cloud-First Meets AI Power
Moving workloads to the cloud isn’t the same thing as modernizing operations. Technology leaders should keep three principles in mind.
First, modernize around business services rather than individual applications; a process is only as resilient as the dependencies connecting it.
Second, preserve visibility across the entire environment, legacy and cloud, during migration.
Third, design for change. Modernization, applications, data sources, regulations and business requirements will continue to evolve.
A unified, cloud-first application and data orchestration approach presents many advantages to financial enterprises that want to model and visualize dependencies; manage cutovers from old systems to new; and ensure reliable execution across environments.
What organizations need is visibility beyond infrastructure into relationships—which applications, data sources and preceding events drive which processes; where workloads execute; and what downstream consequences could follow from a delay or failure. They must also coordinate those processes across environments and apply consistent controls even as individual components change.
Complexity itself isn’t necessarily the problem. Unmanaged complexity is.
Before choosing an orchestration approach, financial institutions should assess their current environment against five questions:
1. Visibility: Can teams see the business workflow across legacy, cloud and third-party systems?
2. Dependencies: Can they identify upstream and downstream dependencies before making a change?
3. Control: Can workloads be coordinated from a common control point without forcing every application onto the same platform?
4. Recovery: When something fails, can teams quickly determine the business impact, isolate the cause and restart safely?
5. Governance: Can the institution demonstrate how critical processes executed, who or what changed them and whether required controls were followed?
Gaps in any of these areas indicate that an institution may have modernized its infrastructure faster than its operational model.
AI-powered orchestration shouldn’t be treated as the automatic next step. It makes the most sense where workflow complexity exceeds what teams can reasonably monitor and manage manually: large numbers of interconnected processes, frequent changes, hybrid or multi-cloud environments, demanding service levels and substantial regulatory exposure.
Even then, organizations need foundations in place. AI is only as useful as the operational data and context available to it. Poorly documented dependencies, inconsistent processes and weak governance don’t disappear when AI is introduced; automation can amplify them. Institutions should establish reliable process data, clear operating policies and defined escalation paths before increasing autonomy.
Human oversight remains particularly important where orchestration intersects with regulatory obligations, security controls, financial transactions or decisions that could impact customers. AI can identify anomalies, recommend actions and increasingly automate routine remediation, but organizations must first determine where human approval is required and maintain an auditable record of what the system did and why. In financial services, autonomy and accountability advance together.
Taking The Next Step
For banks today, the modernization imperative is clear, but the decision is not simply hybrid cloud or AI. It is whether finserv organizations can manage their environments with sufficient visibility, control and accountability.
The goal is not autonomy for its own sake, but a more resilient operating environment that enables modernization without losing control of mission-critical processes.
