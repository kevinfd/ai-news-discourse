# ​From Dashboard Builders To Agent Managers: The New Frontier For Data Teams

Forbes · Francois Lopitaux, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-22

Francois Lopitaux, SVP Product Management at ThoughtSpot, leads strategy & execution for AI analytics products for intelligent experiences.
The rise of autonomous AI agents has sparked a quiet panic in corporate data departments: if anyone can prompt an AI analyst to generate a report, what happens to the data analyst?
The truth is, the agentic era isn’t an existential threat for data teams. It has the potential to be a massive promotion. 
In this new frontier for data teams, the role of analyst will evolve to enable agents and serve as guardians of the semantic layer. They will transition from dashboard builders to agentic architects, and finally have the time to perform deep, complex analysis—which isn’t just a feel-good side benefit, but absolutely critical to the pace of continued innovation. At the end of the day, data analysts are the only ones qualified to do that work. 
To successfully navigate this transition, organizations must shift their focus from output creation to framework governance. In this article, we’ll explore the ways in which leaders can empower their data teams to build and govern the semantic layers that ground AI agents, transforming analysts from reactive report builders into strategic architects of the autonomous enterprise.
It’s All Semantics 
Let’s start by setting some context. What is the semantic layer, why does it matter and why do analysts play such a critical role in its upkeep? 
The semantic or context layer serves as a translation between available data and what it means to the business. Analysts are responsible for the semantic layer, because only they have the context to bring the data and business worlds together. Engineers know the data intimately, but have very little insight into the business. Business leaders, in turn, don’t often have a granular understanding of the data. Historically, analysts have been the bridge between these two worlds. 
An AI agent requires a semantic layer. It needs context to perform its intended function. Without context, it will answer your prompt with confident gibberish. A well-maintained semantic layer becomes the information that is automatically eaten by the agent, allowing it to turn the raw ingredients into something digestible. 
The better the context, the better the outcome. The more accurate the semantic layer, the more accurate your agent. Trust is the ultimate measure of whether your analysts are effectively serving as guardians of the semantic layer. Users should stop wondering whether or not the output is correct. If they ask the same question multiple times, the same answer comes back. 
From Dashboards To Workflows
Data analysts are uniquely positioned within a company to understand both the technical contents of the data and the business goals that data is intended to support. But the role of the modern data analyst has evolved. The world has shifted sharply away from output creation, like dashboards, in favor of framework governance, or AI agents. Analysts must be able to build specialized agents that can dynamically respond to customer or end-user questions. 
Unlike a dashboard, which is static, a team of agents can help create and manage workflows. Think of it as a fleet, providing value to end users by making accurate decisions based on good data. Whoever utilizes that data needs access to the right information. The most critical responsibility of the analyst is to expose trusted data to agents. 
We don’t want agents querying the data directly, for myriad reasons. Security. Missed context. The job of the data analyst is to ensure that all of the data makes sense to the agent by explaining differences, outliers and relationships. 
By creating knowledge around the data, analysts help ensure that any agentic solution deployed will be accurate and trustworthy. This, in turn, enables users to confidently reach into the data for more productive outcomes. 
Empowering Your Data Team
While data analysts themselves will largely drive this shift, it’s leadership’s responsibility to get the ball rolling by creating an environment that fosters successful change. 
Step one? Ditch the dashboards. Some users will continue to ask for a static dashboard experience because that is what they are used to. Change is hard; it’s impossible when leaders don’t trigger, initiate and embrace that change. Facilitate the transition, because the people who actually have their hands on the tech won’t have the time to do that on their own. Some will try anyway if you don’t take charge—and the results are almost always disastrous. 
Facilitating those transitions also needs to be an objective of the company, rooted in measurable ROI. Define the success criteria for your company that will drive the desired change. Demonstrating value helps achieve buy-in. 
At the end of the day, this is a technology migration. It requires a change of mindset, a shift in the way your employees think about utilizing the available tools. Give them the space to run the projects themselves. Initiate change, then step out of the way so that your employees can take ownership of the results.
