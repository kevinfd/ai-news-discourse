# Is the Design Researcher Dead? Three Years of Debate Surrounding Generative AI and the Aftermath of Triangulation

note · Kakeru Asano · 2026-10-22

Is the Design Researcher Dead? Three Years of Debate Surrounding Generative AI and the Aftermath of Triangulation
In July 2024, at the international conference DIS (Designing Interactive Systems) [*1] held in Copenhagen, a workshop titled "Death of the Design Researcher?" was conducted [*2]. This was at a time when OpenAI had already announced its new ChatGPT model, GPT-4o, in May 2024, and its influence was growing in society. As generative AI began creating both text and images, the provocative title questioned whether the role of the design researcher—who seeks to depict unseen worlds through qualitative research and prototyping—would remain necessary.
The year before the workshop, I wrote a short text titled "Triangulation in Design Research in the Era of Large Language Models (LLMs)," in which I optimistically considered that by welcoming LLMs as "diverse investigators"—a concept of triangulation proposed by sociologist Norman K. Denzin—we might expect answers from a user's perspective. Using this workshop and the papers subsequently published by the organizers as a guide, I would like to re-examine my proposal from that time.
A workshop to rethink the role of the design researcher in the era of generative AI
This workshop was the second of three held consecutively at DIS by design researchers such as Willem van der Maden. The first, in 2023, aimed to take stock of the practices emerging in the field and to map out the role that generative AI plays [*3].
The second, in 2024, is "Death of the Design Researcher?," from which I borrowed the title of this article. Following the conference's overall theme, "Why Design?" [*1], it is based on "The Death of the Author," published by literary critic Roland Barthes in 1967. Just as Barthes liberated the meaning of a work from the author's intent and questioned the authority of the author, the premise here is whether generative AI might question the authority and role of the design researcher. Until now, design researchers, especially from the perspective of Research through Design (RtD), have been those who create prototypes and artifacts for inquiry themselves, generating knowledge through judgment and reflection during the creation process. However, if generative AI can instantly output sketches, future scenarios, and prototype screens, who created those artifacts, and whose knowledge can we say was gained from them?
The workshop set four questions: 1) How is generative AI changing design research? 2) Methods for incorporating generative AI into research and the new collaborations it creates. 3) How practices that generate knowledge through making, such as RtD, are changing. 4) How to handle authorship, agency, copyright, and trust in the results. It also aimed to collect published and unpublished research to summarize practical guidelines and ethical issues, and to create an online resource collection and a co-authored paper [*2].
The discussions from these two workshops were compiled by the organizers into a single essay, which would also be carried over into the approach for the third workshop the following year.
Five signs that stop thinking
That essay is "When Discourse Stalls," published by the organizers in March 2025 [*4]. Based on the two workshops and interviews with practitioners, they identified five simplified phrases that shut down discussions surrounding generative AI, calling them "semantic stopsigns."
1) "AI is untrustworthy," 2) "Generative AI is entirely an infringement of intellectual property," 3) "AI is just a tool," 4) "AI is bad for the environment," and 5) "AI is a race to the bottom." The authors' view is that because each of these five stop signs contains a partial truth, they cause people to stop thinking the moment they are uttered.
I would like to focus on 3) "AI is just a tool," which is closest to my proposal of welcoming AI as a partner that can be mastered. The authors argue against this view, which gives the user a sense of security that they can control it, and the refuser an excuse to dismiss it as a passing fad, and instead call for treating AI as a "relational medium" that shapes results.
The third workshop, held at the same DIS that year, was titled "From Dead-ends to Dialogue" in response to this categorization [*5]. Participants were asked to bring specific examples of when they had encountered these thought-stopping signs while using generative AI, and the format involved discussing issues such as using LLMs for transcription and how to attribute research that includes AI-generated content. The year after questioning "death," the unit of inquiry shifted from the survival of a profession to individual practices.
Triangulation that reveals the location
Around the same time, in the field of qualitative research, the pros and cons of "synthetic participants"—making LLMs act as respondents—were being debated. While some case studies suggest they can supplement initial research but cannot replace humans [*6], HCI researchers like Shivani Kapania have pointed out that using LLMs as proxies for participants excludes consent and agency, potentially undermining the legitimacy of qualitative research, and have termed this the "surrogate effect" [*7]. In 2023, I may have been ceding the participant's seat to an LLM that I intended to welcome as an investigator.
Denzin cited "diverse investigators" to have researchers with different positionalities compare interpretations and relativize an individual's bias. Triangulation works because the points of measurement are known. Even if we add an LLM that is not rooted in anyone's experience and inherits the biases of its training data, those points will remain invisible.
More-than-human design researcher Iohanna Nicenboim also questions where knowledge is situated and how to avoid exploitative relationships after prototyping with generative AI tools [*8]. If we overlap this with the proposal in the aforementioned essay to view AI as a relational medium, we are required to compare knowledge after recording where each investigator and tool stands and what they might overlook. I would like to call this "triangulation that reveals the location"—comparing different knowledge while keeping the locations clear. In doing so, the role of the design researcher is not being replaced, but is shifting toward the side that designs and records these relationships.
What should be done in Arimatsu?
I think this way because I have been involved with the shibori (tie-dye) production area in Arimatsu, Nagoya, for over 10 years. In interviews with artisans, they speak of knowledge that cannot be put into words, born between the body and the material—the tension of the binding, the condition of the fabric, and the atmosphere of the dyeing workshop. Even if I have an LLM answer as an artisan, what comes back are words from a location that stands nowhere in the production area (though perhaps if I fed it the data from interviews already conducted, it might return a different answer).
On the other hand, AI has already become indispensable for transcription, organizing records, and as a partner to question my own interpretations. The practice of placing people and materials in the participant's seat, and tools including AI and people in the investigator's seat, while recording and comparing their respective locations, might be exactly what was missing from my 2023 article. How to record the voices of non-human materials and tools is a question that has yet to be answered. Even so, I intend to verify the role of a recorder of locations in the field of Arimatsu from now on.
