# Anthropic’s Ambitions in Legal Technology Get a Reality Check

news.bloomberglaw.com · bloomberglaw · 2026-10-22

It was billed as a “fireside chat,” but law-firm innovation leaders had high hopes for a private meeting with Anthropic’s new head of Claude for Legal at a convention in Nashville, Tenn.
Attendees were accustomed to legal tech startups making well-tailored pitches on how they would train attorneys to use their products and address law firms’ needs for data privacy and work-product accuracy. What they heard in August was that Anthropic won’t let them negotiate key contract terms around privacy and data security unless they commit to a minimum amount of spending. They also left with the impression they’re more or less on their own for training, according to three sources who attended the meeting.
The presentation from Robert Mahari — just a few weeks into his new role at Anthropic at the time — underscored to some law firms that the company, despite its industry-leading AI models and world-altering potential, is still just a 5-year old startup figuring out how to sell its products. It’s trying to enter a market where many lawyers have developed trust in AI products made by legacy platforms or startups like Harvey.
“They need to wake up,” one of the people in the room said of Anthropic. “They do need to hear that we’re not impressed.”
Anthropic’s announcement earlier this year that it was adding legal capabilities including contract review, risk flagging, and contract negotiation to its Claude AI platform helped send legacy legal software and other stocks plummeting as part of fears that AI companies would make traditional software providers irrelevant.
Anthropic’s entrance to the market comes at a time when law firms are figuring out what to buy.
Forty-four percent of firms are using or exploring using Claude, tied with Thomson Reuters CoCounsel and trailing only Microsoft’s Copilot as the most popular AI tool among firms, according to a survey published last month by the International Legal Technology Association. But Anthropic hasn’t yet converted the high interest into long-term customers. Among the firms using or exploring Claude, just 7% said they had fully rolled it out to attorneys.
All that colored how the law firm leaders received Mahari’s presentation, according to interviews with five people who attended the meeting and were granted anonymity to describe a closed-door discussion.
“It landed awkwardly in a room full of people who are trying to see if they can actually deploy Claude and are under pressure from their stakeholders,” an attendee said.
Mahari has a JD-PhD from Harvard and MIT and researched at Stanford. He joined Anthropic after founding Akiva AI, a company that works with legal teams on developing AI tools.
Anthropic declined to make Mahari available to an interview, and he didn’t respond to a request for comment. An Anthropic spokesperson didn’t respond to questions or issues raised by innovation leaders in this story, and directed Bloomberg Law to two websites outlining the company’s products and policies.
Bloomberg Law is a legal technology provider.
Privacy Concerns
Law firms often negotiate some privacy and data security terms in their contracts with software vendors. Different firms have different risk tolerances, which they incorporate through negotiations with vendors.
Anthropic, one meeting attendee said, is not far off the pace on the privacy terms it included in its base contracts. But he said his firm often negotiates privacy terms, and Anthropic isn’t willing to do that.
Another meeting attendee said he balked at giving Claude access to email, because the system could then potentially access data from clients who don’t want their information in AI tools. The same person also took issue with Anthropic’s decision not to offer zero data retention policies for its Fable 5 model.
The Anthropic spokesperson said in an email the company doesn’t discuss “commercial terms,” and pointed to a Claude for Legal support pagethat says Anthropic doesn’t train its models on enterprise customers’ data by default. The spokesperson pointed to a separate Claude support page that says customers can select which services they connect to Claude. Anthropic offers customers zero data retention, meaning prompts aren’t stored, but the company retains inputs and outputs of its Mythos class models for 30 days so that Anthropic can conduct “safety monitoring,” according to the legal support page.
“We believe Claude’s commercial offerings can be configured by lawyers in a way that maintains privilege and confidentiality,” the company says on the Claude support page.
Making it Work
Some firms have accepted Anthropic’s terms, deciding that Claude meets their safety standards.
Ogletree Deakins has been running a pilot with Claude since February. Timothy Fox, the firm’s chief data and AI officer, said it has ownership over the data that goes into Claude and the answers it spits out.
Claude is good at analyzing large data sets, and the firm can use it to build prototypes of AI legal tools it wants to build. It’s easier to sign up for than traditional legal tech, and attorneys haven’t complained about it very much, a rare feat for a software tool, Fox said.
“Our deployment of Claude has been one of our most successful,” he said.
Despite the success, Fox said, “it is not a product that is geared for legal, and so they don’t design their enterprise security like a legal platform would. And so legal vendors are great because they know what a law firm is looking for.”
He’s willing to be patient as Anthropic learns the industry and adds more functions for lawyers. Fox, like other innovation leaders interviewed for this story, said Anthropic was still in the early days of developing products for the legal industry, and that it had only recently attracted the attention of major firms.
“We give them some grace and some credit,” he said.
Rising Competition
Anthropic is swaggering into the legal market as both top dog and underdog.
As top dog, it has raised more than $100 billion, built an industry-leading reputation for Claude, and is eyeing a $2 trillion valuation in a forthcoming IPO planned for mid-November, Bloomberg News has reported.
Those advantages were reflected in that private meeting in August, where innovation chiefs were excited that a company with Anthropic’s reputation is investing in legal work. Despite their misgivings about the meeting, some of the attendees who spoke with Bloomberg Law said they were still interested in paying for Claude—if it stands out from tools they’re already paying for.
Some law firms use enterprise tools developed by Harvey or Legora alongside subscriptions to Claude or ChatGPT Enterprise that attorneys can use for quick queries. Whether firms believe they need both is an open question.
“They may get there, but I don’t know if they’re there yet, especially for a firm that has invested as much in tools as we have,” Maureen Naughton, chief innovation & information officer at Lowenstein Sandler, said of Anthropic.
Anthropic isn’t the only tech giant looking at the legal industry. The day before the Nashville meeting, Google announced a suite of products for lawyers. A few weeks later, OpenAI made a similar announcement. Both have hired former legal tech leaders in roles that appear to be similar to Mahari’s.
“Six months ago, it looked like we had to have Claude because it’s so much better,” one innovation leader said. “We no longer feel the pressure because OpenAI closed the gap, if not surpassed Claude in many ways.”
Lack of Support
In evaluating enterprise software, firms are relying on the companies to show their attorneys how to use AI.
Legora, for example, hosted multiple full-day training sessions with attorneys at Crowell & Moring to show them how to use the tools in litigation, transactions and regulatory work, the firm’s chief innovation officer previously told Bloomberg Law. Polsinelli hosted more than 90 trainings with Thomson Reuters to get up to speed on CoCounsel, the firm’s chief operating officer said.
Innovation leaders said Anthropic doesn’t offer as many ready-to-use tools as those vendors or as much training. Another attendee said without the support of Anthropic, his firm might have to pay consultants to train its attorneys.
“The primary takeaway from that meeting was that unlike a lot of other legal tech vendors, there was a strong sense that we as an industry probably don’t rate significantly high on Anthropic’s universe of expected revenue, and therefore the level of attention that we would get as an industry is relatively low,” one meeting attendee said.
Anthropic could push some of the money it’s spending on computing power into legal-industry specific spending, said Mandeep Singh, global head of technology research at Bloomberg Intelligence. The company is courting users in a wide range of white-collar industries, from coding to healthcare, he said.
“They look for new markets given they do have pressure on sustaining that AI growth,” he said.
Bigger firms may have the resources to implement Claude themselves, but smaller firms lack the resources to do that, said Alex Finkel, chief innovation officer at Offit Kurman. Firms like his are looking for vendors that provide pre-built workflows, trainers and AI experts. Anthropic is still in the process of building that infrastructure, Finkel said.
“They are at the very beginning of that journey.”
