# The world can’t agree on AI safety. So why does the EU keep trying?

The Parliament Magazine · kindermaxi123 · 2026-10-22

The world can’t agree on AI safety. So why does the EU keep trying?
The European Union may not lead the world in developing frontier AI, but wants to lead in regulating it. The 27-member bloc is pushing for international agreements on the safe development of artificial intelligence — even though the United States and China, the world’s biggest AI powers, are unlikely to sign on.
Researchers and AI executives have repeatedly warned about the existential threat runaway AI could pose, and momentum for international guardrails is building.
Finland and Norway rallied 30 countries behind a proposal at a United Nations summit in September to control frontier AI models, calling for human oversight, mandatory pre-deployment testing and a new international oversight body. On Oct. 14, Ireland led 23 other EU members to back the Dublin Declaration for trustworthy AI development.
These agreements are largely symbolic, and U.S.-China competition makes a binding deal unlikely among the biggest players. Still, some leaders at major U.S.-based AI firms have endorsed the EU’s safety approach and called for international rules.
“Competitive pressure is exactly why we need international agreements,” said Risto Uuk, the head of EU AI policy research and governance at the Future of Life Institute. “No company or country can slow down alone.”
What would an international safety agreement actually do?
International AI safety agreements are efforts by multiple countries to set shared rules for developing and deploying general-purpose AI models — the technology behind chatbots such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini.
They establish shared principles for safety, transparency and human oversight but most lack enforcement mechanisms. Still, voluntary pledges can build towards a binding treaty or influence national laws on AI, according to Uuk.
The latest regulatory efforts target frontier models capable of significant harm, amid growing concern about AI acting maliciously. In September, an OpenAI model hacked the Australian government’s Medicare portal, and in July, another model hacked into a popular coding website, Hugging Face.
The EU, China and some U.S. states have some form of AI regulation. But Uuk said that international frameworks remain important because AI competition is global. Without one, he said, companies would race to release dangerous models without oversight.
Some proposals call for a binding treaty and an international body. That organization would monitor models and could authorize pauses in development if risks become too high. For example, the group PauseAI has called for the establishment of an “international AI safety agency” to conduct model evaluations.
Some analysts have proposed an arms-control-style pact, comparing it to the 1968 Nuclear Non-Proliferation treaty to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons. Others say the analogy doesn’t hold, as AI, unlike nuclear weapons, was largely developed by private firms, rather than nations, making it harder to regulate through the same model.
What agreements already exist — and what do they require?
The closest to a binding international treaty is the Council of Europe’s 2024 Framework Convention on AI and Human Rights which required international human rights law to apply to the AI systems of signatories, and called for setting up independent oversight boards.
However, the U.S. has not ratified the agreement, meaning it’s not legally binding on the country with the largest model providers. Microsoft signed a cooperation agreement with the Council of Europe to work on AI, but it’s also not legally binding.
International agreements could apply to the most powerful AI models — for example, models trained using computational power above a certain threshold would come under greater oversight.
They could also require independent groups to evaluate models before release. Companies could be required to test models against sensitive cybersecurity infrastructure, for instance, to ensure that the model couldn’t be used by malicious actors to hack into critical infrastructure.
Advocates of pausing AI development often point to “p(doom),” the probability that increasingly powerful AI models could pose an existential risk to the world. One frequently cited example is the alignment problem, or the possibility that AI systems could become misaligned with human interests.
Why haven’t the U.S. or China signed on?
U.S. firms and Chinese state-backed companies are leading the AI race, but neither government has shown willingness to adhere to a binding global agreement. Instead, both have pursued national or state-level AI agreements instead, effectively competing over who sets the rules for global AI governance.
At the end of September, United States President Donald Trump and the leaders of major U.S. technology companies signed a “White House Accord on Superintelligence.” It called for partnering with independent groups to audit models and a board of directors to fix security risks.
However, Connor Martin, a fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations, said that the agreement is toothless. Trump has repeatedly refused to back an international agreement, arguing that it would benefit China.
China has been more open to international agreements on AI, but has largely used such agreements to stake out a distinctive, development-focused position emphasizing expanding access to the technology for Global South countries, rather than prioritizing safety.
In July, China established the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization, or WAICO, headquartered in Shanghai. No EU or G7 economies joined; the organization has emphasized development and economic growth.
Are AI companies split on safety?
U.S. tech giants leading the AI frontier such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft and Google have differing approaches to safety and international agreements.
GoogleDeepmind co-founder and chair, Demis Hassabis, said that hacking incidents this summer were “early warning shots.” “It seems obvious that we need an international standards body of some sort,” he said.
In September, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei similarly said that it was necessary to “pace the frontier” of model development.
OpenAI, however, struck a different tone. Speaking at the AI summit in Ireland in October, Sarah Friar, the chief financial officer of OpenAI, said that the frontier needs pacing, but that it shouldn’t be slowed down so much that U.S. companies are outpaced by those using the technology with “malicious intent.”
Why does the EU keep pushing for a deal?
Even with the U.S. and China unlikely to sign on, the EU keeps pushing — partly because the bloc has already developed the world’s leading AI regulation, according to Uuk.
With EU firms like Paris-based Mistral, falling far behind their U.S. and China counterparts, Europe has little direct leverage over the technology, and more regulation risks putting its tech companies at a competitive disadvantage.
The EU has also already experienced the risks firsthand. In May, frontier model-provider Anthropic shut the EU out of its most advanced model, a move that heightened cybersecurity exposure in Europe and initially left the bloc unable to test its own systems against it.
Asked why the EU continues to push for agreements even as the U.S. and China look unlikely to sign-on, Commissioner Henna Virkunen said that rising safety incidents made international agreements more important.
“We see clearly that the competition is so high globally that it seems that all the companies are not investing enough time and resources really to test their models.”
Uuk said that the AI Act and its voluntary compliance tool give Europe the regulatory approach and expertise to help govern AI globally.
What happens next?
The next major moment for international AI safety agreements comes in June 2027, at the Geneva AI Safety Convention, continuing the series started at Bletchley Park in 2023. Swiss lawmakers want to make coordinated minimum standards for risky AI models the center of the discussions.
Since Aug. 2, the EU began enforcing the AI Act against largest model providers, including OpenAI and Anthropic, and has requested additional information about both companies’ models.
One promising path forward, according to Zach Meyers, the director of research at CERRE, is for the EU to agree on voluntary safety and testing standards with other middle powers, like Canada and the United Kingdom.
Because frontier firms need customers outside of the U.S. and China, such an agreement could help “set expectations” for the top companies. “It does not mean Washington would necessarily follow, but that it might at least tolerate this type of approach without threatening to withdraw third countries’ access to frontier AI,” he said.
Even without the U.S. and China on board, international AI safety agreements matter. George Colville, a policy analyst with Open Markets Europe, calls this a “form of AI counter-power via third-power collaboration and coordination.” He points to Brazil and South Korea, both of which adopted rules similar to the EU’s AI Act, as evidence of broader alignment — one that could strengthen the power of smaller countries’ power against AI giants.
