# Most AI data centers may never be built. Blame the 'bragawatts.'

Business Insider · Alistair Barr, Alistair Barr (abarr@businessinsider.com) · 2026-10-22

Kevin O'Leary speaks at the University of Southern Indiana. MaCabe Brown / Courier &amp; Press / USA TODAY NETWORK via Imagn Images/Reuters Newcomers like Kevin O'Leary face skepticism due to a lack of experience in data center development. Bernstein analysts dubbed O'Leary and others "bragawatts." Most announced projects will never happen. "Press releases are cheap…concrete is not." The AI industry has a problem with bragging. Companies are announcing enormous new data centers to power the AI boom. Some promise facilities bigger than anything the industry has built before. There's just one problem: Most of these projects may never happen. Bernstein Research analyzed thousands of planned US data centers and concluded that only about 35% of the announced capacity is likely to get built. That's roughly 170 gigawatts of data center capacity out of a total of almost 500 gigawatts. The rest? A significant chunk of it belongs to a category the industry has a wonderfully dismissive name for: "Bragawatts." These are ambitious announcements, often from newcomers with little experience building data centers, that have a slim chance of becoming reality. "Press releases are cheap…concrete is not," Bernstein analysts wrote in a research note. Bernstein began its analysis with more than 7,000 data center projects across North America, drawing on information from Aterio, a company that tracks construction through satellite imagery, permits, and other records. The analysts narrowed that to more than 5,000 projects (partly by excluding those already canceled). Then they assessed the chances of these remaining projects being completed. The analysts considered factors such as whether construction had started, whether electricity was available, and whether the companies involved had successfully built data centers before. The results revealed a striking divide. The 'bragawatt brigade' Kevin O'Leary. Daniel Delgado Jr./Disney via Getty Images Experienced operators including Amazon, Google, Microsoft , and Meta landed at the top . Bernstein gave these companies perfect scores of 5 out of 5 for their development credentials. Other data center specialists, such as Digital Realty , Equinix, and CoreSite, got top marks, too. O'Leary Digital , associated with celebrity investor Kevin O'Leary , ended up at the bottom, with a credibility score of 0.35 out of 5, according to Bernstein. His company has announced 15 gigawatts of data center projects, and Bernstein estimates that only 489 megawatts, or about 3%, will actually materialize. "Sorry, Kevin," the analysts wrote. Although the term "bragawatts" has been doing the rounds in the data center industry lately, it's unclear who coined it, Bernstein analyst Madison Rezaei told me. "I wish I could take credit for that," she said. "I believe I originally heard it from an Equinix executive, but I don't think he owned it either." She said that while some ambitious newcomers could succeed, they haven't yet demonstrated they can deliver. "It's not that all of these guys are sort of terrible and not going to deliver, but we just have lower confidence because we don't know them," Rezaei said. O'Leary Digital responds O'Leary Digital disputed Bernstein's assessment, saying the research doesn't account for substantial work that occurs before construction begins. While acknowledging that O'Leary Digital is a young company, CEO Paul Palandjian said the team has decades of experience building major infrastructure projects and that its proposed 15 gigawatts of capacity would be built in stages over several years. The company said it has secured binding agreements covering about 11,000 acres in Utah and 13,000 acres in Alberta, obtained a 30-year development agreement in Utah, and made progress on planning, engineering, and permits at both sites. It also said the projects would generate their own electricity. The company said its early development work is funded, additional capital is committed to reaching key milestones, and experienced engineering and construction partners are involved. "We would rather be judged on milestones than on a model," Palandjian said. 'An announcement in search of a project' Opponents of the proposed La Osa data center attend a Pinal County Board of Supervisors meeting in Florence, Arizona, on August 26. USA TODAY Network via Reuters Connect Other low scorers in the "bragawatt" brigade included Vermaland and Joule Capital Partners, according to Bernstein analysis based on Aterio data. Joule has announced 50 data center projects with a combined capacity of 11.7 gigawatts. Bernstein estimates that only about 11% of that capacity will actually get built. Almost all of Joule's planned capacity remains at the announcement stage, with little evidence of construction progress or of major customers committing to use the facilities, Bernstein wrote in a July note to investors. The analysts gave Joule a credibility score of 2.05 out of 5, placing it among the lowest-ranked developers in its database. "An announcement in search of a project," the analysts wrote. Vermaland has announced plans for a massive data center project in Arizona. Bernstein estimates that only 4% of its proposed 2.2 gigawatts of capacity will get built. The company is primarily a landowner, not a data center developer. Its proposed $33 billion La Osa project near Eloy had already been sharply scaled back amid local opposition and without construction approval. In late August, regulators formally rejected the project. Even before that, the analysts gave Vermaland a credibility score of 0.75 out of 5. Joule and Vermaland didn't respond to emails seeking comment this week. A yawning gap for data centers Sergio Toro, CEO of Aterio, sees similar patterns in his firm's voluminous, detailed data. Aterio's tracking shows that roughly 30 to 40 gigawatts of new data center capacity get announced each month. Yet only about 7 gigawatts begin construction each month, while 2 to 3 gigawatts actually start operating. This leaves a yawning gap. Many announced projects get withdrawn before construction begins — and before real money has been spent. "They just make the announcement, but they didn't get their approvals. Or they make multiple filings in multiple locations to see which one gets selected," Toro told me. "That's why we track with satellite if there's real movement on the ground or not." Indeed, Bernstein analysts wrote recently that the amount of "stranded" data center capacity has surged. These are projects that are either canceled, delayed, or not approved. The AI boom's three tiers Toro sees three broad groups competing to build AI infrastructure. At the top are the established technology giants, including Amazon, Microsoft, and Google. They've built data centers for decades and generally deliver on their commitments. Most of their projects will get done, he said. In the middle are "neoclouds," newer companies such as CoreWeave that rent out computing power specifically for AI. Although they're more vulnerable to financing problems and delays, many are making solid progress, especially because some of these players already have access to electricity. "We see them advancing quite well," Toro said. "Sometimes we see some delays, but it's not something significant or any kind of big capacity that is at risk." Then come the newcomers making extraordinary promises. That's where most delays and cancellations happen, Toro said. Even Amazon needs a backup plan A technician works at an Amazon Web Services AI data center in New Carlisle, Indiana. Noah Berger/Getty Images via Amazon Web Services Not every project that disappears was a fantasy, though. Even a top player like Amazon struggles sometimes to secure electricity and other supplies on time for these huge projects. Business Insider's Eugene Kim reported exclusively this week on the challenges Amazon faces with its record AI buildout . The biggest, most experienced tech companies deliberately pursue more potential data center capacity than they need because they know some developments will fail or get delayed. Major AI companies were committing to three or four times the space and electricity they needed over the following two years, Jeffrey Moerdler, a data center lawyer, estimated during an interview with Bernstein analysts. His reasoning: Of data center projects that haven't started construction, roughly a third will arrive on schedule, another third will be delayed, and the remainder won't happen. Rezaei and Toro estimate that the top players don't over-commit that much — more like double the capacity they need at most. The strategy provides a "Plan B," as Toro put it, in case of delays involving electricity, equipment, permits, local opposition, or other factors. Texas has a ghost problem The Decker Creek Power Station in Austin. Brandon Bell/Getty Images This helps explain another extraordinary number. Data center companies have requested connections for roughly 450 gigawatts of electricity demand on Texas' main power grid , more than five times the grid's roughly 85 gigawatts of existing capacity, according to Toro and others.  Many of those requests are placeholders rather than firm construction plans. Toro calls them "ghost projects." These speculative projects clog the waiting lists for electricity connections, making the electricity shortage look a lot worse than it actually is. "Developers routinely spam projects across several sites, and many are speculative builds with no customer yet," Ryan McEntush, a partner at venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz, wrote in a recent blog. "Often these new developers have never even plugged in a GPU, let alone a power plant." Texas has paused applications to review and weed out projects that aren't serious. Toro, from Aterio, expects this to favor the established players at the expense of the bragawatts. The real AI boom None of this means the AI infrastructure boom is imaginary. Aterio tracks thousands of buildings, including major projects already under construction. Bernstein estimates that nearly 80% of the capacity the largest technology companies are directly developing is credible. And when speculative projects disappear, Rezaei doesn't expect a wave of unpaid debt. Many will fail before building begins, the stage when developers borrow serious money through project financing.
