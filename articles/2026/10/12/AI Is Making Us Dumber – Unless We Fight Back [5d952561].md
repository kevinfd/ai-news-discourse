# AI Is Making Us Dumber – Unless We Fight Back

baromae.blogspot.com · odilelof · 2026-10-12

Can AI Really Make Us Smarter? What the Research Says
Source of information: This article is based on a publication from the Greater Good Science Center (University of California, Berkeley), titled "How Artificial Intelligence Can Contribute to Critical Thinking," written by Monideepa Tarafdar and published on October 12, 2026. This article is republished from The Conversation under a Creative Commons license.
For years, we've been told that technology will save us time, make us more productive, more efficient. Every new tool, every new platform, every new app arrives wrapped in that same promise. Generative artificial intelligence came with the same pitch, only bigger — louder, more disruptive, more transformative. Scientists, teachers, lawyers, business leaders, supply chain managers, marketing professionals, research scholars — they are all using it now to generate ideas, solve complex problems, and design innovative solutions. Basically, for everything that used to require thinking, creativity, and judgment.
And the appeal is obvious. Why spend three hours drafting a legal brief when AI can give you a skeleton in thirty seconds? Why struggle through a creative block when a chatbot can produce twenty ideas before you finish your coffee? Why wrestle with a complex data problem when a model can crunch it in milliseconds? The efficiency gains are real. The time savings are real. The competitive pressure is real.
But the problem is that research is starting to show a side effect nobody really anticipated. By asking AI to think for us, we slowly stop using our own critical thinking. Researchers call this "cognitive offloading." In plain English: we delegate, we accept the fast and confident answers from the machine, and we stop asking questions. The result? Fast solutions, sure. But sometimes poor quality ones. And worse — a slow erosion of the very skills that made us valuable in the first place.
This isn't just a workplace issue. It's a human issue. It's about who we become when we outsource our thinking. It's about the kind of society we build when convenience becomes the highest value. It's about whether we're raising a generation of thinkers or a generation of prompt-typists.
The Study That Changes the Conversation
And that's where it gets interesting. Because this isn't inevitable. A study led by Monideepa Tarafdar and her colleagues, involving 45 knowledge workers at a U.S. public university, offers another path. The idea is simple but powerful: instead of using AI to confirm what we already think, we can use it to contradict us. To create friction. To force us to think. To make us uncomfortable in the best possible way.
They call it "friction-generating queries." The principle? Ask AI to find information that contradicts your own point of view, to explore unlikely perspectives, to play devil's advocate. And guess what? It works. Workers who use AI this way significantly improve their performance. Previous research on human-AI collaboration for loan evaluations had similar results — people who pushed back against the AI's initial recommendations ended up making better decisions.
The findings suggest something profound: the value of AI isn't in the answers it provides. It's in the questions it forces us to ask. It's not a machine that thinks for us. It's a machine that makes us think harder — if we let it.
Real Examples From the Research
Let's look at a concrete example from the study. A marketing professional asked AI to create virtual customers with personas that would make them dislike a product under development — an accounting certificate program. The negative feedback from the AI revealed customer preferences the program designer had never considered, like using multiple cases from different industries to illustrate a principle, rather than cases from the same industry. A simple idea, but one that might never have surfaced without that intentional friction. The marketing professional didn't just get a better product. They got a better way of thinking about their audience.
Another example: an attorney used AI to find obscure legal loopholes and to consider how companies might exploit them. The result was surprising but realistic scenarios of hard-to-detect unethical behavior in employees. It wasn't the AI that found the solution. It was the attorney who used the AI as an intellectual sparring partner. The AI brought the raw material. The attorney brought the judgment, the experience, the ethical framework. That combination — not the AI alone — produced the insight.
Now, exploring unlikely and opposing ideas can feel disorienting. It shakes you up. It questions what you thought you knew. It forces you to defend your assumptions or abandon them. But that's exactly where the value lies. Knowledge workers should lean on their own expertise to question surprising information that an AI system might provide. It's a balance: the machine suggests, the human decides. The machine generates, the human evaluates. The machine explores, the human judges.
Another case reported in the study: an operations research scientist working with supply chains adapted unfamiliar computing programs from other fields suggested by AI — signal processing and wireless communication. These programs were new to him, but his strong understanding of supply network modeling helped him see how he could apply them. He created a stronger program of his own. AI didn't replace his expertise. It amplified it. It opened doors he didn't know existed, but he was the one who walked through them.
A study Tarafdar was not involved with found that creative writers produce better copy when they use AI as a sounding board rather than to ghostwrite. The nuance is huge. Using AI to discuss, to test ideas, to hear a different voice — that's not the same as asking it to write for you. One is collaboration. The other is substitution. And the difference between those two things is the difference between getting better and getting worse.
Knowledge workers can also use AI's outputs to examine topics from multiple viewpoints, deepening their own knowledge. A research scholar in the study probed an AI system for journal papers that used a concept he was using, but in contrasting settings, which helped him understand the concept from different angles. A lawyer followed up on legal citations suggested by an AI model by asking for different types of illustrative cases. That helped her fully understand the original case and write a strong legal brief. In both cases, the AI wasn't the answer. It was the doorway to a better answer.
How Organizations Can Make This Work
So how can organizations encourage this practice? Leaders can encourage knowledge workers to develop friction-generating queries of AI models. A straightforward approach is to use prompts that challenge rather than agree with a person's thinking. People can also draw on their own knowledge, work experiences, and professional ethics to scrutinize AI outputs. Sometimes people realize that a response lacks originality, even if it is factually correct. Factual correctness is not the same as insight. And insight is what actually moves the needle.
Organization leaders can also create programs for knowledge workers to share their experiences and insights. Regular sessions — led by cross-functional AI task forces or AI champions — can help knowledge workers keep up with rapidly evolving AI tools. The pace of change is relentless. What worked six months ago may be obsolete today. Shared learning is one of the few ways to keep up without burning out.
Providing resources is important, too. Internal AI bootcamps and workshops led by AI experts or experienced users can help workers understand what AI tools can and cannot do. Because let's be honest: most people don't actually know the limits of these systems. They assume the AI knows more than it does. They assume it's more reliable than it is. They assume it's more objective than it is. None of those assumptions are safe.
A professor leading an initiative about AI use in teaching described how she developed internal competitions with prizes for professors who successfully completed AI bootcamps. Organizations can also offer AI sandboxes — safe, private environments where employees can experiment and learn how to use AI tools without risking real-world consequences. The sandbox is where you learn. The real world is where you apply. But without the sandbox, people learn on the job, and sometimes they learn the wrong lessons.
In these efforts to support knowledge workers, an important detail is pointing out that adding friction can appear to make an AI system work against you, but — as new research shows — it actually helps it work for you. It feels counterintuitive. It feels like you're fighting the tool. But you're not. You're using the tool the way it was meant to be used: as a partner, not as a replacement.
The Deeper Implications
Honestly, this research couldn't come at a better time. We're in the middle of an AI boom. Everyone wants to use it, everyone wants to move faster, everyone wants to be the first to market, the first to ship, the first to scale. But we forget one fundamental thing: our brain is like a muscle. If we don't use it, it atrophies. Cognitive offloading isn't some abstract theory — it's a real risk. We already see it around us: people who accept whatever ChatGPT tells them without checking, without questioning, without thinking.
And this isn't an anti-AI argument. Far from it. AI tools are genuinely useful. They help us brainstorm, draft, edit, research, organize. But there's a difference between using AI as a tool and using it as a crutch. A tool extends your reach. A crutch replaces your leg. And right now, a lot of people are reaching for the crutch.
The idea of "friction" is counterintuitive in a culture obsessed with smoothness and frictionless experiences. We want one-click everything. We want instant answers. We want zero resistance. But friction is where learning happens. Friction is where growth happens. Friction is where you discover that your first instinct was wrong, that your assumptions were shaky, that your reasoning had a hole in it. That's not a bug. That's a feature.
Using AI to contradict us, to push back, to force us out of our intellectual comfort zone — that's a way to stay sharp. To stay switched on. To avoid becoming empty shells who let a machine think for them. It's a discipline. It's a practice. It's a habit you have to build intentionally, because the default behavior — the lazy behavior — is to just accept whatever the AI says and move on.
There's a line between using a tool and becoming dependent on it. And that line gets blurry fast when the tool is as seductive as generative AI. The research is clear: the workers who get the most out of AI are the ones who push back against it, not the ones who just nod along. The ones who ask "Is this right?" not the ones who ask "Is this done?"
So yes, AI can help us. But only if we use it intelligently. Not as an oracle. As a debate partner. As a sparring partner. As a mirror that reflects our own contradictions back at us. That is where the real difference lies between those who will use AI to improve themselves and those who will use it to dumb themselves down.
And here's the thing that worries us most: this isn't just about work. It's about how we think, how we raise our kids, how we engage with ideas as a society. If we outsource our critical thinking to machines, we don't just lose productivity — we lose something far more valuable. We lose our ability to disagree thoughtfully, to reason through complexity, to hold two opposing ideas in our heads at once.
Think about it. The whole point of a free society is that people can think for themselves. They can evaluate evidence. They can weigh arguments. They can change their minds when the facts change. If we hand that responsibility over to machines, what's left? What happens to democracy when nobody can reason independently? What happens to innovation when nobody can question the status quo? What happens to us as a people when we stop thinking altogether?
These aren't hypothetical questions. They're the questions of our time. And the research on cognitive offloading suggests we're already drifting in the wrong direction. The fix isn't to abandon AI. The fix is to use it differently. To use it in a way that sharpens us instead of softening us. To use it in a way that makes us think harder, not less. To use it in a way that turns every interaction into an opportunity for growth.
That's what the friction-generating approach offers. It's not a gimmick. It's a discipline. It's a way of working that treats AI as a tool for intellectual challenge rather than intellectual escape. And honestly, that's the only way this ends well.
Final Thoughts
So what do you think? Do you use AI to confirm your ideas or to challenge them? Do you ask it to agree with you or to push back? Do you treat it as an oracle or as a sparring partner? The question is worth asking. Not just for your work, but for your mind. Because the way you use AI today shapes the way you think tomorrow. And the way we all use AI today shapes the kind of culture, the kind of civilization we leave behind.
The research is clear. The path forward is clear. Friction isn't the enemy of progress. It's the engine of it. And the sooner we embrace that — individually, organizationally, culturally — the better off we'll all be.
