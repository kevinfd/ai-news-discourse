# What AI leaves out of the answer still counts

Fast Company · Pete Pachal · 2026-10-23

AI companies are starting to pay for content that shows up in answers. The case for also paying for the library behind them is stronger than it looks.
BY Pete Pachal
Pete Pachal is a journalist and the creator of Media Copilot, a newsletter and podcast that examines how AI is changing media, journalism, and the news.
There’s a contradiction emerging in how publishers get paid when AI uses their content. The licensing deals that OpenAI, Meta, Amazon, and others have been signing these past few years appear to be slowing down, at least for the publishers still waiting on one.
Publishers are speaking about this openly. Digiday hosted a London town hall recently, and one publishing executive said discussions with AI companies have slowed down. Also, Katya Gorchinskaya, a Ukrainian journalist and media consultant who built J·Index, a database of thousands of AI implementations in newsrooms, separately told the Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism that the market for deals with model makers is effectively closed.
At the same time, AI demand for content continues to go up. I recently spoke with Jonathan Roberts, chief innovation officer at People Inc., for The Media Copilot podcast, and he revealed that the number of different bots that try to scrape content from the company’s media properties (including People, InStyle, and Investopedia) now numbers in the tens of thousands every day. The company blocks all but 38 of them. At that Digiday London event, Fiona Spooner, managing director of consumer revenue at the Financial Times, said one trial account tried to access about a million articles in eight hours from 700,000 IP addresses.
I also see this in my GEO client work: the media properties often start from the position that having bots hammer your site all day is cost-prohibitive, so they block them outright. It makes sense, but if the end goal is to monetize your content in AI systems, the slowdown in licensing agreements suggests an all-or-nothing stance won’t give you much leverage.
All of this points to an evolving approach to payment: metered pricing. The industry has already been moving in this direction, but the question that doesn’t yet have a clear answer is what those meters should measure.
Metered pricing has been around for a while. TollBit was one of the first to offer a “pay per crawl” model, directing bots to a gate where they could elect to pay a price set by the site, or go away (the company says its technology is now installed on more than 7,000 publisher sites). Cloudflare followed suit last year with a fanfare announcement that it would block AI bots by default for new sites on its network and provide the tools for publishers to charge by the crawl.
Things have evolved since then. There’s now more energy behind a “pay per use” model, meaning a content provider would only get paid when their content contributes to an answer, not at the moment it’s scraped. Cloudflare itself made that switch in July, with Ceramic.ai and You.com as its first partners, and Google and Microsoft are testing similar models. Cloudflare’s program is still in beta, but Lara Cohen, Cloudflare’s VP of strategic partnerships for media, creators, and AI, told me the company is working toward a system that alerts publishers when a buyer wants their content and lets them accept, decline, or negotiate.
