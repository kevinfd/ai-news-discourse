THE BIG STORY
For months, people have wondered whether the cyber disaster predicted by Anthropic and OpenAI earlier this year would ever materialize, or if it was just doom-mongering and hype. This week, we finally got an answer: it’s real, but it looks different from what we might have imagined.
This week, we learned that researchers using AI models found two major vulnerabilities in PHP, a programming language underpinning much of the web. Details of the vulnerabilities leaked before the fixes were released, causing chaos: government websites, the Internet Archive, and various other important services were quickly yanked so they could be fixed before they were compromised. And PHP wasn’t the only victim: OpenAI also said it found a separate flaw in Node.js, another backbone of the web, which was patched this week.
Widespread panic ensued. “Feels like March 1, 2020 for Cyber. Not normal, something is happening. Internet lockdowns in a couple of months?” said former OpenAI executive Joshua Achiam.
But how worried should we actually be? In some sense, this week’s events were things working as intended. Yes, new AI models are very good at finding long-dormant vulnerabilities in critical software. But there’s a clear advantage for defenders. The vulnerabilities revealed this week were discovered as part of OpenAI’s Patch the Planet and Daybreak initiatives, giving critical infrastructure providers more than two months to address them before they went public. Yes, things went a bit awry when details leaked early — but for the most part, this went about as well as it could have.
“About as well as it could have” is a long way from “well,” though. Yes, the PHP issues can be fixed. But those fixes actually have to be installed across the web. Many sites and devices will take weeks or months to update. Some, still active but no longer maintained, will never get patched. Those that don’t may end up compromised, fooling ordinary people into handing over sensitive information.
And this is just the start. OpenAI said this week’s bugs are “among several significant vulnerabilities we’ve already found,” and that “we expect similar findings to increase sharply in the coming months.” Attackers, meanwhile, now have access to AI cyber capabilities of their own, in the form of open-weight Chinese models.
Defenders just about won the race this time. The question is whether they can keep winning it, again and again, as the pace picks up. That doesn’t mean concrete harm is inevitable or imminent. Joshua Saxe, who worked on AI for security at Meta and now runs a cybersecurity startup, notes that cheaper offensive capability “doesn’t immediately translate into damages.” Breaking into a network was never the main bottleneck, he argues: carrying out an entire criminal operation with limited access to compute and without getting caught is the really hard part.
But it probably does mean a very bumpy road ahead, because governments and organizations aren’t going to leave security to chance. Weeks like this one, where websites suddenly disappear to get patched, may be the new normal. Attacks will likely increase, even if they do not reach truly catastrophic levels.
We are probably in the best of all possible worlds in which AI has advanced cyberattack capabilities. The problem is that none of those worlds were ever good ones.

THE DISCOURSE
Nathan Calvin got “viscerally angry” reading Anthropic’s draft federal AI safety bill:
- “For all of its warning about the potential for AI to cause a catastrophe that kills literally everyone on the planet, Anthropic’s proposed federal law likely makes it harder than under existing law for a court to get an order blocking or restricting the development of frontier models? WTF??”
Anthony Cimino replied carefully on behalf of Anthropic’s federal affairs team:
- “I’ve seen valid questions about whether we have sufficiently updated our proposal following this summer’s incidents. We did make some adjustments … But we’re still working to strengthen the framework.”
Calvin thanked him for responding on a weekend, then said:
- “[I]t’s worth asking the question: what is Anthropic?”
- “One answer is an extremely large tech company that exists to make its employees and investors mind-bogglingly wealthy … Another answer is an AI safety organization, whose core values are those of the sort laid out in the 2023 essay from Anthropic’s co-founders … ‘Anthropic’s role will be to provide as much evidence as possible that AI safety techniques cannot prevent serious or catastrophic safety risks from advanced AI, and to sound the alarm so that the world’s institutions can channel collective effort towards preventing the development of dangerous AIs.’”
- “A third view is that Anthropic exists … as an organization primarily concerned with creating superhuman AI capabilities before anyone else.”
- “When I look at many notable actions that Anthropic has taken over the past year, that evidence seems most consistent to me with the view that Anthropic is primarily trying to win the race to super intelligence, and that other considerations (including safety) are fundamentally secondary.”
Dario Amodei stepped in to reply to Calvin directly:
- “Our strategy has always been what I’ve called the ‘race to the top’ — by showing how it’s possible to build the technology responsibly, we can raise the bar for the entire industry. The forces that drive the race to the top are hugely more effective if the company practicing them is at the frontier.”
- “I believe that if we take a list of things that Anthropic did in the past year that were pro-safety and commercially costly to us, they far outweigh the list you’ve made.”
- “For each decision, you can come up with ulterior motives that could have driven us — I think it is a general truth that any decision, on any topic, can always be portrayed as a nefarious scheme. But most of these end up being pretty farfetched or don’t fully make sense if you actually examine them closely.”
- “I think that even if I conceded every item in your list, I would happily hold up mine next to it; I don’t think it’s a close call.”
Amodei specifically described elements of its draft bill as:
- “Rhetoric or policy communication that wasn’t carefully written or resulting from fast-changing circumstances not propagating from one team to another fast enough.”
Calvin stuck the landing:
- “I hope that Anthropic reflects on this picture, supports stronger regulation, exercises extraordinary caution when advocating for weak regulation, and doubles down on its founding purpose of helping the world safely navigate transformative AI together, instead of prioritizing Anthropic’s ability to win a race that imposes intolerable risks on everyone alive.”
Ashley Gold wondered:
- “You think these AI people ever just call each other instead of posting essays on X”
