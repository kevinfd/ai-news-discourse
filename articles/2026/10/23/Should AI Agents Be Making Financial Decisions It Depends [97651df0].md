# Should AI Agents Be Making Financial Decisions? It Depends.

Signal Capture
Signals from the frontlines of AI adoption
Taktile CEO: AI Agents Shouldn't Make Every Financial Decision
Banks and insurers can now automate parts of underwriting, claims and onboarding that once required people to read documents, investigate cases and apply policy. Maik Taro Wehmeyer, CEO of financial decision platform Taktile, says choosing where agents belong inside those processes is becoming as important as adopting them in the first place.
“Building an agent has become relatively easy, but putting that agent into a regulated process where a bank or insurer can trust and control its decisions is much harder,” Wehmeyer told Newsweek.
In practice, he described a financial decision as a combination of agents, data sources, fixed rules and human review, with each serving a different role in the process.
Claims processing shows how those pieces can fit together. An agent might extract information from documents and map it against a customer’s coverage before an insurer applies its existing rules. Routine claims can proceed automatically, while unusual cases are sent to a person.
“A fixed rule is the right tool when an answer needs to be deterministic,” Wehmeyer said.
His example was a policy preventing approval when a customer’s business is located in a particular country, which can remain a fixed rule. Agents become useful when information requires interpretation, such as financial data scattered across documents or an invoice written in another language.
Banks and insurers often start with a narrow group of straightforward cases and allow more to proceed automatically as the agent proves accurate on simpler work. As more cases move through automatically, the share handled without a person becomes an easy metric for leaders to watch.
“You can increase your automation rate while damaging business performance if the system starts approving bad loans or paying claims it shouldn't,” Wehmeyer said.
Tracking approval and decline rates over time can show whether the balance between the two is changing once an agent is in production. Wehmeyer also pointed to override rate, or how often a person corrects an agent’s work. Patterns in those corrections can reveal which types of cases the agent is repeatedly getting wrong and help improve how similar cases are handled.
Complex cases still move to people, which changes what review entails once an agent has already gathered information and analyzed the case.
“The reviewer should be able to see what information the agent used and understand how it reached its conclusion so that human review doesn’t become a rubber stamp,” he said.
Institutions also need evidence of how agents perform on cases where the correct outcome is already known. Wehmeyer said historical examples can provide that test before an agent reaches production.
“The failure mode the [finance] industry should be most concerned about is when an AI agent gets something wrong in a way that looks right,” he said.
Once an agent is live, organizations can continue comparing its decisions with the data and context behind each outcome to catch recurring errors before they become patterns.
The same testing becomes more consequential if agents are eventually allowed to propose changes to the logic behind regulated processes. Wehmeyer said those changes should be evaluated against past decisions before reaching customers, with people retaining approval authority and the ability to reverse them if outcomes deteriorate.
Responsibility for the final decision remains with the bank or insurer, even when an outside model or technology provider plays a role. Business owners should have direct visibility into and control over the agents involved, including how they affect approvals, declines and other outcomes. The record of how an individual decision was reached also matters months later, when the institution may need to revisit the case.
“The clearest warning sign is when nobody can easily explain why the system made a particular decision,” Wehmeyer said. “If a team has to reconstruct what happened after the fact, governance hasn’t kept pace with automation.”

Prompt Injection
What’s one recent insight you’ve learned about AI?
“One recent realization is that AI has dramatically reduced the time it takes to produce an answer, but not necessarily the time it takes to prove that answer is right.
In marketing, AI can analyze performance data, generate scenarios and recommend budget changes in seconds. But those recommendations are only as reliable as the quality of the data feeding the model. If that data is incomplete, biased or built on flawed assumptions, AI does not correct the problem. AI trained on bad data just gives you bad answers faster and more confidently.
That has shaped how I think about using AI. The technology is extraordinarily valuable for identifying patterns, exploring possibilities and accelerating decisions, but it needs to be grounded in trusted data and evidence. For marketing leaders, that means using incrementality experiments and other rigorous measurement methods to establish what is actually driving business results.
As AI increases the speed and volume of decisions, the value of trusted proof only grows. The goal should not be to automate judgment. It should be to give people better evidence on which to exercise it.”

Run Log
AI use case of the week
Fullstory AI Reveals Where Digital Experiences Break Down
Game Lounge, an iGaming affiliate that operates comparison and review sites and directs users to partner operators, often has two minutes or less to understand what a visitor does on one of its sites before that person clicks through to a partner. Because the company does not control what happens after users leave, traditional analytics gave its product team an incomplete picture of where people were getting stuck.
Lane Greer, head of customer engineering and strategic solutions at Fullstory, a behavioral data company that helps businesses understand how people use their websites and digital products, said Game Lounge used the platform to analyze visitors behavior during that limited time on its sites.
Fullstory captures interactions across Game Lounge’s webpages, while its StoryAI tools analyze that behavioral data for signs of friction. The system can surface problems such as clicks on elements that do nothing or interactions that trigger errors, helping the UX team identify where the experience needs attention.
Game Lounge reported a 20 percent faster validation time for new product updates, along with a 70 percent reduction in dead clicks and a 50 percent reduction in error clicks.
“To understand a digital experience holistically, tons of data must be collected, processed and analyzed,” Greer said.
The lesson? When a business sees only part of the customer journey, extracting more meaning from the interactions it does control becomes more valuable.
