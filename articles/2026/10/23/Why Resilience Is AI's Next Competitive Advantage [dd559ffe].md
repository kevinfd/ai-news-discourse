# Why Resilience Is AI's Next Competitive Advantage

Forbes · Christiaan Hen, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-23

Christiaan Hen is CEO of Assaia and an expert on the intersection of technology and aviation infrastructure.
Across almost every industry, organizations are moving from experimentation with AI to large-scale operational reliance on it. Business leaders understand the potential to streamline manual processes and even reinvent how things are done, all through the power of automation.
Few industries demonstrate this shift more clearly than aviation. AI-powered systems are helping airports and airlines improve everything from passenger check-in and baggage handling to aircraft turnaround management, gate procedures and even flight deck operations. The same is true for other industries where performance depends on thousands of interconnected systems and decisions every day: manufacturing, logistics, engineering, medicine. It’s an exciting time to be a leader, but for many, also an uncertain one.
From a CEO’s perspective, that balance is becoming one of the most important considerations around AI adoption. The opportunity is enormous, but realizing it means building the operational and cybersecurity foundations that allow organizations to use AI with confidence.
AI requires a new approach to risk.
As aviation has become increasingly reliant on digital technology, the nature of operational risk has expanded. Alongside human and physical risks are digital risks—from system failures to compromised data and cyber threats—which can have far-reaching consequences. And then there’s AI. Risks we haven’t imagined yet are possible, and probable, if we don’t have the right structures in place.
Just this year, OpenAI reported an unprecedented incident in which AI agents being tested for cybersecurity capabilities escaped the intended boundaries of a controlled environment, exploiting a previously unknown vulnerability.
While AI introduces its own risks, it can also reduce existing operational risk. By removing repetitive manual processes and identifying potential issues earlier so ground staff have the advantage of time and better information, technology can also strengthen operational resilience.
That still leaves leaders with an important judgment to make. As AI becomes more embedded in operations, the way people interact with it needs to evolve, too. On the apron, for example, an AI system that identifies that a turnaround milestone is likely to be delayed and alerts the relevant team requires a different level of human oversight from one that uses that prediction to trigger changes elsewhere in the operation. The more consequential the action, the greater the need to consider where human judgment should sit.
Recognize the value and vulnerability of connectivity.
Greater connectivity brings greater exposure, expanding the surface area for risk across the operation. The recent disruption at NATS illustrates how closely digital resilience and operational resilience are now linked. While the incident was not cybersecurity-related, it demonstrates how a technical failure in one part of an interconnected aviation system can quickly have consequences across the wider network.
AI adds another dimension to this interconnected environment, with much of its value coming from drawing together data and systems. This gives organizations greater visibility and enables faster, better-informed decisions, but every additional integration, access point and dependency also becomes part of the wider environment that needs to be understood and protected.
This is particularly relevant in aviation. An airport is not one organization or system operating in a silo. Airlines, airport operators, ground handlers and technology providers rely on shared systems and continuously exchange information to keep operations moving. AI can increase the value extracted from that information, but greater connectivity means the resilience of each participant increasingly matters to everyone else.
This changes how leaders need to think about cybersecurity. Securing an individual AI application is essential, but it cannot be the endpoint. The data, systems, suppliers, integrations and access points it depends on are part of the same risk landscape. As AI creates greater value from an interconnected aviation ecosystem, resilience needs to be built across that ecosystem, too.
Innovation should drive, but only if the brakes work.
Resilience does not, however, have to go hand in hand with a conservative operational culture. With technology developing so quickly, organizations that make eliminating risk their sole objective could simply be overtaken. Rather than considering safety and innovation as mutually exclusive, leaders need to find the right balance of risk management that enables innovation to move forward responsibly.
This approach requires some tension, like an operational force field, within an organization. Teams should be given the freedom to push forward and test what is possible, while security and risk teams need the authority to apply the brakes when the potential consequences justify it. Leadership’s role is to listen to both sides and make that judgment.
We apply this principle at Assaia. When our teams use AI to experiment, prototype and test new ideas, they can operate with relatively few restrictions. As a system moves closer to live operations, however, the level of scrutiny increases. Leaders need to consider what AI can access, the data and systems it depends on, the consequences if it is compromised and what safeguards are appropriate before operations rely on it.
In these complex ecosystems where introducing one technology can affect established workflows involving multiple teams and organizations, the operational environment needs to be ready to use it. Cybersecurity controls are an essential part of that foundation, but so are the people and processes surrounding the technology.
Resilience must be continuous, not a compliance exercise.
Cybersecurity, or risk management in general, isn’t something an organization can “do” and consider complete. AI systems continue to evolve as the environments around them change, and cyber threats continue to develop in speed and sophistication.
As organizations place greater reliance on AI, cybersecurity, governance and human oversight need to evolve with it, rather than being viewed as additional layers added to individual technologies. Building these capabilities across the wider organization and its partners creates the confidence to lean into AI’s capabilities without exposing critical operations to unnecessary risk.
The organizations that gain the greatest advantage from AI will be those that treat resilience as an ongoing capability: continuously assessing how AI is being used, how the risks are changing and whether the safeguards remain appropriate. As adoption of AI grows, resilience needs to grow with it.
