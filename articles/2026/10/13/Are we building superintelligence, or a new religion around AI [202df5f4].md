# Are we building superintelligence, or a new religion around AI?

Reddit · whoislucian · 2026-10-13

We usually discuss AGI in terms of technology, jobs, money and existential risk. But something else is happening: the debate about AI is starting to sound surprisingly similar to a debate about religion. There are two broad camps. Accelerate or slow down? Effective accelerationism, or e/acc, argues that technological progress should move as fast as possible. Supporters believe that slowing down AI could delay medical breakthroughs, scientific discoveries and a future of abundance. AI safety advocates argue that developing superintelligence without knowing how to control it could put humanity at risk. Both sides believe they are protecting humanity. They simply disagree about what threatens it most. Why is the Antichrist being discussed alongside AI? Billionaire Peter Thiel has discussed the Antichrist in the context of technological progress and existential risk. His interpretation presents the Antichrist as someone who promises peace and safety by stopping scientific progress and centralizing global power. In this worldview, people who want to slow down dangerous technologies could become part of the problem rather than the solution. This raises an uncomfortable question: could fear of AI catastrophe eventually be used to justify a global system of control? And could the belief that technological progress must never be stopped justify taking risks we do not fully understand? Why are AI companies talking to religious leaders? Anthropic, the company behind Claude, has consulted religious leaders, theologians and philosophers about questions that go beyond engineering. How should an AI respond to grief? How should it approach its own shutdown? Could an AI ever have moral status? Should it need consent to work, or deserve compensation? Could it have something comparable to a soul? These questions may sound strange today, but they touch on a serious problem. We still do not fully understand consciousness, and we have no universally accepted way to determine whether an AI could ever have subjective experiences. Some religious leaders argue that AI is simply mathematics and cannot be conscious. Others believe the ethical questions deserve more attention. Are we witnessing the birth of a new religion? The strange thing is that both AI optimists and AI pessimists can sometimes sound like religious believers. One side expects a technological paradise in which disease, poverty and scarcity become manageable. The other fears a technological apocalypse that could threaten humanity itself. Both imagine a superintelligence powerful enough to transform civilization. They disagree about whether the outcome will be heaven or hell. When people begin treating AI as an ultimate source of truth, assigning it moral authority or believing that it will save humanity, where does technological optimism end and faith begin? What about China and the United States? Beneath the philosophical debate lies a geopolitical race. The argument for acceleration is simple: if the United States slows down AI development, China might not. Neither side wants to give its rival a decisive advantage in science, economics or military technology. This creates a dangerous incentive. Even if everyone agrees that safety matters, each competitor may believe that slowing down unilaterally is too risky. Meanwhile, increasingly capable Chinese AI models are becoming available worldwide, and governments are struggling to balance innovation, security and regulation. The result could be a global race in which nobody wants to hit the brakes, even if nobody can confidently predict where the road leads. Why should ordinary people care? Because this is not just a debate between billionaires, researchers and politicians. AI could transform our jobs, healthcare, education and access to knowledge. Decisions made by a small number of companies and governments could shape how billions of people live. We also face a choice about dependence. Should we rely on a handful of powerful AI providers, or preserve access to different models and technologies? Who should decide what values these systems follow? And how much control should governments have over their development? We may be approaching a technological revolution so significant that even its creators do not fully understand its consequences. My question is this: If both sides believe they are saving humanity, how do we distinguish responsible caution from fear-driven control, and genuine progress from blind faith in technology? And if we eventually create an intelligence far beyond our own, will we still be the ones deciding what humanity’s future should look like?
