# I let an AI assistant shop for me and the reviewers got nothing

Fast Company · Pete Pachal · 2026-10-13

A test with Meta’s Muse shows how product recommendations can flow through AI while the sites that did the testing are cut out of the sale.
BY Pete Pachal
Pete Pachal is a journalist and the creator of Media Copilot, a newsletter and podcast that examines how AI is changing media, journalism, and the news.
There’s a new kind of AI in town, and it’s all about you. Which is to say you, personally—these are the new personal AI assistants that go by names like Muse, Dots, and Grok Bot, and they’re ready to dismantle much of the friction in your life. Need to research a trip, book a restaurant, or choose between healthcare plans? With the right context, they can handle it all for you, complete with a smile on their mascot-like avatars.
But just how does one of these assistants recommend what to buy? To find out, I asked my Muse, who I named Jones, which robot vacuum to buy. (Muse is made by Meta, which debuted it on Sept. 8) Jones responded with a surprisingly small number of recommendations—just a couple of models—with links to go make the purchase. Each of those links provided options to go to a retailer website or buy the vacuum right there in the app.
When I pressed Jones on why it had chosen those particular models, and whether it had looked at review sites specifically, it fessed up that it had looked at both retail sites and review roundups. On the latter, it leaned heavily on a site called Vacuum Wars, which does extensive hands-on testing of this category, and Jones seemed to understand that made it more authoritative. Of the other “review sites” it cited, one was a thin repackaging of Vacuum Wars data, and another was an AI-generated roundup on an aromatherapy blog. Vacuum Wars, I should point out, makes most of its money from the affiliate revenue it gets when its readers transact.
At this point I should caveat that my example is one test done in one AI app. These personal assistants are brand new, and the AI information economy is still developing. Nothing definitive should be gleaned from this experience. Nonetheless, I think what I discovered is instructive since it shows the vast distance between how product recommendations work in AI vs. the open web, and what that gap means for publishers whose business depends on them.
I think this is worth talking about, now, because I wholeheartedly believe these new apps—or some version of them—will be a success. The idea of the single, all-knowing AI with a simple “talk to me” interface has been the dream since at least the original Star Trek, where the Enterprise computer would somehow magically always know the right context to give the best and most precise answer. The AI platforms have been heading this way, with Anthropic discarding the Chat/Cowork toggle, and OpenAI’s move to sunset custom GPTs being the latest indicators. Everything is pointed toward a singular AI experience.
Back to buying my robot vacuum: Muse looking to Vacuum Wars as the most definitive review site is probably appropriate, but it also wasn’t a fair fight. Absent from its considerations were Wirecutter, PCMag, and Consumer Reports, all major review sites that have published roundups of robot vacuums. There are plausible reasons for this: Wirecutter is a New York Times property, PCMag is owned by Ziff Davis, and both are hostile to AI companies substituting their content with AI answers. The Times blocks AI crawlers across the board, and Ziff Davis is suing OpenAI. Consumer Reports is likewise defensive about its content, keeping its full ratings behind a membership. But that’s my inference. Jones never said it was blocked from anything.
I should pause for another caveat: What Jones told me it did and what it actually did aren’t necessarily the same thing. It’s possible other sites were in the mix and it never told me, or there may be guardrails in place that it’s not even aware of. So grains of generative salt all around.
