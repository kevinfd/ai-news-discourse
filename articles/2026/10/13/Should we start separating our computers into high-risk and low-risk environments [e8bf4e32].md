# Should we start separating our computers into high-risk and low-risk environments?

Reddit · onektruths · 2026-10-13

(Disclosure: I generated this summary using chatgpt, as I had a lengthy discussion with it regarding these ideas, sorry too lazy to summarise it by hand, but I did read through the paragraphs to ensure there are no major mistakes, ty) I've been thinking about this as AI agents become increasingly capable of interacting with our computers, files, accounts and eventually our physical environment. First, I'm not anti-AI. Quite the opposite. I use AI extensively and think it will bring enormous gains. However, I think there's an important distinction between trusting AI to be useful and trusting it with irreversible actions. For example, I'm perfectly happy to let an AI agent experiment with software, write code, research information or automate workflows. But giving it unrestricted access to my main computer, financial accounts or personal files is another matter. Even if an agent performs correctly 95% or 99% of the time, the remaining failures could be extremely costly. Accidentally deleting important files or transferring $50,000 to the wrong account isn't something that can necessarily be reversed. Humans make mistakes too, of course, but we retain direct control over our own actions and can intervene. With increasingly autonomous agents, that control becomes less immediate. My idea is to treat computing environments like an investment portfolio, with different levels of risk exposure. High-risk environment: A sandbox computer or virtual machine where AI agents can operate relatively freely, experiment, install software, execute code and perform complex tasks. If something goes wrong, the damage is contained. Normal environment: Everyday computing, with AI assistance but limited permissions, backups and human approval for consequential actions. Low-risk environment: A separate, preferably offline computer or collection of simple devices containing important personal records, financial documentation, essential software and an offline knowledge library. Minimal connectivity, minimal automation and no autonomous agents. I also think there's a place for deliberately retaining low-tech devices and systems. Not everything needs to be smart, connected or AI-controlled. Sometimes a simple offline device is preferable precisely because it has fewer ways to fail or be compromised. This isn't about preparing for an AI apocalypse or rejecting technological progress. It's about compartmentalisation and limiting the consequences of failure. The analogy I keep coming back to is investing. You can be extremely bullish on a high-growth asset while still keeping part of your wealth in safer assets. You don't need to put everything at risk to benefit from the upside. Similarly, I want to take full advantage of AI without exposing everything I value to the same technological risks. In fact, the more powerful AI becomes, the more I think this separation makes sense. I'm curious what others think: Will sandboxed AI environments become standard for households, not just developers and enterprises? Is maintaining a separate offline computer or low-tech backup a sensible precaution, or unnecessary complexity? Could operating systems eventually offer genuinely isolated AI workspaces by default? Are there better approaches to achieving the same balance between capability and security?
