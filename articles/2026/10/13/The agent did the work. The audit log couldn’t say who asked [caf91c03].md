# The agent did the work. The audit log couldn’t say who asked.

The New Stack · TNS Staff · 2026-10-13

The agent did the work. The audit log couldn’t say who asked.
More than 20 percent of the MCP-related access policies that Shawn Petty’s team reviewed across customer and prospect environments were broken or missing, according to a recent account published in The New Stack. In most cases, the MCP server ran on someone’s personal token rather than a service account.

The problem, though, usually shows up after an incident: That personal token puts an engineer’s name into the audit trail for everything the agent did. Meanwhile, a shared service account puts the account’s name there instead. In both cases, the audit trail isn’t able to identify who precisely asked the agent to do the work or who approved the access it used.
Access that goes through a request, approval, and grant in the identity provider is provisioned, and the identity provider records it. Access that an agent picks up along the way is emergent: A role inherited from the service that launched it, or a developer’s own token lent out to finish a task. The identity provider holds no record of those grants, so when an agent touches something it shouldn’t, you can’t trace it back.
In this live conversation, the CloudBolt team will explain how agents end up with access nobody approved, then show how to trace that access back to a person with CloudBolt: linking agent activity to your existing identity provider, setting limits on what an agent can touch, and reading the audit trail that results.
