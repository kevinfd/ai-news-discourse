# ChatGPT English Conversation Day 65 | Do people exchange business cards overseas?

ChatGPT English Conversation Day 65 | Do people exchange business cards overseas?
Today's ChatGPT English conversation is Scene 065, "Visiting a Client ② Reception at a Client's Office."
This time, the practice involves visiting a client with an appointment and conveying my name, company name, and reason for the visit to the receptionist.
I worked on STEP 1 key phrase practice, STEP 2 model conversation reading, and STEP 3 free role-play.
Today, I was particularly curious about the custom of exchanging business cards.
It's done as a matter of course in Japan, but what is it like overseas?
Speaking English at a client's reception
In STEP 3, I did a free role-play where I was the visitor and Chappy-sensei was the receptionist.
First, I said, 
Good morning. I’m here to see Mr. James Lee.
"Good morning. I am here to see Mr. James Lee."
I conveyed that.
Since the receptionist asked for my name and company name, I replied, 
My name is Ken Yamada from ABC.
"I am Ken Yamada from ABC."
I also explained that I had an appointment at 1:00 PM regarding a new proposal.
Visiting a client is something I'm used to in Japanese.
However, when it comes to English, just practicing the sequence of stating my name, company name, appointment time, and reason for the visit is good practice.
I passed this STEP 3.
Politely asking about wait times
At reception, you can't always assume the person will come out immediately.
That's when I practiced the expression, 
May I ask how long the wait might be?
"May I ask how long the wait might be?"
This is a polite way to ask.
"May I ask" means "May I inquire."
"how long the wait might be" means "how long the wait is likely to be."
I practiced the pronunciation of smoothly connecting "May I ask" directly into "how long."
Even if you understand the meaning of the words, it is difficult to speak them naturally as a sentence in English.
I still need more practice.
Handing over a business card at reception?
In this role-play, I used another expression:
If it helps, here’s my business card.
"If it helps, here is my business card."
Hmm?
At this point, I had a slight question.
Do people exchange business cards overseas, too?
In Japan, exchanging business cards with a new client is a very common business etiquette.
You visit, greet them, and present your business card.
When working, this flow becomes naturally ingrained.
But what is it like overseas?
In the first place, if I ever end up working overseas, will I even have business cards?
I was curious, so I asked Chappy-sensei.
It seems that exchanging business cards is not mandatory in the United States.
According to Chappy-sensei, business cards are used overseas as well.
In English, they are called "business cards."
However, customs differ depending on the country.
For example, in the United States, it seems it is often not a formal etiquette to always exchange business cards upon a first meeting like in Japan.
You hand over a business card or exchange contact information when it is necessary for work.
It seems that just not having a business card is not considered rude in itself.
Oh, I see.
In Japan, there is a strong sense that you must exchange business cards first, so this was a bit refreshing.
Of course, even when we say "overseas," customs vary by country and company.
However, I thought the expression that appeared in the English example sentence,
If it helps, here’s my business card.
made a lot of sense.
Rather than "Let's exchange business cards," it feels more like,
"If you need it, here you go."
It might be useful for the receptionist to convey my arrival to the person in charge or to confirm my name and contact information.
Thinking of it that way, I think this expression is natural.
When doing English conversation, I imagine myself working overseas.
This lesson's material was about English conversation at a client's reception.
Conveying my name and company name, explaining the appointment time, and confirming the wait time.
These are all situations that are likely to happen at work.
But when I'm actually practicing, I become curious not just about the English phrasing, but also about overseas business customs.
What is taken for granted in Japan is not necessarily the same overseas.
If I were to work overseas, how would I visit a client?
Would I have business cards?
I even started thinking about things like that.
Becoming able to speak English might not just be about memorizing words and grammar.
It is also interesting to learn how people work in the places where that language is used.
