# The Grace Hopper Celebration was more than a conference. Its cancellation should be a wake-up call

Fast Company · Susan Gouijnstook · 2026-10-26

This week, thousands of women and nonbinary technologists were supposed to walk into the Grace Hopper Celebration in Anaheim, California. Instead, the doors are closed. The tech industry named some of its most powerful AI chips after Grace Hopper. This year, it could not sustain the conference that bears her name.
AnitaB.org canceled this year’s celebration after individual registrations and sponsorship commitments fell short of what it needed to run the event. The celebration was first held in 1994, co-founded by computing pioneer Anita Borg. Last year, it drew nearly 15,000 attendees from more than 60 countries. The loss is bigger than a few days on the technology industry’s calendar. The Grace Hopper Celebration was not merely a conference or a symbolic celebration of women’s contributions. It was part of the industry’s career infrastructure, and when budgets tighten, convenings are often the first to go.
Confidence and information
For students and early-career technologists, that infrastructure matters. Research from Girls Who Code and the Burning Glass Institute, set to be released next month, shows the entry-level door for software engineers has narrowed. From early 2022 to early 2026, the share of software-developer postings open to people with two years of experience or less fell from 19.3% to 14.1%, a 27% decline. This makes a conversation with a recruiter or mentor, or having a community of peers, more important than ever. Those people can give someone the confidence and information to remain in a field where they are underrepresented.
At Girls Who Code, we see and hear stories like this often among our 425,000 alumni. Technical skills are essential, but skills alone don’t create careers. Young people also need someone to open doors, make introductions, and help them imagine where they belong.
Those doors are becoming harder to find as artificial intelligence reshapes the technology workforce. PwC’s “2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer,” which analyzed 2.4 million U.S. entry-level job postings, found that the roles most exposed to AI are now seven times more likely to require traditionally senior skills like leadership and judgment. We are asking young people to arrive with experience no one has given them the chance to build.
More barriers than doors
Women made up 37% of computer scientists in 1995; today, it’s about a quarter. The next generation is ready, though. When Girls Who Code surveyed 2,328 teens for our “Agency in the Age of AI” study, we found that girls aren’t disengaged from AI. They’re discerning about it, and they use it more when they feel ownership over how they use it. The same study found that by around age 17, girls are losing confidence that they can make a difference in AI. It’s easy to see why, when they see more barriers than doors.
The Grace Hopper Celebration is one piece of a broader ecosystem that supports young women’s professional development in tech. Its loss this year signals a need to reexamine how people enter the technology workforce. The technology industry has an opportunity to rethink how it supports the next generation of talent by reducing barriers to entry, creating more accessible pathways into emerging fields like AI and cybersecurity, and expanding access to career opportunities. By investing in young talent and strengthening connections among employers, educators, and communities, we can help ensure more young people can build meaningful careers in technology. These actions can create more durable, accessible pathways into technology that remain open year-round and have a wide reach.
