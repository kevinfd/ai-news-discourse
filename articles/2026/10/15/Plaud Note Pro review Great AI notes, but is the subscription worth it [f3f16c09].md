# Plaud Note Pro review: Great AI notes, but is the subscription worth it?

The Hindu · John Xavier · 2026-10-15

I’ve been using the Plaud Note Pro for several weeks now, and honestly, it’s a pretty slick little gadget — even if I’m not convinced I need everything it does.
The Plaud Note Pro is a thin, card-shaped device that can be attached to the back of your phone. It can record phone calls, and in-person meetings. The audio feeds are almost instantaneously transmitted to a companion app that generates transcripts and summaries.
The audio quality is decent enough to generate an error-free transcript. Its four-microphone array does a good job of cutting through background noise. The little AMOLED screen turns up every time you record and also gives a heads up the battery level and transcription transfer status. It also lets you highlight specific parts in the conversation that you think are more important.
These all look good and the user interface is seamless, but broader question is: how much of what Plaud does can I already do with my smartphone and the AI tools I can access?
The smartphone voice memo alternative
For instance, the voice memo feature or application on the smartphone is now capable of giving you a transcript. And you can take it and paste it into your ChatGPT, Claude, or open-source AI tools, and ask it to summarise, pick action points, and organise the conversation into a useful document or deck.
This workflow is not as smooth as in Plaud. Depending on recording and the AI tool used, there could be more steps to get what you were really looking for. Plaud gives a frictionless workflow with a structured and useful report, especially when you want meeting notes, surface key points, items, and pick follow-up actions without having to design a prompt every time. These features are helpful for someone who records a lot.
But for someone who records only occasionally, and already has access to an AI assistant, the marginal cost of producing a useful summary can be very low, depending on the existing subscriptions and usage limits.
The subscription question
Plaud does offer a free allowance of 300 transcription minutes every month. For light users, that may be enough to get started without paying an additional monthly fee for transcription.
The paid plans, however, change the economics. The Pro plan offers 1,200 transcription minutes a month for $17, or about ₹1,650 a month. The Unlimited plan costs nearly $30 a month, which works out to roughly ₹2,910.
Someone recording a few short conversations each month may find the 300-minute allowance sufficient. But a professional who records several hours of meetings every week could exhaust that allowance quickly, making the Pro plan a recurring expense.
Those who record extensively may have to consider the Unlimited plan, pushing the ongoing cost closer to ₹3,000 a month.
Paying upfront for a well-made piece of hardware is reasonable. What is harder to swallow is having to pay an ongoing fee for access to much of the transcription and AI functionality that gives the device its value.
The ability to retrieve information from past conversations and turn discussions into actionable notes may be worth paying for.
So, where does Plaud fit in?
The Plaud Note Pro is a well-designed product, but its relevance depends on how frequently I need it and how much friction I want to remove from the process.
For a regular user who spends the day in meetings, conducts interviews, or regularly records calls and in-person discussions, it makes a strong case. But for someone who wants a reliable transcription and summary service without buying another device, there are other serious alternatives that offer an app-based recording and transcription service that is more practical for those who move between devices.
There is also the physical-device question. Even though the Plaud Note Pro is compact, it is still one more thing to remember to charge, attach and carry. Part of me wonders when phones will simply offer an equally seamless experience natively.
Ultimately, Plaud’s challenge is not convincing people that AI-generated meeting notes are useful. That has already been established. Its challenge is convincing them that a dedicated recorder and its accompanying subscription are the best way to get those notes.
