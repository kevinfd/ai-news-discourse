# The World’s New Agronomists are AI Agents

Newsweek · Eileen Falkenberg-Hull, Katie Tincello · 2026-10-15

Artificial intelligence in agriculture is nothing new, but more advanced technologies are now giving rise to a new generation of AI agents reshaping how food and crops are grown.
Agriculture technology has moved beyond dashboards and data collection into systems that can interpret conditions and act on them: adjusting irrigation, flagging disease and even guiding equipment, with less human input at each step. Some deployments have already lifted crop yields by as much as 40 percent while cutting water use by up to 30 percent.
From Dashboards to Decisions
For years, "digital agronomy" meant gathering data, such as soil readings, satellite imagery and weather forecasts, which farmers would then act on. AI agents work differently.
“Earlier precision-ag tools collected, visualized or analyzed individual data streams, leaving the farmer to integrate the information and make the final decision.” Nipuna Chamara, a research assistant professor at the University of Nebraska-Lincoln, told Newsweek. “An AI agent can synthesize agronomic, soil, weather, imagery, machinery and economic data, evaluate alternative scenarios, and translate them into an actionable management recommendation such as when and how much to irrigate based on both crop need and profitability.”
These tools represent a move towards "autonomous agronomy," where AI agents can actively manage crop and soil health with little human involvement. The global market for agricultural AI is projected to grow between 24 and 26 percent annually through 2030, as research in this area shows positive impact for farmers.
“A single field can have substantial variation in soil properties, topography, and microclimatic conditions, all of which can influence crop growth and yield.” Jasmine Neupane, an assistant professor of agricultural systems technology at the University of Missouri, told Newsweek. “In my research, we used AI models to analyze yield, soil, and topographic data across 12 site-years, helping us identify these patterns and develop planting-rate recommendations tailored to different parts of the same field.”
The benefits of real-world application are already visible. John Deere's See & Spray Gen 2 system treated more than 5 million acres in 2025, cutting an estimated 31 million gallons of herbicide mix.
Closing the Adoption Gap
Despite these advances, the adoption of core precision-agriculture tools remains below 25 percent, and this is concentrated amongst larger farms. Midsize farms lagged behind and small family farms recorded the lowest adoption rate, according to the U.S. Department of Agriculture's (USDA) Economic Research Service.
“We are moving forward rapidly, but connectivity, integration of data formats and data accessibility are now the main bottlenecks to full integration of AI across the farming landscape.” Ignacio Ciampitti, professor of agronomy at Purdue University, told Newsweek. “From the operational side, a few tasks, such as irrigation, can be fully operated in an automated approach, but whole farming autonomy is not here yet.”
Faced with the growing climate crisis, technology that improves efficiency, precision, and crop yield is paramount. The rising age of farmers, now averaging 58 years old, and labor shortages also add urgency to the demand.
A Partner, Not a Replacement
The USDA's fiscal 2025–2026 AI strategy treats this shift as infrastructure work, prioritizing governance and workforce readiness alongside the technology itself. Researchers are quick to note, however, that AI agents are meant to work alongside farmers, not instead of them.
“Farmers have decades of practical knowledge about their fields, and that experience is invaluable. I see AI as another tool in the farmer’s toolbox that can augment, rather than replace, that experience.” Neupane added.
Ciampitti echoed her comments, adding that support in research, education and workforce preparation will be critical to successful adoption. The direction is clear: autonomous agronomy is growing, but there is still work to be done before it becomes an effective and widespread tool across agriculture.
