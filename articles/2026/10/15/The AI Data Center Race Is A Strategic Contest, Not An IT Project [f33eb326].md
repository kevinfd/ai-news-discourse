# The AI Data Center Race Is A Strategic Contest, Not An IT Project

Forbes · Dennis-Kenji Kipker, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-15

Prof. Dr. Dennis-Kenji Kipker is a cybersecurity expert and works as Scientific Director of the cyberintelligence.institute.
When I sit down with executive teams to discuss their AI roadmaps, the conversation used to begin with models and software. Today, it starts with megawatts, because training and running advanced AI systems depend on physical infrastructure that takes years to plan and billions to finance.
Leaders who still treat data centers as an IT budget line item are misreading the game. The U.S., Europe and China each face a different physical bottleneck, and comparing them shows executives where to build and what to secure first.
America leads because capital moves fast.
The U.S. entered this race with a head start and is spending as if it intends to keep it. Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta plan to invest roughly $725 billion in capital expenditures this year, which is about 77% more than the record $410 billion of 2025. Much of it flows into AI data centers, chips and power.
That money pulls the entire supply chain along, from chip fabrication to transformer manufacturing. Speed comes at a price, however. Nearly half of American data center capacity sits in just five regional clusters, according to the International Energy Agency (IEA), which expects data centers to drive almost half of U.S. electricity demand growth through 2030.
While the American model is strong on capital and ambition, it is increasingly limited by grids, permits and local tolerance, and utilities in several states have already had to ration new connections while they wait for generation capacity to catch up with demand.
For business leaders, site selection starts with power access. Securing grid capacity early beats queuing for connections later.
Europe bets on sovereignty.
Europe cannot match American spending volumes, so it is pulling a different lever.
On June 3, 2026, the European Commission presented its Tech Sovereignty Package, which spans the entire technology stack from chips and infrastructure to software, cloud and AI. The package is explicitly framed as an answer to Europe’s dependence on non-European suppliers.
The aim is to triple EU data center capacity within five to seven years and to strengthen the semiconductor base underneath.
This is part of a broader push toward a “European way“ to technological sovereignty, one that also touches open-source software and the digitalization of the energy grid.
The ambition is right, but my own experience with data center projects in Europe suggests that the binding constraint is rarely capital or demand. Far more often, it is the years lost waiting for a grid connection or a building permit, and no sovereignty label can compensate for a site that cannot be energized on schedule.
The real test for Brussels will be whether the new rules shorten approval times rather than add compliance layers. A sovereignty framework that keeps investors waiting will simply push the next generation of capacity to friendlier jurisdictions. For business leaders, permit and grid-connection lead times belong in the roadmap from day one, so early talks with authorities pay off.
China plans in five-year cycles.
China’s 15th Five-Year Plan, which covers 2026 to 2030, responds to American export controls on advanced semiconductors by focusing on how efficiently existing hardware is used.
The plan continues the buildout of a national computing power network that routes workloads to underused data centers, and it aims to stop local officials from wasting capital on projects that ignore grid capacity.
The contrast with the American approach, which relies more on deregulation and private investment, could hardly be sharper. Where Washington bets on markets to sort out winners, Beijing bets on coordination, pairing its computing buildout with a parallel plan to route data center demand toward regions rich in wind, solar and hydropower.
From what I have observed, scarcity has turned utilization into a discipline for Chinese operators, unlike Western companies with easy access to chips. Efficiency, after all, is also a form of capacity. For business leaders, the lesson is to optimize utilization before adding capacity. A five-year horizon also favors multi-year capacity planning over annual budgets.
Power, water and public trust set the limits.
Wherever the racks stand, the physical limits look similar. In the report cited above, the IEA projects that global data center electricity consumption will more than double to about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, which is slightly more than Japan consumes today.
Water is another constraint, since cooling draws heavily on local supplies in many climates, and communities that see their aquifers or power bills come under pressure will not stay silent for long, whether they live in Arizona, Ireland or Guizhou province.
I have watched a well-funded project stall for more than a year because nobody had given residents a convincing answer about water use, noise and grid impact. Public acceptance and ecological credibility are not soft factors that can be handled at the end of a project, but conditions that decide whether it gets built at all.
The Bottom Line
The race for AI data centers will not be won by whoever pours the most concrete, but by those who secure reliable energy and sustainable water supplies, earn public acceptance, stay ecologically defensible and protect their access to semiconductors and hardware through resilient global supply chains.
Building a data center is no longer an IT decision but a strategic one, and it belongs on the agenda of every board and every cabinet table.
