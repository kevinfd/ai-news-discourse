# Trump Used Grok on Venezuela; DOJ Then Called Presidential Records Act Unconstitutional

Tech Times · Shannon Harwood · 2026-10-29

When President Trump queried Grok — the AI chatbot built by Elon Musk's company xAI — about Venezuela before U.S. forces captured Nicolás Maduro on January 3, 2026, those queries likely went unpreserved. No law required anyone to save them. No regulation specified that AI chatbot consultations in the Oval Office constitute official government records. And the administration Trump leads has since moved to challenge the Presidential Records Act, the one statute that might have applied.
That legal void is now the focus of a formal congressional demand. House Judiciary Committee Ranking Member Rep. Jamie Raskin (D-Md.) and House Oversight Committee Ranking Member Rep. Robert Garcia (D-Calif.) sent a letter to the White House on Wednesday, October 28, 2026, demanding that the Trump administration preserve all AI chatbot query logs and outputs under the Presidential Records Act. The letter gives the White House until November 11, 2026 to provide a briefing on current record-keeping practices — or explain why no such practices cover Trump's documented Grok queries about the Maduro operation.
The Venezuela episode anchors the letter, but it is not an isolated case. The same Grok system — in a classified-network variant called Grok Gov Model — was integrated into the Pentagon's Maven Smart System during Operation Epic Fury in Iran, the February 28, 2026 U.S. strikes on Iranian nuclear infrastructure. AI chatbot queries were present at both the planning stage of a foreign-policy operation and the execution stage of a military one. In neither case does a clear statutory framework exist for whether the resulting outputs must be preserved, disclosed, or archived.
Four Demands, One Deadline
The Raskin-Garcia letter makes four specific demands: the White House must preserve all AI chatbot query logs, outputs, and model-response records; confirm whether AI chatbot systems are being used in deliberative presidential decision-making; identify which systems are in use and by whom; and appear for a briefing by November 11 to explain current records practices. The deadline gives the White House less than two weeks to respond.
The demands land in familiar legal territory for both lawmakers. Raskin leads the House Judiciary Committee's oversight of AI policy and has probed secret AI data center deals involving several major AI firms since September 2026. Garcia sent a Federal Records Act preservation letter to 40 federal agencies in August 2026 specifically demanding that agencies preserve digital communications, including Signal messages, from officials including those at the Department of Government Efficiency. That letter, like most records demands from the Democratic minority, received no formal response from most agencies.
Both lawmakers have no subpoena power as minority members. The letter is a pressure instrument, not a legal mandate. The administration has previously described Raskin's records demands as "pathetic" through a White House spokesperson. There is no indication the current letter will receive a different response.
Grok Advised Trump on Venezuela Before Maduro Capture, TIME Report Found
According to a TIME magazine investigation — independently flagged as a documented AI hazard by the OECD AI Incidents Monitor — Trump consulted Grok about Venezuela in approximately December 2025, weeks before U.S. forces captured Maduro. The specific queries Trump submitted and the outputs Grok provided are unknown. Whether those outputs were treated as advisory intelligence, conversation, or something in between is also unknown. What is known is that Maduro was captured January 3, 2026, that Grok is a product of xAI, a company founded and controlled by Elon Musk, and that Musk was simultaneously serving as an informal White House adviser and head of the Department of Government Efficiency at the time.
The OECD's AI Incidents Monitor — which tracks real-world AI safety failures — catalogued the Venezuela episode specifically because an AI chatbot product from a company with direct financial ties to the White House was used as a policy-advisory tool in a national security decision, with no disclosed conflict-of-interest review and no records preservation framework. Reid Hoffman, a technology investor who has publicly scrutinized the Musk-Trump AI relationship, described the overlap as "a serious conflict of interest with few precedents in the history of AI development."
The conflict does not end with the Venezuela consultation. In February 2026, the Pentagon finalized a contract with xAI to deploy Grok Gov Model — a classified-network variant of Grok — into the Maven Smart System, the Defense Department's primary AI targeting platform. Four days after the contract announcement, a senior Pentagon official overseeing AI acquisitions sold xAI stock for profit, with estimated gains of up to 4,800 percent. The official held a divestiture certificate from the Office of Government Ethics, which covered forward-looking conflicts but did not, apparently, prevent the sale from occurring in the immediate aftermath of the contract's public announcement.
Does the Presidential Records Act Cover AI Chatbot Queries?
The Presidential Records Act was signed into law on November 4, 1978 — five years after the Saturday Night Massacre and three years after Nixon's departure from the White House — as a direct legislative response to a president claiming personal ownership of official White House records. Its core definition covers documentary materials "created or received by the President, the President's immediate staff, or a unit or individual of the Executive Office of the President" in the course of conducting official duties.
The phrase "documentary materials" was written 44 years before large language models existed. It was written for paper, for tape recordings, for typed memoranda, and for telegrams. It has been extended by executive order and litigation to cover email (2014 amendment), social media direct messages (contested), and end-to-end encrypted communications (partially contested). It has never been extended — by statute, by rule, or by court order — to cover AI chatbot query logs, prompt inputs, or model outputs.
Whether a presidential query to Grok constitutes a "documentary material created by the President in the conduct of official duties" is a legal question no court or agency has answered. Legal scholars and archivists have noted that the PRA's language is broad enough to plausibly cover such records — if the queries were made in the context of official business — but that no enforcement mechanism currently exists to compel their preservation.
The gap is not theoretical. The Grok Gov Model's classified-network deployment means that some query logs may reside on Pentagon servers rather than in White House custody, creating a records chain-of-custody problem the 1978 law cannot address. Records the White House does not possess cannot be transferred to the National Archives and Records Administration on departure from office. They are, effectively, in a legal void.
The Timing Problem: PRA Attack Came After Grok Entered the Policy Loop
On April 1, 2026, the Trump administration's Office of Legal Counsel issued a formal opinion authored by Assistant Attorney General T. Elliott Gaiser concluding that the Presidential Records Act "intrudes impermissibly on executive authority" and is therefore unconstitutional. The opinion did not immediately dissolve the PRA — federal law does not work that way — but it signaled to federal agencies that the administration considered itself under no legal obligation to comply.
The OLC opinion came after Trump had already been documented consulting Grok about Venezuela. It came after the Pentagon had finalized the xAI Grok Gov Model contract. It came as congressional Democrats were beginning to raise questions about AI records in government more broadly. The sequencing is not proof of intent — OLC opinions can take months to develop, and the administration's broad constitutional objection to the PRA predates the specific AI records question — but it means that the administration's legal attack on the one records law that could apply to AI chatbot queries landed at a moment when preserving those queries had become consequential.
On May 20, 2026, U.S. District Judge John D. Bates issued a preliminary injunction finding the PRA likely constitutional and ordering the administration to comply. The administration appealed. The appeal is pending. Neither the OLC opinion nor the Bates injunction addressed the specific question of AI chatbot records — because no one has yet litigated that question.
Anthropic Was Sidelined While Musk's AI Got the Contract
The conflict-of-interest architecture around Grok's White House role became clearer in spring 2026, when the Pentagon's treatment of xAI's competitor revealed what was at stake. Anthropic, maker of the Claude AI system, had been working with Palantir on a Pentagon AI integration when the Defense Department labeled Anthropic a supply-chain risk in approximately March 2026. The designation came after Anthropic declined to permit its models to be used for autonomous weapons targeting and mass surveillance — uses explicitly barred by Anthropic's published acceptable-use policy and by the company's interpretation of its own safety commitments.
Anthropic then sued Trump's administration over the supply-chain-risk designation, with litigation ongoing. The effective result of the designation was that the AI company that declined to operate without safety constraints was excluded from Pentagon contracts, while the AI company owned by the president's top informal adviser received them.
A senior White House science official acknowledged in Senate testimony that Grok's outputs — including responses that included antisemitic content — directly conflicted with the administration's own executive order requiring AI systems to be "true-seeking." No remediation or contract review followed that acknowledgment.
Government's Own AI Chatbot Was Modified to Align With Trump's Claims
The administration's AI record-keeping practices extend beyond national security. The America.gov AI chatbot — a government-operated tool launched September 29, 2026 to provide Americans with information about U.S. policy — was modified on its launch day after it initially responded to questions using official government records in ways that contradicted positions publicly stated by President Trump.
Among the responses that prompted the modifications: the chatbot initially confirmed, using official State Department records, that Joe Biden had been elected president in 2020. Within hours of launch, the system's response set was modified. The episode illustrates a related records dimension that the Raskin-Garcia letter does not directly address: not only are AI chatbot outputs generated by the White House potentially unpreserved — AI chatbot outputs deployed by the government to inform the public may be modified in real time to align with political claims rather than official records.
Garcia has co-sponsored H.Res. 286 on DOGE AI, a resolution demanding records of AI training and deployment practices at the Department of Government Efficiency. Both lawmakers have also conducted AI oversight at a broader level — Raskin addressed the House Judiciary Committee at its September 18, 2025 hearing on "AI at a Crossroads: A Nationwide Strategy or Californication?" examining the federal government's approach to AI regulation.
Neither has the subpoena authority to compel the records they are demanding. The November 11 deadline is a political deadline, not a legal one.
What Lawmakers Can and Cannot Force
The minority position that Raskin and Garcia occupy determines what the November 11 deadline can realistically accomplish. In the current 119th Congress, House Democrats lack committee chairmanships and the subpoena authority those chairmanships confer. Their oversight letters and deadline demands are public record — meaning they establish a documented request-and-non-response timeline that could become significant if political control of the House shifts — but they cannot compel production of a single document.
The American Historical Association and American Oversight have brought litigation challenging the administration's OLC opinion on the PRA's constitutionality. Judge Bates's preliminary injunction found the law likely constitutional, but the appeal means that question remains live. In that litigation, the specific question of whether AI chatbot query logs constitute "documentary materials" under the PRA has not yet been raised — because the briefing deadline from the Raskin-Garcia letter falls after the filing windows for the current phase of the appeal.
The November 11, 2026 deadline gives the White House roughly two weeks to respond to the demands. Based on the administration's documented response to prior records demands from the same lawmakers, a response is not expected.
Frequently Asked Questions
Does the Presidential Records Act actually cover AI chatbot queries like Trump's Grok consultations?
No law or court ruling currently answers that question definitively. The PRA's language — covering "documentary materials created or received by the President in the conduct of official duties" — was written in 1978, 44 years before large language models existed. Legal scholars argue the broad language could plausibly cover AI chatbot queries made in the course of official business, but no enforcement mechanism has been applied, no court has ruled on the question, and the Trump administration's DOJ OLC opinion declaring the PRA itself unconstitutional further complicates the picture. The gap in the law is the central issue the Raskin-Garcia letter is designed to expose.
What is Grok, and who owns it — and why does that matter here?
Grok is an AI chatbot and large language model developed by xAI, a company founded and controlled by Elon Musk. During the period when Trump reportedly consulted Grok about Venezuela foreign policy, Musk was simultaneously serving as the informal head of the Department of Government Efficiency and as one of Trump's most prominent political allies. xAI subsequently received a Pentagon contract for Grok Gov Model — a classified-network variant deployed into the Pentagon's Maven Smart System targeting platform. The overlap — a president consulting an AI product owned by his top adviser, and that adviser's company then receiving government AI contracts — is what the OECD AI Incidents Monitor flagged as a hazard and what Reid Hoffman described as "a serious conflict of interest with few precedents in the history of AI development."
Could the OLC opinion declaring the PRA unconstitutional have been motivated by the need to suppress AI chatbot records?
The DOJ OLC opinion was issued April 1, 2026 — after Trump had already been documented consulting Grok about Venezuela and after the Pentagon had finalized its contract with xAI. The sequencing does not prove intent: OLC opinions develop over months and the administration's broad constitutional objection to the PRA predates the specific AI records question. But the timing means that the administration's legal attack on the one records law that could apply to AI chatbot queries landed at precisely the moment when preserving those queries had become consequential to accountability. The Raskin-Garcia letter explicitly raises this sequencing concern.
What happens if the White House ignores the November 11 deadline?
Almost certainly, nothing immediate. Raskin and Garcia are minority-party lawmakers with no subpoena power in the current Congress. Their letters establish a documented public record of the request and the non-response, which becomes politically and legally significant if House control changes — but cannot compel production of any document on its own. The more consequential legal pressure comes from the ongoing litigation over the PRA's constitutionality in federal court, where Judge Bates's preliminary injunction found the law likely constitutional and ordered compliance. That appeal, not the November 11 deadline, is the live legal mechanism.
