# Oracle AI World 26 - building data centers? Look, it's complicated, says Oracle co-CEO Clay Magouyrk, but it's under control

diginomica · Stuart Lauchlan · 2026-10-29

Oracle AI World 26 - building data centers? Look, it's complicated, says Oracle co-CEO Clay Magouyrk, but it's under control
- Summary:
- CapEx looks like reaching a point where it's capped, but for now Magouyrk has a few myths to dispel.
For some time now Oracle has been the subject of some scuttlebutt about whether it’s over-stretched in terms of its borrowing on the back of its infrastructure expansion commitments and its part on what some commentators see as a closed loop of financial dependency centered around a handful of vendors.
Of course, fears of an East India Company style AI bubble bursting are nothing new and hardly restricted to Oracle. Consultancy ISG warned of the dangers of circular funding earlier this month when it noted:
The cloud providers are investing in the model companies, the model companies are committing significant portions of that capital back to the cloud infrastructure, and those commitments are showing up in the backlog and future demand.
But the reality for Oracle - and others - is that demand has outstripped supply for a long time, even before the AI hype cycle took off, and the firm needs to expand its infrastructure footprint if it is to be able to close the gap. That, of course, leads to rising Capital Expenditure (CapEx) costs which in turn makes the short termists on Wall Street howl and hit out.
Oracle has seen its stock fall by around 50% over the past calendar year in large part due to that its rising CapEx commitments. But firm had a good day on Wall Street this week after CFO Hilary Maxson intimated that spending will peak this fiscal year and next, stating that the plans now are to raise no more than $20 billion in additional funding through fiscal 2030.
At its AI World conference in Las Vegas, the company also made a number of announcements around infrastructure expansion, including a boost to its partnership with NVIDIA which will see Oracle deploy the latest Vera Rubin AI server systems at a scale measured in multiple gigawatts. Meanwhile it also intends to offer Google’s Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) through Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI) for the first time, expanding its supplier base in the process.
Clearing up mis-understanding
So, all good news for calming the febrile nerves of the red suspender brigade on Wall Street. But there are still challenges to be met, not least in the form of rumors of delays and problems with existing data center projects. Oracle has faced delays accessing power at a site in Wisconsin and has experienced pushback from the local community around a project in Michigan.
Meanwhile in a move that did nothing to dispel scuttlebutt of problems, last month, Oracle served Blue Owl subsidiary Stack Infrastructure, developer of a 1,400-acre campus in Doña Ana County, New Mexico, a protective force majeure notice which would delay payment obligations should the project get derailed or not come online in 2028 as planned.
It’s that sort of speculation that Oracle co-CEO Clay Magouyrk hopes to calm when he says there’s some mis-understanding out there in the market and this spreads and becomes gospel truth:
Then what are just kind of normal courses of action suddenly become front-page news. Sometimes it feels like you wake up and there's like, ‘Actors showed up to the set today, and they had to wait until the lighting was right before they could record their lines again’. That's how movies are made! Building large construction projects of this scope is complicated, and I think anyone that goes into those projects without assuming that they're going to be complicated is sadly mistaken.
It’s important to separate out the marketing from reality, he argues:
People will read the press releases, and they seem to ignore this really important term called ‘up to’. Up to is a very important concept. It means irrelevant. I could be up to 100 ft tall. I am not, but I could be up to.
The complexity extends to questions of cost, he suggests, with the actual breakdown of spend being multi-layered:
In terms of the actual technical piece, there's what we put inside of the data centers themselves, but there's also the data center components. I think that people misunderstand that if you look at the total capital that goes into a completed data center, the building itself does cost money, but it is not as significant. You take out the racks and you take out the network gear, you take out all the fiber optics, and you take out all the cabling, because all of that you can put somewhere else. You take out all the chillers because you could move those. You could take out all the UPSs because you can re-locate those.
Suddenly what you find is that as a percentage of the total site expense, yes, you have to do a bunch of grading, and there is specific, if you're hooking into the grid, certain substation transformer work and certain things. In terms of the overall percentage of the project, it's very small. Sometimes it's a little bit like Bob Ross would say, you have happy little accidents. You may have something that's delayed over here, but you can use those components to accelerate a different project over here.
So, it’s all a moving feast in many ways:
You have a complex apparatus that you're hyper-optimizing all the time. Look, do I think that it requires a huge amount of effort and focus every day for us to wake up and focus on it and to make them work? Yes. That is our job. That's why people trust us to deliver capacity for them...You see the predictions that we've made in terms of revenue and capacity and the success that we're having about hitting the deliverables that we're talking about.
People keep asking, ‘Well, are you confident that you can plug in this stuff?. It's like, ‘Yes’, and you can see it. We show you the videos of the sites. We show you the revenue that's associated with the customers paying for it. We show you the profitability that comes directly and operating income and how that flows through to EPS each quarter.
This is not the future. When we said we delivered 850 MW last quarter, it doesn't take many quarters to get to That's a lot. It's not some abstract thing in the future. This is happening right now every day.
Constraints, but...
That’s not to say there aren’t inevitable constraints, he adds:
If you actually understand our industry, there are some built-in self-governors. There is only so much memory in the world. There is only so much wafer fab. I think everybody who spends time thinking about how much power is coming online is missing the point. I think you can actually look at how much memory is being produced and how much fabrication capacity is, and that is a much clearer sample of how much you could put in, because we do not need power without chips. That is a much smaller set of things that you have to analyze.
The power debate is something that clearly frustrates Magouyrk:
I do not understand why people get caught up in [it]. I have got people emailing me that they have got a farm in Iowa that I could put up to 300 GW on. If you add that to your number, then it is 340 GW just next year. But it does not mean that I could actually get any computers to put into it. What I can tell you is that any smart provider - and to be fair, I think, again ignoring the marketing, a lot of the people who are making most of these decisions are actually quite intelligent - if they are not building data centers, they do not [have to buy] network fiber optic cables, transceivers. You do not go out and put all this money into mechanical, electrical, plumbing if you do not have the stuff to go with it. Your job is to make match sets.
OK, so that’s the supply side complexity. What about the demand side? Again Magouyrk thinks there is mis-understanding on show:
I do not think people can understand yet the rate at which compute is going to be needed to fund the AI demand. A year ago, using AI for software development was an idea, but it was not rocking. It is there now. I think every year you are going to see significant new industries that are turned on [to AI]... I just do not think people understand the breadth of demand that is out there.
For his part, Magouyrk is phlegmatic:
People are like, ‘But what if somehow we are able to build just so many more GPUs than there is demand for?’. I just do not see that. Not for the timeline that I feel like I can confidently have some perspective on over the next three to five years. I do not see it happening.
His expectation is that infrastructure rollout will be a mixed bag in terms of the scale of the projects embarked upon:
I think the way to think about it is that large sites are great, except for all the downsides of large sites. In other words, I actually think that the gigawatt scale campuses make a lot of sense for training workloads, because you need to be able to harness all of that capacity in one area. But as you see inferencing get larger and larger and larger, inferencing is not going to be 2MW sites everywhere, but we're delivering a lot of 50MW, 100MW, 200MW sites.
I think you'll continue to see a mixture because the customer demand is a mixture. You have training, but you also have lots of inferencing demand. There's that. Then in terms of the customers, we have 1,000+ AI customers on the platform. We see it from all angles. It's the big model providers that you think about, but it's also this trading analytics firm who wants to do their own analysis, or this start-up who's going into space and needs this thing.
There's also a misunderstanding of where AI demand is coming from, he insists:
As AI becomes more and more ubiquitous, this is just computing...We’re installing far more AI-accelerated compute than traditional compute, even while traditional compute has gone way up. This is not a fad. When mini-computers came along, people didn't ask, ‘But what about the punch cards?’. The reality is this is the new normal for computing, and the demand for it is immense because it has a fundamentally different capability than we've seen before.
I think we will see massive demand from the big players, but also we see huge demand across the portfolio from new start-ups to established enterprises. We will continue to build a more diverse set of options, just from a risk management perspective. But obviously, big sites fill a role. The thing about a big site is you can carve it up into a lot of small allocations. You can't take a bunch of small data centers and join them together as well as you can as a large campus. You will continue to see a mixture of both from us, and I think you will see that throughout the entire industry.
My take
Magouyrk and Maxson did a good job this week calming down the nervous souls on Wall Street, still baffled by the basic truth that it costs money to build out the AI infrastructure needed to make more money! They won’t have removed the ongoing tension around the prospect of an AI bubble bursting - and so long as the likes of the IMF and Jamie Dimon are there to remind us of that, that’s a problem for another day - but the healthy bump that the Oracle share price got this week is a sign of mission partly-accomplished.
