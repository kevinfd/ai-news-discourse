# Anthropic’s IPO is selling investors a story. The numbers tell another one

Fast Company · Mark Sullivan · 2026-10-29

Welcome to AI Decoded, Fast Company’s weekly newsletter that breaks down the most important news in the world of AI. I’m Mark Sullivan, a senior writer at Fast Company, covering emerging tech, AI, and tech policy.
Sign up to receive this newsletter every week via email here. And if you have comments on this issue and/or ideas for future ones, drop me a line at sullivan@fastcompany.com, and follow me on X @thesullivan.
Anthropic IPO: Do retail investors know what they’re getting into?
Anthropic officially filed its IPO papers earlier this week, hoping to raise up to $100 billion, and as much as 20% of the shares may be reserved for retail investors (aka regular folks). On Tuesday, Meta said it would sell equity shares on the public market to raise $95 billion to finance its AI infrastructure build-out. Retail investors have long been exposed to the AI business through shares in tech behemoths like Google and Microsoft, but they’re now getting the chance to place bets on pure-play AI labs like Anthropic and OpenAI.
Do they know what they’re getting into? I have my doubts. Does anyone really know what we’re getting into? Retail investors make investing decisions differently from institutional investors (such as pension funds or hedge funds). While institutional investors dig into the financials of prospective investments, retail investors often base their decisions on the narrative a company lays out as it goes public.
“Retail investors buy the story, not the model,” PitchBook senior late-stage research analyst Harrison Rolfes tells Fast Company. “The story for Anthropic is $11.6 billion of revenue in a single quarter; the model is $8.9 billion spent on compute in six months and more than $430 billion of non-cancelable compute contracts.” The financials in Anthropic’s S-1 imply an 85% gross margin, but Rolfes points out that after factoring in the cost of the compute that trains the next model, only “about 45 cents of each revenue dollar is left.”
A big chunk of Anthropic’s commitments—at least $269 billion—is owed to Amazon, Google, and Microsoft for cloud computing resources through 2036. Ten years is a long timeline for a commitment that big, especially when it’s not even clear that AI will continue to be served almost exclusively from cloud servers a decade from now. WebAI’s David Stout argues that as AI becomes more efficient and capable of running directly on devices (see Apple’s new iMac and MacBook Pro with the M6 chip), the industry risks pouring trillions into data centers it may not ultimately need. WebAI is one of a wave of companies building private, localized AI that runs on devices organizations already own.
As the cadence of new model releases accelerates, enterprises are struggling to keep up—to build the infrastructure and applications they need to take advantage of the latest models’ capabilities. The pace at which enterprises can adapt their processes and people to AI may be limited by forces outside the AI labs’ control. For example, the best AI model is still only as good as the contextual data an enterprise can give it, and preparing that data and building the conduits to get it to the right place takes time and resources.
