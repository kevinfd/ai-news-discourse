# Gamer's World | Mario Creator Backs a Balanced Approach to Generative AI in Games

JAPAN Forward · Daniel Robson · 2026-10-29

I was interested to see Shigeru Miyamoto, the creator of timeless video game franchises like Mario, Zelda, and Donkey Kong, make encouraging comments about the potential opportunities of generative AI in game development.
Miyamoto spoke at a press conference on October 23, where it was announced he will receive the Order of Culture, Japan's highest honor for contributions to the arts, science, or general culture, at a ceremony on November 3.
Miyamoto is the first game designer to ever receive the award. Japanese media site Game Watch reported on the press conference in which Miyamoto reflected on his 50 years in the game business.
Drawing the Line Between Creating and Labor
Miyamoto's generation lived through a period of rapid computer development, as tools moved from typewriters to word processors. He recalled that designers were once taught at school how to letter posters beautifully, and that this skill alone earned the title of designer. Others simply took a design trending abroad, gave it a "Japanese-style" twist, and were labeled designers all the same.
"As computers and technology advanced, a designer's true individual capabilities, such as their innate sense of layout, finally came to the fore," he said. "I believe generative AI represents a similar shift."
He distinguishes between creating something and the labor involved in the process. Creation begins with a broad structure, followed by the hands-on work of filling gaps and refining details.
"The tricky part is that this labor can actually be quite enjoyable," Miyamoto said. "While the act of creation is inherently satisfying, there is a danger: one might find satisfaction in the labor itself and mistakenly believe that a high-quality piece of work has been produced."
In that sense, he said, the more AI takes over these tasks, the more it gives people a chance to reflect on what is truly important, which he sees as a positive thing.
He also pointed to past designs that were made merely by copying and pasting. Such work rarely stands the test of time, he said, because it lacks the individual's particular design intention.
"Because the process relies on an individual's intent, with the computer serving as an assistant for the labor, I believe that even as technology continues to evolve, and even if AI were to eventually handle everything, there will always be a role left for us to play."
It was a thoughtful, measured evaluation of the opportunities AI promises from an unparalleled creative innovator in Japanese games. Miyamoto has been central to the success of Nintendo these past 50 years, and he has helped write the rules everyone else follows to this day. And so his insight on AI is of particular value.
Slop Floods Western Storefronts
While game developers have always used artificial intelligence as a tool to create games, the use of generative AI in creative fields has become a controversial topic. Game stores are already being flooded with "slop": crudely made "vibe-coded" games and instant copycats that siphon attention and sales away from real games.
For example, the viral hit game Needle In A Haystack Simulator has generated so many clones on Steam that it's impossible to tell which one is the original; while a recent Steam game demo festival featured an overwhelming 8,000 games, many of which were AI slop, drowning out the handmade ones.
Meanwhile, replacing human staff with cheaper generative AI tools has led to mass layoffs in the West and fueled fears that games will become derivative, homogeneous, and bland. To put it lightly, core gamers and many Western developers really don't like generative AI.
Asia Takes a Different Path
But in Asia, things are very different. In China, AI use has become routine, with many of the larger game companies investing heavily in research, mandating that staff use AI where possible to improve efficiency, and proudly marketing new games with characters, dialogue, or other elements generated by AI.
China benefits from a new law that prevents companies from replacing human staff with AI, which adds job security while eliminating some of the risks involved with implementing AI more widely into game development.
Japan, as usual, is somewhere in between. While game designers here tend to have a more artisanal approach, they are also pragmatic, with a risk-averse mindset that encourages them to plan more carefully and keep budgets from spiraling.
As such, the potential boosts to productivity offered by AI tools can be hard to ignore. A recent survey by Japan's game industry body CESA revealed that over 85% of Japanese developers use generative AI in their work, in comparison with only 36% of their counterparts polled at the Game Developers Conference in the US.
Why Miyamoto Matters in This Debate
This is the context for Shigeru Miyamoto's comments. It was possible that such a creative mind may have shunned the use of generative AI altogether, as many see it as the death of creativity itself.
Then again, on the other end of the pendulum you have Tim Sweeney, the billionaire head of Fortnite and Unreal Engine maker Epic Games, who is so gung-ho for gen AI that he has spent the past few weeks trolling anyone with a contrary view with ever more bizarre political analogies on X.
So it was a relief to see Miyamoto take a more moderate stance, lending nuance to a conversation that has been sorely lacking any. And more than that, I saw it as a revealing insight into Miyamoto's approach to the art of creating.
As a writer myself, I understand that the hard parts of creating something, finding ways to solve a problem and working methodically until it is solved, are the fun part. The end result is rewarding because you made it yourself.
On the other hand, no one wants to be a luddite. There's no question that AI tools can be empowering, and in a world where games require more time and money than ever to make, the temptation to take shortcuts is real. But when the machine is simply making the game for you, isn't that really the opposite of empowerment?
Miyamoto was able to create some of gaming's most enduring iconic characters and games without AI. But that was a different time. Game development was much simpler in the 1970s when he was starting out; the rules had yet to be defined, and consumer expectations were not as high.
I suppose the hope is that today, a creator of his caliber would have the strength of vision to use those tools responsibly; that rather than be led astray by AI's deceptive snake-oil promise of unlimited power, he could use AI tools to create what was in his head more efficiently within the constraints of modern game design.
Level5 Faces Fan Backlash
Another storied Japanese creator who has openly embraced AI is Akihiro Hino, the CEO of Level5 and creator of Professor Layton, Ni No Kuni and Inazuma Eleven.
Level5 has struggled in recent years to keep up with player demands, with game development cycles reaching five years or more, which isn't unusual for bigger games, but is too long and expensive for the types of games Level5 makes. Hino has spoken in the past about his intention to use AI tools to shorten this cycle.
However, a recent online showcase of new games such as a remake of Professor Layton and the Curious Village, Yo-Kai Watch 2: Haunted Domain and DecaPolice caused a stink when fans deduced the games had used generative AI for things like background designs.
It's a real shame when you consider the highly authored and exquisite quality of Level5's past work. Fans in the West were quick to complain on social media, labeling the games as "AI slop."
Hino responded on X with an apology and an explanation of his rationale, reiterating that the games' "scenarios, character designs and settings have been created by human hands."
He countered the criticism by arguing that the time freed up by AI efficiency lets creators spend more time on "the essence of creation." He said he believes the finished products will satisfy everyone even more than before.
Level5 is working to shorten the development period for major titles from five years to two, Hino explained, and the studio is still in the stage of "trial and error, and struggling forward."
Gamers Want Proof, Not Promises
I think the underlying problem is one of show versus tell. Whenever game companies have tried aggressively to sell gamers on new technology (NFTs, blockchain, metaverse, 3D TVs, VR, always-online DRM), gamers have resisted until the companies either found a beneficial way to integrate the technology or simply gave up.
Gamers are right to be suspicious, because so far the integration of AI has been about cutting corners, copying existing ideas, flooding the market, cutting development costs, and slashing headcounts.
But once we start to see some truly amazing examples of AI used well, maybe gamers will change their minds. I'm sure it will take someone with the fortitude of Shigeru Miyamoto to do it.
