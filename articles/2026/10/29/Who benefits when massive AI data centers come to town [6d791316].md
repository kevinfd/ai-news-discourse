# Who benefits when massive AI data centers come to town?

NPR · Tonya Mosley · 2026-10-29

Who benefits when massive AI data centers come to town?
The battle over data centers is hitting the ballot box. Brookings scholar Darrell West talks about how the backlash against the AI boom, flock cameras and tech companies is crossing party lines.
TONYA MOSLEY, HOST:
This is FRESH AIR. I'm Tonya Mosley. The AI boom has a problem. It has to be built somewhere, and to power it, tech companies are building massive data centers. They need land, enormous amounts of electricity, and in many cases, water. In exchange, they promise jobs and community investment. But people who live in these communities are demanding to know, what's this going to cost us? There's also another question, who gets to decide? In communities from Wisconsin to Louisiana, residents say local officials are negotiating data center deals without their input.
This midterm election, voters in more than 40 cities and towns across eight states will decide ballot measures on data center bans, moratoriums and requirements that residents get a vote before one is built. And Democrats and Republicans are going after their opponents for what used to be a bragging right, the ability to bring billions of dollars in investment to town. My guest is Darrell West, a senior fellow at the Brookings Institution. He and his colleague, Nicol Turner Lee, write about the backlash against AI data centers and how these local fights are becoming part of something much bigger, a challenge to the enormous power of the tech industry and who gets to decide what our future looks like. And, Darrell West, welcome to FRESH AIR.
DARRELL WEST: Thank you, Tonya. It's nice to be with you.
MOSLEY: Well, I actually want to start with a couple of political ads. And the first one is from Ohio, where Democrat Sherrod Brown is challenging Republican Senator Jon Husted. Our lakes run dry, family farms paved over - why? - because Crowley's campaign is funded by the people building them. Data Center David Crowley; Big Tech wins, Wisconsin pays.
MOSLEY: All right. So, Darrell, what's fascinating to me about these ads is you've got a Democrat from Ohio and a Republican in Wisconsin, and they're making practically the same attack. When did you realize this wasn't just a string of maybe local zoning fights anymore, that it really has become a national political story?
WEST: Now, Tonya, you're completely right. It has become a national political story. And public opinion has changed dramatically just over the course of the last year. As you mentioned in your intro, a year ago, politicians of both parties were falling all over themselves trying to attract these economic development dollars into their local communities. They were basically providing tax breaks to these very wealthy tech companies in order to get the data centers built there.
But over the last six to nine months, we've just seen this huge change where people either went from being mildly positive or at least neutral towards data centers, to today, 70% of Americans now say they have serious reservations about data centers. A recent survey showed 50% of Americans said they did not want to live next to a data center. And so these ads that you just showed basically demonstrate how that has changed. But it's not just Democrats versus Republicans. I mean, whoever has supported data centers in the past, whoever has supported tax breaks for data centers in the past, is now finding that policy position being used against them in this campaign.
MOSLEY: Data centers, though, they've really been around for decades. So what's different about the enormous ones that are being built now for AI?
WEST: The big difference is just the size of these new facilities. So you're right that data centers have been around for a long time. A lot of companies have facilities that house their computing equipment and their file servers just in order to run their internal operations. But the data centers that are being built today are huge in size. A number of them often take the space of two or three football fields, if not bigger.
They are enormous in terms of the expenditures that are behind them. And as they have grown bigger, the public has started to grow more wary of them. Like, if you're living next to a data center, neighbors complain about noise. You mentioned the issues of energy, water and land use. People are worried about those aspects as well. And of course, people are just worried about the uses to which data centers are being put, which is to run AI, which also has become very contentious in recent months.
MOSLEY: Yeah, we'll talk about that a little bit later. But can you also take us inside? Like, what does it actually look like inside those buildings?
WEST: Well, I have toured data centers. And there actually are a lot of videos online. So people can look at them themselves. But basically, it's just rows and rows of computing equipment. They're actually called file servers. These are the devices that run the entire digital revolution. Like, when people shop online, or if they engage in an online banking transaction, all of that electronic stuff is going through a data center. There are file servers there that store information, process requests, undertake financial transactions. Everything that people do online goes through a data center.
MOSLEY: In all of your research, has anyone living near one actually said something that really stayed with you?
WEST: A lot of the comments of people who live very close to a data center are negative. They complain about the noise issue. They don't want to live next to this big industrial facility. I actually grew up in rural Ohio. And people who live in the country want to look at rolling farmland. Like, they don't want to live next to this big industrial facility.
So the neighbors complain a lot. They worry about the water demands, the impact on their electric rates. In a number of states, we've seen electric rates go up a lot in recent years. That's not all due to data centers. You know, we have inflation, the war in Iran, tariffs and a bunch of other things that contribute to that. But people can actually see that connection. They're drawing a connection between those data centers and negative effects on them financially, as well as on environmental considerations.
MOSLEY: OK. So I'm imagining that I'm a mayor in a rural town in rural Ohio or anywhere. And it's a struggling town. And I am sitting across from a company offering billions, with more lawyers than really maybe the town even has employees. How much leverage does that mayor really have in those types of negotiations when they're being offered that type of money?
WEST: A year ago, those mayors and other local officials felt like they had very little leverage because, as you point out, you know, rural communities suffer economically. There aren't a lot of jobs. There just isn't very much economic activity going on. And so these big tech companies showed up and said, hey, we're going to build these big facilities. It's going to provide jobs. You'll get tax revenues out of this. And the local officials were so desperate a year ago for this economic development that they would offer tremendous packages. They would offer them tax breaks. They would provide infrastructure for these facilities.
But over the course of the last year, as public opinion has shifted, city officials realize they now actually have much greater leverage. As opposed to having to offer tax breaks to these companies, a lot of these local officials are now saying, you, very wealthy tech company, you need to be paying us. There are going to be problems associated with this development. We want you to pay for any environmental problems that come up, we want you to cover all of the energy needs of your facility. If there are water issues, you need to tell us how you're going to handle that. So local officials actually are getting tougher.
At Brookings, my co-author Nicol Turner Lee and I have written a paper arguing that local officials need to engage in what we call community benefit agreements where they have formal negotiations with these companies, lay out the costs and the benefits, who's going to pay for the various problems. And so the local officials actually are getting much tougher in their bargaining because they now realize that with the contentiousness of data centers, many communities actually no longer want them. They can drive a much tougher bargain with the tech companies.
MOSLEY: Is there an example you can give us of an area that is really pushing back and demanding a tech company really provide those things?
WEST: Well, right outside of Washington D.C., where I am located right now, is Loudoun County, Virginia. Loudoun County is the Silicon Valley of data centers. So, like, that area has more data centers than any other part of America. And the reason they have so many data centers is that they have, until quite recently, actually been very welcoming. They have provided tax breaks for data centers. They've been very encouraging of the tech companies to locate in those areas. But as has happened in many other communities, their local residents now are getting fed up with this. They feel like there are too many data centers, the data centers are creating problems for their local communities.
So even the local officials there who generally have been supportive of data centers are starting to drive a harder bargain. They want companies to pay them for the development rights because the local officials realize they have a great power. In order to build a data center, you need a construction permit. Like, you cannot build a major facility like this until the local officials approve of that. And so Loudoun County, as well as other places around the country, are basically saying, you need to do X, Y and Z for my local community. And if you don't do it, we're not going to let you build the data center here.
MOSLEY: Do you see this fight against data centers as also a way of fighting back against the power of the tech industry itself?
WEST: Yes, absolutely. The data center fight is no longer just about data centers. It's about AI and its possible impact on people. You know, we've also seen the headlines about job losses coming from AI. We've seen the stories about AI agents going rogue and doing things and going into websites where they don't belong. So the backlash against data centers is now aligned with the backlash against AI. But it's also bigger than that in the sense that both data centers and AI are being developed by these ultra-wealthy tech companies, multi-multibillion or some of them even have valuations of over $1 trillion.
The idea that these very wealthy companies would come into local communities and demand a tax break just infuriates people because they think, you know, if you're going to come into my community, build a facility, create problems for my local community, check up my electric rates, at least you better pay me for the right to do that. And so the complaints that people have about data centers are now tied into AI, income inequality, the tech billionaires and just the great power that technology plays in all of our lives. Now, we can't really control the way that companies roll out AI products. You know, in a private enterprise system, they can basically develop whatever products they want and put them in the marketplace.
So data centers kind of became the flashpoint of these larger fights because it was an area where people had human agency. They could basically organize locally, convince three out of five people on their local city council or their local planning commission to vote no. They discovered they had leverage. And so data centers became the chokepoint for this bigger fight, much of which we can't control some of those bigger issues, but we can control whether a data center gets built in our local community.

(SOUNDBITE OF SLOWBERN'S "WHEN WAR WAS KING")
MOSLEY: This is FRESH AIR, and today, I am talking with Darrell West of the Brookings Institution about one of the biggest issues in the midterm elections - the massive data centers being built to power artificial intelligence.
OK, Darrell, I want to get into some of the specifics of communities throughout the country. And I want to stop and look at Cedar Rapids, where Google could get a 70% property tax exemption for 20 years. And this deal requires at least 31 permanent jobs, along with other commitments. But the big flashpoint for people, the big thing that folks are talking about is the number of jobs - 31 jobs. That is the floor. They say that that could be more. But how do residents know whether that's a good deal?
WEST: Well, that is one of the reasons why people are upset with data centers because the companies often promise a large number of jobs that sometimes fail to materialize. So the largest number of jobs actually comes in the construction phase because these are huge facilities. So you need construction workers, electricians, pipe fitters, welders, kind of the full array of local tradespeople. And with these very large facilities, oftentimes, there could be as many as 1,000 jobs for the duration of that construction. But then of course...

WEST: Yes. The construction ends, and you're right. In terms of the operational jobs, the jobs required just to operate that facility, it tends to be a pretty small number. Obviously, depending on the size of the facility, but it could be in your example, 31, it could be 50, it might even be as large as 100, but it's not really a lot of jobs on an ongoing basis. That is very aggravating to people because, like, the whole point of economic development is to provide jobs. And when you have a big facility, and especially a company that has demanded tax breaks to build that facility, coming in, and the number of permanent jobs aren't as many as people would like, that is part of the backlash. That's part of the reason these local people all across the country are getting upset with data centers.
MOSLEY: President Trump has been pretty blunt in his messaging about this. He actually wrote on Truth Social that the only reason they wouldn't want them, meaning communities, is if they want to be, quote, "backwards and poor." And he says China couldn't be happier about all of this opposition that we're seeing. Kind of talk to me about his argument that communities turning down data centers are essentially turning down prosperity.
WEST: Well, he seems to think there are only two choices, either you build the facility or you don't build the facility. There actually is a third choice, which is you build the facility, but local communities drive a much harder bargain, make sure the companies pay their complete energy costs, that they provide worker training that kind of helps the community on a long-term basis. Like, there are lots of things communities could actually get out of data centers that, in some cases, they're not currently getting.
Trump is on the wrong side of public opinion on the data center subject. Even within his own party, there's a clear majority of Republicans who are opposed to data centers. I mentioned earlier I grew up in rural Ohio. I still have, you know, high school friends from there, family members and so on. And I've been surprised over the course of the last year how these very conservative supporters of President Trump don't like data centers. And so Trump doesn't seem to understand it's not liberal Democrats who are upset with data centers. It's really become a bipartisan issue. And he's just simply on the wrong side of public opinion on this.
MOSLEY: Well, I want to interrogate your idea that there's a third choice. So many times, these companies can negotiate their own electricity agreements. But, you know, the family down the road can't negotiate their rates. So if a data center needs new power lines, maybe even a new power plant, under what circumstances does that family's electric bill actually help pay for it? How can you differentiate all of that?
WEST: You're right. It is hard to differentiate those things. But right now, local officials are basically telling companies, you need to pay the complete energy costs of this data center. And they are enforcing it. And they have local experts who know about electric rates and, you know, infrastructure development. And so they are basically driving a much harder bargain.
But the conversation also has changed recently in the sense that it's moved from just asking companies to cover their energy costs, but sometimes we need to upgrade the infrastructure in a local community. Sometimes we need to modernize the electric grid. Our grid across this country is kind of woefully out of date. And so, increasingly, local officials are asking data center developers not just to cover the energy costs, but to cover infrastructure improvements, as well as helping to modernize the electric grid as a whole.
MOSLEY: There's another cost that I've heard less about, but there's more reporting that's starting to come out, and it's about people's health. So there's this Washington Post analysis that found that diesel backup generators at Virginia data centers could worsen asthma and heart problems, even though they're rarely used. So when a town is calculating the economic benefits of these projects, who's actually calculating the health costs?
WEST: Well, local officials need to be calculating the health costs because that is an issue. And what people have to keep in mind about data centers is, you know, because we all rely on these facilities for the entire digital revolution, they have to be up and running 24/7. There should not be any downtime. Like, people do online shopping at all hours of the day. We engage in online financial transactions all hours of the day. We stream movies all hours of the day.
And so what the tech companies have done is, you know, they rely on either their own energy sources or the local grid, but they do have backup systems, which often are diesel-fuel-supplied or diesel-fuel-run. As that Washington Post story indicated, there have been arguments that that creates health risks for local communities. And so, you know, it's like, most economic development projects have some problems like this. Like, you know, if you live next to a mine, there are going to be enormous health costs associated with that.
If you live next to a major manufacturing facility, there are going to be health risks associated with that. But what we do in other areas is we try and find out what are the problems, how to mitigate them, who's going to pay and how do we protect the health and safety of the people who live there? We haven't really been doing that until recently with a lot of these data centers. But as the public opposition has grown, the tech companies realize they now need to do a better job at both addressing these issues, explaining their stance, covering the costs and dealing with the various side effects of these developments.
MOSLEY: Our guest today is Darrell West from the Brookings Institution. We'll be right back after a short break. I'm Tonya Mosley, and this is FRESH AIR.

MOSLEY: This is FRESH AIR. I'm Tonya Mosley. And my guest today is Darrell West, a political scientist who studies how technology is shaping American politics and democracy. So who gets left with it?
WEST: Well, this is the so-called AI bubble. And in our research on community benefit agreements, we argue this is something that local communities need to address in writing with these companies. Like, what happens if either that company goes bankrupt in three years or demand is lower than projected and they don't need that data center? Like, what's going to happen to that facility? What will be the consequences of that facility closing? How do we deal with the impact on the community? Like, that is something that local communities need to be negotiating. They should be aware that even though AI is expected to grow, it may not grow at the exponential levels that either Wall Street is anticipating or that the tech companies themselves are expecting, and they need to think about how to handle the impact on their local communities if a data center goes bust.
MOSLEY: I want to talk a little bit more on how to do that because there's this research in Virginia. Virginia is really a hot spot for this. It found that 25 of 31 communities they examined had nondisclosure agreements with data center developers. And I should say that that's not unheard of when it comes to corporations building in communities. Some of those agreements, though, they cover it far more than trade secrets. So how are residents supposed to know whether a deal is good for their community if they can't actually see what their officials are negotiating?
WEST: From my standpoint, nondisclosure agreements are one of the worst aspects of the local data center issue. And you're right that, until quite recently, most of these companies did require nondisclosure agreements when they were negotiating with local officials. So people in the area that were going to be affected by these new facilities didn't know what was being negotiated, what the agreements were, who is going to cover the various costs, how the companies were going to deal with their energy needs and their water needs.
And in some respect, the nondisclosure agreement has helped to propel the public backlash that is developed because we know in all aspects of government, when agreements are negotiated in secret, people are going to be skeptical of it. And so part of the backlash that we're seeing is not just the result of the data center, but the process by which these agreements have been negotiated.
And companies are actually starting to realize this. Some of the big companies just in the last few months have announced they're no longer going to use nondisclosure agreements in their local data center negotiations. They want more information to be available. They think they have a good story to tell. They say, we're going to provide jobs, there'll be tax revenues. We want people to be aware of this. But the whole sector made a serious mistake, in my view, in, up until recently, using nondisclosure agreements, because people are going to assume the worst if they don't know the details on what is being discussed.
MOSLEY: Another story that caught my attention, it was out of Mississippi, Amazon challenging the assessed value of its data centers. The county says it could lose 5 to 6 million a year in expected tax revenue. So I was just wondering what protects a town when a company challenges the very revenue the deal was supposed to bring?
WEST: That will be an issue, I think, in several communities because the tax rate depends on the assessed value. And we know that companies often contest high valuations because that will boost their tax bill. Local communities have to be prepared not just to oversee the building of the data center, but the consequences that are going to flow from that. There're going to continue to be controversies between companies and local communities over tax rates and tax assessments. And so communities need to be prepared for this and to kind of think about what kind of expertise they need and what kind of legal resources they may need if there are lawsuits that come out of these fights.
MOSLEY: Let's talk a little bit more about community benefit agreements. In your writing, you point to Lancaster, Pennsylvania, as a model. And tell me a little bit about what they're doing there.
WEST: What they did in Lancaster was basically open up the process, negotiate things in public and then have a formal, publicly available signed agreement between the local officials and the tech company developing the local data centers that laid out all of the issues associated with that development - who's going to cover the energy costs, how the water needs were going to be handled, how land use aspects were going to be covered, what types of tax revenues would come into the community as a result of the development, how many jobs, construction jobs and operational jobs, would be there. Like, all the things that people are worried about with data centers were negotiated in public and there was a written agreement. And if the company violated the terms of the agreement, that would be considered a breach of contract, and the company would be subject to civil penalties and fines based on that.
From the standpoint of my co-author, Nicol Turner Lee, and myself, we feel like this is a responsible way to think about data centers in the future, in the sense of a public negotiation, a written document laying out the costs and benefits, people can see them, and communities then can decide, is this a good project for our local community or not?
MOSLEY: Then there are just communities that are straight up saying no. They'll be voting in the midterms, whether to have one built or not. And there are also those that have put moratoriums on the building of centers until they get ahold of it. But you, in your writing, you've actually argued that putting a temporary stop to construction is not enough. Communities need real oversight. Can you talk about what that would look like?
WEST: There are a number of cities and even states around the country that have imposed moratoriums. Oftentimes, they're either six or 12 months. What we argue is what these officials need to do during that six or 12 month time period is to think about what they want, does the project fit with their community needs and then how the various issues associated with that data center are going to be addressed? And is it a good deal for the local communities? So, you know, putting a moratorium is fine, and it's actually popular. You know, since the public is skeptical about data centers, it kind of buys time for the local officials. But they still have to address the substantive issues of data center development and is it a good deal for their local community?
