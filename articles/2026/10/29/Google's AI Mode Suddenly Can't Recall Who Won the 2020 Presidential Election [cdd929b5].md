# Google's AI Mode Suddenly Can't Recall Who Won the 2020 Presidential Election

PCMag · Rob Pegoraro · 2026-10-29

Google's AI Mode is supposed to be the company’s most versatile and far-reaching web-search tool, but for much of Wednesday, it returned the equivalent of a 404 error when asked one of the simplest queries imaginable: Who won the 2020 presidential election?
Instead of replying with the truth that Joe Biden defeated Donald Trump in that election, AI Mode returned the following non-response, devoid of links to any other web page: "For official results, electoral vote counts, popular vote breakdowns, and historical details regarding the 2020 US presidential election, please consult official government archives or trusted election records."
Not answering that question or offering weasel-worded non-answers along the lines of "President Biden took office in 2021" has become almost a rite of passage for Trump nominees in confirmation hearings. Trump has spent years insisting that he won in 2020, a lie that led to the Jan. 6, 2021, insurrection at the Capitol, so contradicting the boss on that point is a career-limiting move. But it’s something else for the world’s leading search site to duck this question.
AI Mode had no such hang-up answering the same question about the 2024 election, listing President Trump as the winning candidate and his share of the popular vote and total votes with extraordinary precision: 49.91% and 77,303,573, compared with Democratic nominee Kamala Harris, who received 48.43% of the popular vote and 75,019,257 votes. Google’s regular, non-AI search, meanwhile, correctly fielded the 2020 query.
Social media lit up with posts mocking Google, including screen grabs of AI Mode results for the 2020 question in other countries that offered the same precise totals documenting Biden’s win over Trump, a result I was able to reproduce by using the free Proton VPN service to put my phone on a Swiss IP address.
Google is among the many large US tech firms to have gone out of its way to curry favor with Trump or at least try to persuade him to dial his tariffs down a notch. CEO Sundar Pichai showed up for his inauguration; the company matched its peers in donating $1 million to Trump’s inauguration fund; and Google quickly updated Google Maps when Trump ordered geographic renamings, such as calling Lake Ontario “Lake America.”
But Pichai has yet to go out and sing Trump’s praises in the way that Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang keeps doing, nor has he shown up in the Oval Office to hand the president a plaque on a 24-karat gold base as former Apple CEO Tim Cook did last August. Further, Google has yet to follow the example of such AI providers as SpaceXAI in indulging Trump’s edict rebranding “artificial intelligence” as “super intelligence”; AI Mode is, so far, not “SI Mode.”
Google chose to answer questions about its AI search’s odd behavior in an odd way: not with a post on its own blog, but with a reply from its @NewsFromGoogle X account to a post on that platform from climate journalist Zeke Hausfather.
That response, on a platform on which X owner Elon Musk regularly posts his own evidence-starved election conspiracy theories, read as follows: “We’re fixing the issue with 2020 election queries in AI Mode; the Gemini app answers the question more fully. Our systems verify election responses using news articles or AP data for recent elections, and historic records for older ones. There was a technical gap with 2020 - same is true for 2022 Senate races, which we’re also working to fix.”
I tested that Wednesday night by asking AI Mode, “Who won the 2022 Senate race in Ohio?” It returned a non-answer, strikingly similar to its dodge of the 2020 question: “For official results and candidate vote percentages from the 2022 United States Senate election in Ohio, please consult the Ohio Secretary of State's official election reports or certified election results portals.”
But next to that pablum, AI Mode also showed links to a Wikipedia article and an NPR story about that election—both correctly listing Republican JD Vance, now Trump’s vice president, as the winner over the Democratic incumbent, Sherrod Brown.
One other reason to think Google is having a bigger problem with this application of AI: Last month, my fellow PCMag contributor Rakesh Agrawal caught Google’s Gemini chatbot sharing its own tightly written guidelines about answering election questions that include avoiding making predictions about upcoming elections and pointing people to “official sources.”
So, there may not be a case for Google putting a thumb on the AI-search scale in some weird attempt to suck up to election deniers. But this little episode does provide another reason to question the usefulness of Google’s fixation on AI as a web-search interface.
