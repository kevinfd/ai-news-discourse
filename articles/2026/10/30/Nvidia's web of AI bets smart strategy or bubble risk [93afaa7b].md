# Nvidia's web of AI bets: smart strategy or bubble risk?

The Hindu · AFP · 2026-10-30

Nvidia's $3 billion investment in Meta's share sale, announced Tuesday, is the latest addition to a portfolio of stakes, loans and guarantees that has made the chip giant what some analysts describe as the central bank of the artificial intelligence boom.
The galaxy of deals has helped keep demand running high for Nvidia's processors, which power vast data centres around the world and are the central ingredient of the AI revolution.
That demand has made Nvidia the world's most valuable company by market capitalisation and turned chief executive Jensen Huang into AI's kingmaker.
Nvidia has taken stakes in leading AI developers including OpenAI, Anthropic and Elon Musk's xAI, now part of SpaceX.
It has also invested in "neocloud" companies such as CoreWeave and Nebius, which rent out computing power built on Nvidia chips.
Its bets extend well beyond the United States. In Europe, Nvidia has backed French AI developer Mistral and British self-driving startup Wayve. In Asia, it has teamed up with South Korea's SK Group to build data centers.
Beyond equity, the company has offered financial backstops, acting as a customer of last resort.
In CoreWeave's case, Nvidia committed to buy up to $6.3 billion of unused computing capacity if no other customer takes it.
Nvidia also has around $36 billion in cloud backstops and a guarantee of up to $105 billion linked to an OpenAI data centre, according to US media.
Experts say Nvidia's enormous profits give it few better options.
"They're essentially becoming a bank," said Michael Cusumano, a professor at the MIT Sloan School of Management.
"Channeling it back to infrastructure investment tied to purchases of Nvidia equipment is a smart thing."
He said the alternatives were less attractive.
Running its own data centres would put Nvidia in competition with its biggest customers "and that might also trigger a whole bunch of antitrust complaints," Cusumano added.
Gil Luria, head of technology research at D.A. Davidson, said Nvidia identifies parts of the AI ecosystem that are not investing enough and puts money into them to fill the gaps.
"It's unprecedented, but it appears to be working," he said.
Huang has defended the approach, arguing that frontier AI labs are the first generation of startups that need tens of billions of dollars to get started, not just millions.
Nvidia's investments effectively give those companies more time to succeed.
Critics have compared the deals to the dot-com era, when companies inflated their growth by investing in startups that then bought their products.
"There is nothing intrinsically wrong with Nvidia helping its clients," said Paulo Carvão, a senior fellow at the Harvard Kennedy School.
"But the closer these relationships are, the harder it is for one to assess true sustainable demand."
Luria said not all demand was equal.
"If Nvidia gives CoreWeave money to turn around and buy Nvidia chips, that's not as good as Microsoft buying Nvidia chips because they have a customer in OpenAI that has a customer in an enterprise technology that's willing to pay for it," he said.
The main concern is concentration. Two direct customers accounted for 22 percent and 14 percent of Nvidia's revenue in its 2026 fiscal year, according to the company's annual report.
Carvão said the web of overlapping investments increases the risk of contagion if a weaker company such as CoreWeave runs into trouble.
"If AI demand disappoints, spending cuts could hit chipmakers, data-center builders and their financiers at once," he said.
Those concerns have grown this month. Shares of several Nvidia-linked neocloud companies, including CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN, fell at least 30 percent in October amid worries about tighter credit for AI projects.
Carvão noted that the market for AI chips remains limited by supply. He estimated demand would keep growing for another 12 to 18 months before it begins to level off.
Cusumano said he expected high demand for Nvidia chips to continue for three to five years.
