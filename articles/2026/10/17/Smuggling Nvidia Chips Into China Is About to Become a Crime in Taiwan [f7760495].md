# Smuggling Nvidia Chips Into China Is About to Become a Crime in Taiwan

24/7 Wall St. · Omor Ibne Ehsan · 2026-10-17

Smuggling Nvidia Chips Into China Is About to Become a Crime in Taiwan
Taiwan just announced plans to criminalize AI chip shipments to China, and the legal gap still open is the only thing standing between three major semiconductor companies and a tighter regulatory net.
Until now, someone in Taiwan who shipped a rack of AI servers to a buyer in China faced no direct criminal penalty at home. On October 16, 2026, Taiwan’s Minister of Economic Affairs Kung Ming-hsin confirmed that Taipei plans to add AI servers and cutting-edge semiconductors to its controlled goods list. That would make these shipments a crime under the Foreign Trade Act.
“About to” means the plan has been announced but is still waiting on the law. It needs a legal amendment, and Kung gave no timeline.
For shareholders of NVIDIA (NASDAQ:NVDA | NVDA Price Prediction), Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (NYSE:TSM) and Super Micro Computer (NASDAQ:SMCI), the effect is mixed. Closing the Taiwan channel could remove gray-market demand that never showed up in official China sales, but it also reduces regulatory risk around all three companies.
Friday’s moves came with a broader chip selloff and had little to do with this news. NVIDIA closed at $214.13, down 1.43%, worth $5.17 trillion. Taiwan Semiconductor fell 2.28% to $412.61, down 8.98% over the past week, and is worth $ 1.89 trillion. Super Micro dropped 9.86% to $31.63, worth $20.78 billion, after a one-week decline of 24.43%.
What Taipei Has Proposed
Taiwan’s current controls rely on a company blacklist that added Huawei and SMIC in mid-2025. The new approach would replace that list with a restriction covering the entire Chinese market.
In remarks reported in translation, Kung said “the measures we take on certain controlled items will correspond with those of the United States,” giving enforcement agencies “more legal tools” against smuggling.
He tied the move to the trade agreement Taiwan signed with Washington on January 15. That deal cut US tariffs on most Taiwanese goods from 20% to 15% and came with $250 billion in Taiwanese company commitments for US projects. Separately, DPP legislator Chung Chia-pin has drafted an amendment that adds a mainland China semiconductor clause, and it still has to pass.
The channel through which restricted hardware entered China was simple: servers built in or routed through Taiwan and other hubs ended up with Chinese buyers. Taiwan detained alleged AI chip smugglers in May.
In July, prosecutors pursued Super Micro executives on forgery charges. No one has been charged with chip smuggling in that case.
Why Super Micro Carries the Most Exposure
NVIDIA has already removed China from its numbers. Its third-quarter fiscal 2027 outlook of $108.0 billion excludes all China data center compute revenue, so lost gray-market demand would not hurt its guidance, and closer alignment with Washington could help when future licenses come up.
Taiwan Semiconductor makes the chips but does not ship servers. Its main gain is stability in the trade deal that sets its home market’s tariff rate.
Super Micro builds systems in Taiwan, and its board has disclosed an independent review of certain transactions related to export-control issues. That makes it the most exposed and most volatile of the three.
Regardless, Kung gave no start date, penalty schedule, or final list of covered products. Smuggling enforcement had already been a US and Singapore story, so Taiwan’s step adds to existing efforts, and the market appeared to treat it that way.
What the Taiwan Rule Means for NVIDIA’s Outlook
NVIDIA looks well positioned. The Taiwan rule cuts off demand the company never booked, while revenue grew 105.8% in the last quarter and management called its outlook “supply-constrained.”
At about 43 times earnings, the stock is priced for growth. But the new policy takes risk away from the business and adds none.
Taiwan Semiconductor participates in the AI expansion without server exposure. After a weekly drop of 8.98%, it raised fourth-quarter revenue guidance from $52.3 billion to $54.3 billion.
This view will be tested by whether Chung’s amendment passes Taiwan’s legislature with a start date and by whether NVIDIA’s next earnings report meets its $108.0 billion guidance.
