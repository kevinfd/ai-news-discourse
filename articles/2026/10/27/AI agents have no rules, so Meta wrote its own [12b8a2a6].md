# AI agents have no rules, so Meta wrote its own

Publishers have good reason to distrust Meta. But its scraping fight reveals a bigger gap: No one has defined what AI agents are allowed to do.
BY Pete Pachal
Pete Pachal is a journalist and the creator of Media Copilot, a newsletter and podcast that examines how AI is changing media, journalism, and the news.
Why would anyone trust Meta?
After all, this is the company that became the world’s avatar for shameless and exploitative data harvesting. The same data mastery that powers one of the most effective advertising platforms ever made also gave us Cambridge Analytica, whistleblowers like Frances Haugen and Sarah Wynn-Williams, and unflattering films like The Social Dilemma and, just this month, The Social Reckoning. In the Axios-Harris Poll 100, Meta scored a lowly 59.6, down in the basement with ByteDance and X.
Publishers have their own reasons to be suspicious. Meta once vastly increased the reach of its properties, and even paid them for content, then abruptly took it all away when its priorities shifted. Meta has blocked news entirely in Canada since 2023, and briefly did the same in Australia in 2021.
So when Nieman Lab reported that Meta had changed its terms of service in a way that seemed to give itself permission to scrape the site of any company with a Meta Business account, nobody was inclined to give it the benefit of the doubt.
The new terms, which allow Meta to use automated tools to “access, index, cache, analyze, collect, retrieve, or crawl” websites “associated with” a business account, and to use that content to “provide, develop, improve, research, train” Meta products, could be interpreted as signing over to Meta the license to do whatever it wants with your content, including training its AI models or even offering up AI answers based on it. Indeed, three legal experts Nieman Lab consulted warned publishers of exactly that danger.
Meta quickly backpedaled, sort of. Within a day, it removed the word train and limited scraped content to its advertising and business products. A spokesperson insisted, “This is not about scraping news sites.” That didn’t calm anyone down. Legal experts said the revised language still permits mass scraping, and publisher groups on both sides of the Atlantic demanded a pause and filed complaints with regulators. This week Meta pushed the effective date from October 30 to November 20. The U.K.’s News Media Association called the delay “a pause, not a solution.”
