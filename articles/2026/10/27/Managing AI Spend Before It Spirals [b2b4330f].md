# Managing AI Spend Before It Spirals

Forbes · Conal Gallagher, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-27

Conal Gallagher is the CIO at both Flexera and its division Revenera, where he manages IT and information security programs.
Cloud adoption moved faster than visibility, governance and financial discipline, leaving organizations to retrofit FinOps after costs had already escalated. AI is now creating a similar challenge, with token-based consumption making spending even more volatile, distributed and difficult to forecast.
This year marked significant enterprise investment in AI, with Gartner researchers predicting that “AI spend will more than double to $5.6 trillion by 2030.” It’s no surprise, then, that traditional software budgeting models are increasingly ill-suited to the economics of AI. Unlike conventional software, every prompt, response, API call and workflow can consume tokens and generate cost. On the enterprise scale, even small usage decisions can compound quickly, making budget forecasting nearly impossible. And because AI costs can surface through cloud infrastructure, SaaS subscriptions, embedded functionality and the data required to support each use case, CIOs and other IT leaders may not have a consolidated view of where that usage, and the associated cost, is coming from.
Instead, CIOs need to bring the same financial discipline to AI that FinOps brought to the cloud, starting with three principles that can help organizations better understand, govern and optimize AI spending.
Visibility: Know Where AI Costs Are Going
AI is being used across every level of the enterprise. But before optimization, organizations need to first understand which teams, applications, models and workflows are driving consumption.
Unfortunately, most enterprises don’t yet have a complete picture of what they’re spending on AI. Flexera 2026 data shows that only 31% of leaders believe they have accurate visibility into their AI software, and 59% stated that they had a year-over-year increase in wasted AI spend.
Without complete visibility, managing and forecasting that spend becomes difficult. Good token visibility means understanding which business units and applications are consuming AI, which models are driving that consumption, how usage is changing over time and what business purpose it supports.
A lesson that first emerged during cloud adoption is becoming relevant again as organizations scale their use of AI. The challenge wasn’t tracking down AI usage that was too expensive. It was realizing how much AI activity was happening outside centralized visibility and standards. Teams were adopting AI-enabled SaaS products, experimenting with embedded AI capabilities and testing standalone tools faster than governance processes could keep pace. Before enterprises could optimize costs, they first had to understand where AI was being used, who was consuming it and what business value those investments were expected to deliver.
That experience reinforced an important sequence of making sure that visibility comes before governance and that governance comes before optimization.
When AI bill shock occurs, leaders should ask: What are we consuming, where, at what cost and for what business purpose? And then, once the answers are clear, governance needs to be placed around that usage.
Governance: Match Model Choice To The Use Case
Not every workload needs the most capable and expensive model. Instead, leaders can establish guidelines around when premium models are justified versus when lower-cost models can accomplish the same task. By creating policies for model selection, teams can move beyond a one-size-fits-all approach and align model choice with the value and complexity of each use case.
AI governance can also be difficult because agents can generate consumption without a person actively initiating every interaction. As agents execute more autonomously, relatively small consumption decisions can scale quickly. That makes clear guidance even more important as agentic AI becomes more embedded in enterprise operations.
In tandem, establishing who has authority over select models across teams and applications helps prevent unnecessary costs from becoming embedded in everyday workflows. This responsibility can’t exclusively sit with IT or FinOps teams. Technology, finance, procurement and business owners also need shared accountability for the entire enterprise to derive the most business value from their AI investments.
Optimization: Measure Cost Against Value
The recent focus on “tokenmaxxing” exposed a weakness in how organizations measure AI performance. It’s clear that the goal shouldn’t be to simply maximize or minimize token usage but to instead focus on the value generated. For example, a high-token workflow may be worthwhile if it materially accelerates an action, while a lower-cost AI feature may still be wasteful if employees barely use it.
Measuring AI value needs to go beyond token volume alone. The real challenge for leaders is figuring out where AI investments create meaningful business outcomes and connecting AI spend to those results. The right metric will of course vary by use case, but the next phase of AI adoption is understanding how consumption connects to outcomes the business already values.
Enterprises are beginning to catch on. The FinOps Foundation’s “State of FinOps 2026 Report“ identifies AI cost management as the top skill set teams need to develop. And according to Gartner researchers, “organizations that use AI for analysis and data management report up to 42% and 33% improvements in business value, respectively, demonstrating the importance of focusing AI investment where it can generate measurable returns.”
FinOps practices teach us that cost optimization is about maximizing return, not minimizing investment. The same principle should apply to token consumption: Higher usage can make sense when it produces greater business value, while even inexpensive usage can be wasteful if the outcome doesn’t justify the cost.
The Future Of AI Spend
As organizations shift mindsets away from tracking AI consumption over to focusing on actual AI value, CIOs have an opportunity to build in financial discipline. Those looking to implement AI at scale need to keep control of the AI economics.
