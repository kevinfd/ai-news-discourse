# Generative AI services becoming like mobile carriers. Can Meta's Muse become the 'Rakuten' of the industry?

note · 週末ラボ · 2026-10-27

Generative AI services becoming like mobile carriers. Can Meta's Muse become the 'Rakuten' of the industry?
Meta's personal AI agent, 'Muse,' became available in Japan on October 23rd. The service, which had a head start in North America in September, has now arrived in Japan. Reference 1
Honestly, with ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Grok already available, there is a strong sense of 'Muse? Why?'
Now, how will Meta get people who have already decided on their generative AI to choose Muse?
Doesn't this competition feel like the battle between mobile carriers?
I personally use ChatGPT Pro and Grok's SuperGrok, paying over 20,000 yen a month in total. I have uses for each, such as creating materials and scripts for my side business, running machine learning systems for horse racing, and writing these articles for Note.
Just because Muse has arrived, will it become a third option? Do I want to use it in addition to the two I already have? Or do I want to quit one and switch?
At this timing? (Though if you think about it normally, it's early), Muse has started to look like Rakuten in the mobile carrier world. It's entering a market where people already have contracts as a new option. So, what reason will they give for people to choose them...
Will they win on response quality? Will they shake things up with price? Or will they bring a use case that no previous generative AI has had?
Taking Muse's expansion into Japan as an opportunity, I would like to think about the competition among generative AI services by comparing it to mobile carriers.
'Network quality' would generally correspond to the accuracy of the responses.
For mobile phones, the first thing you care about is network quality. Even if the price is incredibly cheap, it's trash if it doesn't connect.
If we replace that with generative AI, would it be the accuracy of the response?
Beyond response accuracy, there's the question of how long the wait time is, whether it can handle long tasks without stopping, whether it can handle files and link with external tools, and whether it works as intended.
Perhaps, recently, 'quality' might even include those things.
If we map them out roughly, it might look something like this.
For the work I entrust to it (for example, this machine learning system, etc.), if it seems like it will do an excellent job, it will be chosen preferentially.
However, when both are sufficient for everyday use, the reasons for choosing one should expand beyond just accuracy.
Mobile phones weren't chosen just by comparing network quality either.
Looking back at the history of mobile phones, there wasn't just one reason to choose a carrier.
Docomo's i-mode expanded uses beyond just voice calls. In 2008, SoftBank launched the iPhone 3G in Japan. Rakuten Mobile, which entered the market in earnest in 2020, introduced a 2,980 yen monthly plan and unlimited data within the Rakuten network area at the time. Reference 2 Reference 3 Reference 4
I want to use this service. I want that device. I want to lower my monthly bill. Even when choosing the same communication service, what people look for varies.
Even with generative AI, the model that solves the most difficult problems might not necessarily be the service everyone chooses.
Some people use expensive plans for difficult development, while others are satisfied if their daily research can be done for free. Some might prioritize being able to call it up from apps they already use, and perhaps it might even come down to the phrasing of the response text.
I feel that winning on model performance and getting people to choose a monthly subscription are gradually becoming two different battles.
Looking at the pricing plans, it already feels like a carrier.
How much can you use for free? What do you get if you upgrade to a higher plan? How much does the price go up when you want to use it a lot?
For example, Claude's Max plan offers choices for 5x or 20x the usage limit. ChatGPT also has different models, usage quotas, and features depending on the plan.
Isn't comparing these the same as thinking about 'how many GB do I need per month?'
Even if the monthly fee looks high, if you can use it without worrying about limits while working, there are people (like me) who would pay that amount. Conversely, if you only use it a few times a month, there is no need to sign up for a high-performance plan.
Value-added services are not just extras, but 'how much can you delegate?'
So, what is the value-added of generative AI?
There is the number of features, such as being able to create images or write code. However, I feel that what is more important than that is how much things can be completed using only generative AI.
For example, when asking it to 'plan next week's schedule.' Do you have to copy and paste your schedule every time, or can it refer to your authorized calendar and related documents? This difference cannot be understood just by comparing the text of the responses.
This is also reflected in how the pricing is structured. Google's generative AI plan combines not just Gemini usage quotas, but also storage used for Gmail, Google Drive, and Google Photos.Reference 5
It's not about how much you pay for generative AI alone, but thinking about it in combination with the services you use daily. This looks quite similar to the bundled sales of mobile carriers.
How much work can I stop doing as a result of being connected?
If things change that much, even if another generative AI is at a level that can solve unsolved problems, it might not be easy to switch.
Can Meta win from the 'Rakuten' position?
From the perspective of someone like me who has already decided which generative AI to use, Muse is a service that has entered the comparison pool later. In that sense, it looks like the position of Rakuten in the mobile carrier market. (Although they have technically been doing it for quite a while)
Will they shake things up with price like Rakuten? Or is there another path to victory?
Just because it's a latecomer doesn't mean it has to compete on the same playing field as existing services and answer every problem more intelligently. So where will it win, and does it have a killer app?
Will generative AI also have a 'main line'?
With ChatGPT, if you keep using the same generative AI for things like how to write these notes, horse racing system specifications, or side hustles, you'll want to use it for your next request as well.
So, what happens when you decide to switch to another generative AI service? If you get banned, you have to explain the premises from scratch.
What we might actually want to carry over with a generative AI version of MNP (Mobile Number Portability) isn't a phone number, but our 'memory'.
It's not like you can't use others if you choose one generative AI company. There's also the way of using a generative AI as the center of your work, and another when you want a different perspective. I think there's a way to separate them into main and sub.
However, there is a limit to how many monthly subscriptions you can keep paying.
If I were to use Muse, would I want to add it as a third option? Or could I see myself quitting one of the ones I'm currently using? For me, that's the point of interest. (By the way, I thought it was the free version of Dots, so I closed it immediately.)
From 'which generative AI is the smartest' to 'which generative AI will I keep paying for every month'.
I believe the competition in generative AI is gradually reaching a point similar to choosing a mobile carrier.
