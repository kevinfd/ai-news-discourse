# This Economist Sees 'No Signs of AI Payoff' Beyond the Major Tech Firms

PCMag · Jon Martindale · 2026-10-27

As Anthropic moves to go public and Microsoft floats voluntary exit offers to tens of thousands of employees to help it pay for compute costs, you might be wondering what the AI endgame is. One economist doesn't see much benefit yet beyond the major tech firms.
In a recent blog post, Torsten Slok, a partner at Apollo Global Management, notes that while revenue per employee at the "Magnificent 7" companies has risen by around 30% over the past four years, other companies have been relatively unaffected, Bloomberg reports. "So far, there is no evidence that revenue growth or margins are improving outside the tech sector," he writes. "And there are no signs of AI boosting productivity growth."
The big sell to businesses for the "Artificial Intelligence" large language models that power the likes of ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini is that they can help workers do more in less time. But Slok argues that outside Silicon Valley, the benefit is negligible at the moment.
Comparing the revenue per employee of Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia, and Tesla with the rest of the S&P 500, Slok shows clear growth at those tech companies, but stagnation elsewhere. The same is true for profit margins over an even longer 11-year period.
You could argue that these companies are more likely to benefit from AI; they're all selling it, just as much as they are utilizing it. But then we should at least see productivity increase for other business sectors that are investing in AI tools. Slok, however, suggests productivity growth is down. Following an initial spike in worker output per hour in early 2023, perhaps attributable to the early adoption of AI in highly affected industries such as programming, Total Factor Productivity (a comparison of an industry's total output to the combined inputs used to produce it) has since fallen to zero.
Slok concludes from this data that while major venture capital was investing in tokens, mainstream corporate America now sees AI use as a cost that weighs on its profits, rather than accelerates them. Although he suggests there may be a future in which models become affordable enough to be used on a grander scale, he sees that as unlikely because of the inherent flaws in the design of LLM-derived artificial intelligence.
"AI does not understand accountability, trust, culture, unwritten rules, ethical trade-offs, or fairness, all of which shape reputational risk and ultimately drive revenue growth and profit margins," he says. "The bottom line is that the AI story will be decided in the S&P 493, not the Mag 7, and so far, there is no evidence that revenue growth or margins are improving outside the tech sector."
