# Sheridan residents weigh risks, regulations surrounding AI data centers

Wyoming Tribune Eagle · Georgia Lodewyk The Sheridan Press · 2026-10-27

SHERIDAN — Residents expressed few upsides to data center development in the Sheridan region, raising concerns about its potential impacts during an evening community forum last week hosted by the Wyoming Outdoor Council.
“I’m tired of the little man getting screwed over,” said forum attendee Brent Peterman. “So the big people, who are already on top, can buy another (expletive) yacht.”
About 30 people gathered in Sheridan Junior High School’s cafeteria to discuss the future of data centers in Sheridan County, particularly hyperscale data centers that use multiple gigawatts of energy — a unit of power equivalent to one billion watts.
While data centers have existed for more than 30 years, Jocelyn Wulf, WOC’s data center campaign manager, said hyperscale data centers are more commonly referred to as artificial intelligence data centers. They support AI and cloud services and are usually backed by large tech companies or colocation firms.
An example is Google’s Project Tembo campus planned in Laramie County.
“We’re seeing these hyperscale data centers use as much or more power as all of the homes and all of the businesses in Wyoming combined,” Wulf said.
The WOC’s forum included a presentation and listening session for Sheridan County residents to discuss potential impacts, current regulations and long-term solutions on the legislative level and in individual communities. The conservation advocacy group has visited six other communities around the state, and has also brought forward a petition to demand the government to create proactive, responsible guardrails to hold large corporations accountable.
Wulf said she doesn’t know of any current proposals to build a data center in Sheridan County, but she and other members of the WOC said communities should have a proactive game plan in place.
“This conversation really was for a long time happening in Laramie County,” said WOC’s energy and climate policy director John Burrows. “In the last two years things have started to change … we’re seeing proposals that are much bigger in terms of dollars and size and energy around the state.”
Data center impacts
Wulf identified six areas of impact from data center development: water, energy, air quality, noise, heat and wildlife impacts. During the listening session, Sheridan County residents identified water and wildlife impacts as two of the biggest concerns.
Wulf said many hyperscale data centers are being built with a closed-loop cooling system that allows its water mixture to run through for several years, thereby conserving water sources. But she is concerned these companies are able to operate with very little transparency about water quality and safety. She cited an incident in Cheyenne last July when Meta’s in-progress data center released a discharge that introduced a rare bacteria into the city’s wastewater treatment system.
“It was not Meta who caught that. It was not the contractor. It was Cheyenne’s Board of Public Utilities,” Wulf said. “I think that example really serves to highlight the need for additional transparency and reporting requirements for data centers.”
For wildlife, Wulf said data center facilities are not required to use wildlife-friendly fencing; and, like many large industrial developments, it could damage migration corridors.
While Sheridan residents did not respond with any impacts during the listening session, the WOC discussed potential jobs created from data center facilities.
Most local jobs would be created during the construction process, but Wulf said “only a handful” of people will run a fully-operational data center.
Current regulations
The Wyoming Department of Environmental Quality’s Industrial Siting Division assesses socio-economic and environmental impacts for companies planning major industrial developments, like data centers.
The Industrial Siting Act requires a rigorous, multi-month process under state review. But Wulf said there’s a loophole: if a developer sites in a preexisting city or county industrial park, it does not have to go through the ISA review process.
“When they site these preexisting industrial zones, the burden for review then falls on the local governments,” Wulf said. “Despite their best intentions, (local governmental entities) have far more limited capacity for thorough review.”
In June, Gov. Mark Gordon signed the executive order, Data Centers the Wyoming Way, outlining a set of principles to make Wyoming competitive in attracting investment while also protecting residents.
However, because it is an executive order and not a Wyoming law, it directs state agencies on how to move forward with data center development. It does not have the power to enforce or regulate developers coming into the state.
Data center developers that do choose to build in Wyoming do have the perks of plenty of land, low energy prices, little regulation and additional sales tax exemptions.
According to the Wyoming Business Council, data centers are exempt from sales tax if they spend $5 million in capital infrastructure and a $2 million or larger investment in data center equipment and software, as outlined in Wyoming Statute 39-15-105. With these policies in place, Wulf believes Wyoming is missing out on millions of dollars in revenue.
“That sales and use tax exemption repeal is something we’d be really excited to see worked on in this legislative session,” she said.
Potential solutions
The WOC said its petition would advocate for more stringent statewide protections and ensure potential data center developers will comply with residents’ wishes. Wulf said the WOC is working on model legislation to outline certain protections and guidelines they believe are imperative in moving forward. She said these plans are in the preliminary draft phases.
Sheridan resident and Powder River Basin Resource Council Executive Director Donna Birkholz agreed that Wyoming is falling behind when it comes to data center regulation, and the upcoming legislative session is an opportunity to correct that.
“I think most legislators have been caught flat-footed,” Birkholz said. “When you’re got billion-dollar industries, trillion-dollar industries building them, it’s hard for legislators to catch up.”
Wulf believes other regulations and protections can happen at the city and county levels. Community benefit agreements are a legally-binding contract between a community and a developer.
“The best time for a community to engage in a community benefit agreement is before a project has been approved, before a project has been cited, while that community still has the leverage to have a seat at the table,” Wulf said.
The community of Lancaster, Pennsylvania, approved a Community Benefit Agreement in November 2025, prior to the construction of two AI campuses. In the agreement, Lancaster demanded $10 million to an economic development fund, $10 million to sustainable development and clean energy, along with water use limits, 100% clean-energy commitments and ways to enforce noncompliance, like interest on overdue payments.
Could Wyoming implement something similar in its communities? Wulf said it’s possible.
“We do think that now is the time,” Wulf said. “We think the time is right to push for this, to push for a seat at the table and ask for the protections that we think Wyoming needs.”
