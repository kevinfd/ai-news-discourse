# The AI Cost Question Every Leader Should Be Asking

Forbes · Hanah-Marie Darley, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-27

Hanah-Marie Darley, Co-Founder and Chief AI Officer, Geordie.
The invoice is only the beginning.
Spend is the natural starting point for any conversation about AI cost. What I’ve found to be the more useful question is what that spend enabled: What was the agent trying to achieve, and was the outcome worth the resources involved?
AI cost is moving from a technology line item to an operating question as agentic work becomes the new normal in the enterprise. That is a welcome development. As agents research, analyze, build and communicate across the enterprise, their work is beginning to shape how teams allocate time, resources and attention.
Most organizations can already see model invoices, token consumption and aggregate usage. Those measures answer an important accounting question, but they do not yet explain what the organization achieved from that spend.
A higher bill could reflect an agent completing a complex piece of analysis that would otherwise take a team days. It could also reflect a task that repeated itself, selected a more expensive model than necessary or called tools without moving the work forward. The number alone cannot tell an executive which is which.
This is why one of the most useful units of AI cost is work.
Work gives cost its context.
I find the unit of work offers a practical path from spend to a decision. It allows leaders to ask what an agent was trying to accomplish, what resources it used, how it approached the task and what happened as a result.
The model is only one part of that picture. The cost of an agent’s work is shaped by its behavior, including the decisions it makes, the tools and information it uses, the retries it requires and any human intervention along the way. Those elements matter because they explain the economics of the work, not because they are interesting technical details in isolation.
An agent that uses a premium model for a high-value, time-sensitive decision may be making an entirely sensible trade-off. The same choice for routine classification work may deserve a different design. The point is not to impose one spending threshold across every agent, but to make the trade-offs visible enough for the business to judge them in context.
That distinction also changes the conversation about return on investment. ROI is rarely a single universal number for agentic work. One team may value time saved, while another may value accuracy, throughput, response time or the ability to take on work that previously had no practical owner.
Connecting cost to work gives each team a way to assess value against the outcomes it actually cares about. This also changes who needs access to AI cost data. Traditional FinOps models often center on budgets, allocation and shared infrastructure. Agentic work distributes the decision. In flatter organizations, the teams closest to a workflow may need to understand the spend their agents create to forecast what they can deliver in a quarter. They are not simply tracking consumption. They are making decisions about capacity, priorities and where agents can take on more responsibility.
Leaders need a framework for the economics of agentic work.
Leaders do not need a new dashboard for every new metric. They need a repeatable way to follow the relationship between investment and activity. A useful sequence is: spend, agent, work, behavior, resources and outcome; then observe over time to optimize.
Start with spend. What did this work cost? Then identify the responsible agent and the work it was trying to complete. Review the behavior that created the cost, including the path it took through models, tools and systems. Consider the resources involved, from compute, data access and human review. Finally, look at the outcome in the terms that matter for that use case.
This sequence helps make cost conversations more precise. It separates a high-cost workflow that is delivering clear business value from one that needs design attention. It also makes ownership easier to establish. Finance, technology and business leaders can discuss the same unit of work rather than reconciling disconnected views of usage, budgets and outcomes.
What can leaders do now?
The first step to operationalize this is to choose a small number of agent workflows that already matter to the business. For each one, define the intended outcome and the person or team accountable for deciding whether that outcome justifies the resources used.
Next, make the work traceable. Cost data needs enough operational context to show which agent generated it, what task it was performing and how the work unfolded. This does not require every executive to inspect execution detail. It gives the people closest to the work a common evidence base for explaining material patterns.
Finally, use the evidence to make a decision. Expand work that is proving valuable. Improve workflows where the cost is out of proportion to the outcome. Change model choices, tool use or task design where the evidence supports it. Give agents more responsibility where the economics are clear.
The aim is to understand the economics of agentic work well enough to invest with intent. As agents become a foundational part of how the enterprise works, that understanding will become a core management advantage.
