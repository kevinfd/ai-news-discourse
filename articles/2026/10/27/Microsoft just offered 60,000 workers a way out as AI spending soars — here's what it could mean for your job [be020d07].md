# Microsoft just offered 60,000 workers a way out as AI spending soars — here's what it could mean for your job

Tom's Guide · Amanda Caswell · 2026-10-27

Microsoft just offered 60,000 workers a way out as AI spending soars — here's what it could mean for your job
Corporations are pouring billions of dollars into AI, causing mass layoffs
Microsoft is offering roughly 60,000 employees a chance to leave the company, and the timing is hard to ignore. As the tech giant pours billions into AI, it's giving more than a quarter of its workforce the option to take a buyout.
But while it's easy to blame AI, it's important to understand that it hasn't necessarily replaced these Microsoft employees. The buyout offers are voluntary, and the company hasn't disclosed how many people it expects to leave or whether AI played a direct role in the decision.
Still, the announcement raises an uncomfortable question for anyone watching AI reshape the workplace. If one of the world's biggest AI investors is rethinking its workforce, what could that mean for your job?
New research suggests the changes may already be underway, although not necessarily in the way people expect. Instead of widespread layoffs, some of the earliest signs are showing up in hiring, particularly among younger workers.
What's happening at Microsoft?
On October 26, Microsoft announced a Voluntary Exit Offer for eligible employees in the United States, Canada, India and the United Kingdom.
The program allows employees in participating teams at level 70 and below to leave with financial support and career-planning resources. Not every division is included, and participation is optional.
According to CRN, Microsoft confirmed that approximately 60,000 employees qualify, representing about 27% of its 223,000-person global workforce.

In her announcement, Microsoft's chief people officer Amy Coleman said the program would give participating organizations more flexibility to adapt their workforces to changing needs.
The decision comes as Microsoft continues investing heavily in AI infrastructure, including the data centers and computing power required to run increasingly sophisticated models.
But the timing doesn't establish a direct connection between AI spending and the buyout offers. Microsoft has not said these employees are being replaced by AI, and there's no indication that all 60,000 will leave.
Which jobs are most vulnerable to AI?
The workers facing the biggest changes aren't necessarily those doing the most technical jobs. They're often people whose responsibilities involve repetitive, predictable tasks that AI can increasingly perform.
In a March 2026 study, Anthropic examined occupations using a combination of AI capabilities and real-world usage data.
Computer programmers, customer service representatives and financial analysts were among the occupations with substantial exposure to AI. Their work includes tasks such as writing code, answering routine questions, analyzing documents and processing information.
Anthropic found no systematic increase in unemployment among workers in highly exposed occupations, although it identified signs that hiring of younger workers may be slowing.
Stanford researchers found a similar pattern. In an August 2026 study examining payroll data from millions of U.S. workers, researchers found that employment among people ages 22 to 25 in highly AI-exposed occupations was 19% below where it would have been if it had grown at the same rate as employment among their less-exposed peers.
Again, that doesn't mean AI eliminated 19% of those jobs. The researchers found that much of the difference came from slower hiring rather than workers being laid off. They also cautioned that their findings don't prove AI caused the entire employment gap.
The research highlights that workers whose jobs rely heavily on documented knowledge and repeatable procedures may be more exposed than those whose expertise comes from experience, relationships and judgment.
For employees, the message isn't necessarily that their entire profession is at risk. It's that the tasks employers need them to perform are changing, and entry-level opportunities may be particularly vulnerable.
3 ways to protect your career as AI changes the workplace
There's no guaranteed way to make a job immune to automation. But the research suggests workers can take practical steps to become more adaptable as employers change their expectations.
1. Learn to use AI in your current job
One of the simplest ways to prepare is to become comfortable using the technology that's changing your industry.
That doesn't mean learning to code or becoming an AI expert. It means understanding how tools such as ChatGPT, Claude and Microsoft Copilot can help with the work you already do.
An administrative assistant might use AI to organize meeting notes, while someone in marketing could use it to analyze campaign feedback. A financial professional might use it to summarize reports before checking the figures independently.
There's evidence that these skills are increasingly valuable. According to LinkedIn's 2026 labor-market report, U.S. job postings requiring AI literacy skills grew 70% year over year.
What you can do today: Choose one repetitive task and experiment with using an approved AI tool to complete it. Compare the results with your usual approach, check for errors and avoid sharing confidential company information.
2. Build skills that go beyond what AI can automate
AI can summarize documents, write emails and generate reports, but completing a task isn't the same as understanding how it fits into a larger business decision.
Stanford's research found that experienced workers generally fared better than younger employees in AI-exposed occupations. The researchers also observed stronger employment patterns in roles that depend more heavily on knowledge developed through practice and experience.
That suggests there is value in developing skills that aren't easily captured in a set of instructions, including problem-solving, communication, leadership and the ability to make decisions when the answer isn't obvious.
What you can do today: Look for opportunities to take ownership of projects, work directly with customers or solve problems that require coordination with other people. These experiences can help you build expertise beyond routine tasks.
3. Show employers the value you bring
As AI changes how quickly work can be completed, simply being able to produce more may not be enough to stand out.
Instead, focus on the results of your work. That might mean improving customer satisfaction, reducing unnecessary costs, helping a team adopt a useful tool or identifying a problem before it becomes expensive.
Keep track of those accomplishments. If your organization restructures or you decide to look for another job, you'll have concrete examples of the value you bring beyond a list of daily responsibilities.
What you can do today: Write down three recent accomplishments and the impact they had. Where possible, include measurable outcomes such as time saved, revenue generated or improvements in customer satisfaction.
The takeaway
Microsoft's announcement arrives at a time when research is beginning to show changes in how employers hire, particularly for jobs involving tasks that AI can automate.
For workers, the immediate concern should be more about whether the skills that got them hired will be enough to keep them competitive. By learning to work with AI, building experience that goes beyond routine tasks and demonstrating measurable results won't guarantee job security, employees can gain a practical starting point as the workplace continues to change.
