# Democrats prepare to battle over AI regulation

Transformer · Shakeel Hashim · 2026-10-09

NEED TO KNOW

But first…
THE BIG STORY
If Democrats retake both the House and Senate next month — as looks increasingly likely — they will be in the not-so-enviable position of having to figure out how to regulate AI. In preparation, many are planting a stake in the ground: in the past few weeks, a plethora of legislative ideas has been put forward, each making a claim to be the way forward for AI legislation.
One fear among the party is that Republicans will try to use the lame-duck session to ram through a bill of their choosing. That could be Sens. John Thune and Amy Klobuchar’s reported bill, though that appears to be a long way from a deal. It could also be Reps. Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan’s FRONTIER Act, which was partly endorsed by House Energy and Commerce Chair Brett Guthrie this week. Many Democrats, however, view that bill as a nonstarter thanks to its preemption provisions — while party leadership takes particular umbrage with the fact Trahan went over their heads in proposing it.
Dems know that if they want to avoid momentum building behind these bills, they have to provide an alternative. So that’s what they’ve started doing. At The Curve conference last week, Reps. Don Beyer and Sara Jacobs teased a bill which would establish a standard-setting federal agency and an “emergency switch” to allow the government to shut down models. (Both said they disliked FRONTIER because of preemption.) And this week, Sen. Maria Cantwell — who will chair the Commerce Committee if Dems take the Senate — published a legislative framework calling for “constant oversight” of frontier models by both the government and independent experts. Notably, it too does not discuss preemption.
Others are taking different approaches. Trahan published a liability-focused bill this week, while Sen. Ruben Gallego put out a “jobs plan” that calls for a “New Deal-style public service jobs program” to address AI-driven unemployment. On the more extreme end, there’s Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. Greg Casar’s Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act — a radical position that is nevertheless gaining traction.
None of these bills are going to get passed this year, and even if Democrats control Congress, nothing will get passed at all unless they can convince some Republicans to peel off and vote with them — something much easier said than done. But for all its faults, Congress is finally seriously considering a wide range of legislative options to deal with AI.
— Shakeel Hashim

THE DISCOURSE
Sam Altman doesn’t seem to love Anthropic’s work with religious scholars:
- “I am very uncomfortable about people trying to ascribe religious force or a surrender of human judgment to AI models, and think it is a real safety issue.”
Altman also made a surprisingly candid statement about AI risks:
- “We believe that the world should accept some bad things happening for the benefits of [AI] and people having the agency.”
OpenAI safety team member David Robinson quit, lambasting the company’s culture in a resignation essay:
- “My former colleagues are smart, work hard, and try to make good choices. But as the company sprints from one launch to the next, it is failing to achieve the level of care that I believe is needed.”
- “Given today’s risks, frontier labs need to run like nuclear-power plants or busy airports, with layers of redundancy and careful, time-consuming planning, so that the occasional and inevitable human error does not open a door to disaster.”
Dean Ball corrected the record about alignment research:
- “The “alignment *to whom*?” question IS important, but it creates the illusion that the primary questions in alignment are philosophical or political rather than scientific. The current high-order question in alignment is, “can you align a smarter-than-human intelligence *to any set of values*?” instead of “but whose values should it be aligned to?” I wish we had the luxury of having the rather more familiar political/philosophical debate of “to whom?,” but at this moment we do not.”
Adam Thierer said Sen. Cantwell’s resistance to preemption was a non-starter:
- “...there will be no compromise or effective national AI governance framework unless she is willing to get serious about preemption of state AI frontier model regulation.”
Anthropic researcher Justin Curl had a theory for China’s relative opposition to AI safety:
- “The CCP is less safety-pilled than the US because it has stronger control mechanisms.”
Google DeepMind’s Séb Krier offered 20 interesting takes on AI:
- “[I’m] disappointed that parts of the AI policy ecosystem has felt so comfortable with populism, and happy [to] sacrifice important norms/principles to advance their aims.”
- “Many critics of AI safety also fail to really engage honestly with the fact that there will, in fact, be all sorts of important risks as we decrease the barriers to entry to many activities that otherwise require some degree of expertise.”
- “Too much of the AI safety community is a giant homogenous blob of correlated views, who read the same stuff and have the same groups of friends. This means you have a lot of correlated errors.”

INDUSTRY
OpenAI
- Three fired OpenAI researchers wrote a letter to OpenAI boardmembers and safety committees urging the company to preserve AI chain-of-thought monitoring and work with outside safety auditors. 
  - The three researchers, Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak and Mikita Balesni, who had worked on safety and alignment teams, said in the letter that their firing was “chilling those who remain at OpenAI.”
  - Each offered more detail on what OpenAI told them when firing them: 
    - Jasmine Wang: “I was given one reason: that I accessed an executive’s email … OpenAI delegated that access to me for recruiting. When I no longer needed it, I asked IT to remove it. They did not action my request, I couldn’t remove it myself … When I opened a sensitive email by mistake, I told the executive within minutes and asked IT again.”
    - Mikita Balesni: “In the exit call, I was told OpenAI no longer trusts me because I was speaking too much to third party safety organizations, implying I leaked company IP. I never shared company IP.”
    - Tomek Korbak: “I was told verbally I was fired because of the way I communicated with METR. No details on what I said or did or when. No other reasons were given and nothing was put in writing. To be clear, talking to METR was my job.”
  - OpenAI said they were fired for “a significant breach of trust beyond what’s outlined in the letter they published,” and that “these decisions were not about them raising safety concerns.”
