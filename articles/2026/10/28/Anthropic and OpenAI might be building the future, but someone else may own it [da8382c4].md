# Anthropic and OpenAI might be building the future, but someone else may own it

Fast Company · Naomi Haefner · 2026-10-28

Wall Street is busy preparing for what could become the three biggest initial public offerings in history. SpaceX has already gone public, and Anthropic and OpenAI are expected to follow suit. Yet even as investors scramble to get in, doubts are growing over whether these frontier AI labs can ever generate enough value to justify their astronomical spending.
The sheer scale of it is just staggering. Anthropic lost more than $8 billion last year despite generating $4.6 billion of revenue, and has already committed more than $500 billion to the computing power and artificial intelligence infrastructure it will need to keep growing in the years ahead.
OpenAI, meanwhile, expects revenue to rise almost tenfold—from $36 billion this year to $350 billion by 2030—yet still projects a cumulative cash burn of nearly $280 billion over the same period.
On the face of it, the economics are ugly. Even so, Anthropic’s investors believe the company could float at a valuation of more than $2 trillion. But those backers are looking in the wrong place. Frontier AI labs may never be the biggest winners of the AI race they ignited.

That’s because the companies that invent breakthrough technologies rarely capture the biggest chunk of the value the techologies generate. This value usually accrues to those who build the complementary assets, in this case the products, platforms, and services around them. And that means the frontier AI labs could end up creating enormous value without capturing most of it themselves.
History is not on the side of these labs. In the early twentieth century, electricity came to transform pretty much every industry. But it wasn’t the power generators who gained the most; the biggest productivity gains came from manufacturing companies like Ford that used it to revolutionize production. The risk is the AI labs end up like utility companies, providing a shared foundation that others profit from.
The internet was similar. Telecoms companies spent heavily fashioning the fiber networks and connectivity that made the web possible. But much of the long-term value went to the businesses that built services on top of it: search engines, e-commerce and social media platforms, plus cloud software.
