# The Real Reason AI Isn’t Delivering Business Value

Forbes · Dhiraj Rajaram, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-28

Dhiraj Rajaram, is the Founder & CEO of Mu Sigma and a tech visionary. Mu Sigma is one of India’s early unicorns.
Every day, it seems there is a new study highlighting that AI deployments haven’t paid off for businesses. None of this should be surprising.
AI is great. The technology is impressive and gets better every day. The problem with AI, however, is not the technology itself. Rather, it’s the unaddressed organizational deficiencies that limit an organization’s ability to take real advantage of it. Unfortunately, these are much bigger, more structural issues that need to be addressed. Organizations are good at capturing what happened but much weaker at preserving how reality was perceived, why decisions were made and what was learned. AI is exposing that gap, not creating it.
Here’s what I mean.
Spaghettification
A core problem limiting AI’s value in an organization is the unaddressed complexity that any large organization experiences. As a Fortune 500 company becomes more and more successful, it adopts layers and layers of unaddressed complexity. This creates what I call the “spaghettification” of the organization.
Take, for example, a large footwear brand that has expanded into new channels or geographies. It will have more transactional data as a result, which is good. But insights into buying patterns become increasingly fragmented, making measurement and progress tracking difficult. Of course, this spaghettification came about for valid reasons. It came from success, and great companies are the most spaghettified. But it also creates complexity at the data layer, the IT layer and, most importantly, the insights layer.
It’s very hard for new technology to land in a spaghettified world. Infrastructure, standards and better data tagging matter, but they only go so far. Even with clean data, organizations can lose the assumptions, interactions and judgment behind decisions. The problem is not just fragmented data; it’s also fragmented context.
Outdated Thinking
We grew up in a world that was far more certain than the one we live in today, and our problem-solving mechanisms came from that world of certainty. Today’s world of uncertainty requires a new problem-solving paradigm. This is where I like to talk about ROO.
In the old world, one current state had one future state, so you could use ROI as a governing principle. Now, one current state has many possible future states. ROI still matters, but under uncertainty, it is not enough on its own. We also need ROO: return on options. The only answer to uncertainty is optionality.
An oil and gas company that uses AI to test multiple ways of predicting equipment failure—that’s ROO. Take a large energy producer deciding where to put billions in capital next, for example: how much to drill, where to invest, which pipeline or refinery to expand. Instead of picking one forecast, it can simulate aggressive and conservative investment scenarios side by side, surfacing which one drains reserves too fast or strains pipeline capacity before either shows up on the P&L. That’s ROO in action. Rather than placing one bet, the company places several, with the option to scale what works best and adapt as problems inevitably change.
That kind of nonlinear thinking is hard for organizations to accept, let alone implement. And, as a result, AI investments suffer. The old world was linear. The new world can’t be. You have to be a little nonlinear in your thinking.
Bad Problem Design
Relatedly, a large part of AI failures comes from companies not spending enough time defining the problem and jumping the gun on using AI. Human beings often do not put enough emphasis on problem design.
As everyone talks about AI’s potential, we dive in and move too quickly. We jump into problem-solving without design and without thinking through how problems are interconnected. AI can only reason over the problem it is given. If that representation is incomplete, it can produce a sophisticated answer to the wrong question.
Consider this example. We worked with a Fortune 50 bank to improve the customer experience on its website. We didn’t start with AI. We identified 150 potential problem areas, then narrowed them down to 14 that were both priorities and solvable. AI was part of the solution, but good problem design had to come first.
A Field Of Context
Most organizations are excellent at capturing data in rows and columns but far worse at capturing the context around it. So, how do we fix this? As is the case with any problem, it’s complicated. But we need to start by understanding what I call the “field of context.”
A field of context helps us see the organization as a whole. It makes the connections hidden by spaghettification more visible. It helps us move beyond linear problem-solving and forces us to better define the problem before jumping to a solution. That means understanding not just what happened (what I call the action trace) but also the decisions and processes that produced it (the decision trace), as well as the purpose, observations and conditions that shaped those decisions (the perception trace).
Let’s look at that energy producer again. The action trace is the capital decision itself—how much was invested and where. The decision trace is the reasoning behind it—the reserve economics, pipeline capacity and other levers that were weighed. The perception trace is the assumptions in play at the time about demand, policy or geopolitics that shaped the call. Understanding all three together is what turns a pile of data into a usable field of context.
By connecting perception, decision and action, we give AI the context it needs to understand how problems are interconnected. Over time, that context becomes organizational memory, helping turn experience into better judgment. Intelligence may be increasingly abundant, but judgment remains scarce. If we want AI to deliver real value, organizations need to preserve what they did, why they did it and what they learned.
