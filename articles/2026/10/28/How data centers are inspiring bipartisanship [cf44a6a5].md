# How data centers are inspiring bipartisanship

Fast Company · The Conversation · 2026-10-28

It may not be as shocking as rogue AI agents hacking another system, but the current political resistance to data centers has taken a surprising turn: It’s bringing together strange bedfellows, from democratic socialist Sen. Bernie Sanders to right-wing strategist Steve Bannon.
Organized opposition to new data centers, made up of a coalition of varied interests, has spread across the nation. Through protests, packed zoning commission meetings, moratoriums, and other government action, the resistance has upended dozens of proposed projects.
Data centers are key components in artificial intelligence infrastructure, housing the computing resources used to train AI models. They’re also massive facilities that require extraordinary amounts of energy. By 2030, U.S. data center electricity use is expected to reach 12% of the nation’s total consumption.
They carry some benefits. At the national level, data centers fuel the computing power seen as critical to U.S. competition with China. Locally, they create an uptick in jobs, especially during the construction phase, and can generate tax revenue. In the lead-up to the 2026 midterm elections, though, the heavy costs associated with them—such as land use, higher household utility bills, and environmental degradation—appear to outweigh the benefits, bringing together people who usually find themselves politically at odds.
I’m a professor of political science who studies political parties, electoral politics, and technology. My forthcoming book, Democratic Intelligence: Navigating Electoral Politics in an AI World, examines how the adoption of AI in political campaigns is reshaping established practices and power dynamics. I believe data centers may be disrupting some of the partisan issue alignments Americans have grown to expect.
Political disruption—AI style
Despite ongoing debate about the extent of and reasons for polarization, one thing is for sure: Democrats and Republicans stake out distinct positions on many issues. Pew Research Center data shows, for example, that 63% of Democrats say climate change is a very big problem facing the country, compared with 14% of Republicans, a 49-point gap.
By contrast, the partisan gap on data centers is small—and getting smaller: In summer 2026, the Annenberg Public Policy Center asked Americans whether they support the construction of data centers in their communities. Supporters of both parties were more opposed than supportive, although opposition was stronger among Democrats, at 69%, than Republicans, at 54%. That partisan gap had narrowed over the previous four months, moving from 18 to 15 points.
