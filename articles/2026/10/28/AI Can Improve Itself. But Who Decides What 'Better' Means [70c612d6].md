# AI Can Improve Itself. But Who Decides What 'Better' Means?

Forbes · Vahe Andonians, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-28

Vahe Andonians is the founder and CEO at Cognaize.
AI is becoming part of its own development process. It can improve software, find more efficient ways to use computing resources and help improve how new models are built. Those gains can make the next round of development faster and more effective.
That is a real improvement loop but not self-sufficient alone. It still depends on goals, tests and feedback that come from outside the system.
An AI can get better at reaching a target without knowing whether that target is worth pursuing. It can improve its test results without delivering a better real-world outcome.
For business leaders, the opportunity is to automate more of the work of improvement. The responsibility is to decide what counts as progress, and ensure the system is being measured against it.
The score is not the goal.
Every improvement loop needs a way to distinguish progress from activity. In coding, that might mean passing a test. In operations, it might mean lower costs.
Suppose a customer-service system is rewarded for closing cases quickly. It could improve that metric while making customers feel dismissed. A coding agent could pass the available tests while introducing a vulnerability those tests do not cover.
The management problem is familiar: A target can become a substitute for the purpose it was meant to serve.
A system that rapidly generates and tests alternatives may discover weaknesses in a poorly designed measure faster than a human team. It does not need to share the organization’s broader purpose to optimize the number it has been given.
The right response is not to abandon measurement. It is to treat the design of the measurement as seriously as the design of the system.
Before asking an AI to improve something, leaders should be able to answer two questions: What outcome do we actually want? And what could get worse while our chosen score gets better?
Faster execution does not choose the destination.
Some problems have clear boundaries. A calculation is correct or incorrect. A program meets a specification, or it does not. Those are promising places to automate improvement, although the specification’s quality matters.
Other decisions are less straightforward. Which customer problem deserves investment? Which product should be built? Is a faster process worth losing reliability? What should the company refuse to optimize, even if doing so would increase short-term returns?
While AI can help explore these questions, a person still needs to own the decision.
As execution becomes faster, a poorly chosen objective can introduce intolerable risks before anyone notices. That makes judgment more important, not less. Leaders may spend less time directing each task, but they still need to determine which tasks matter and what evidence would justify continuing them.
An AI system can take the task. It should not get to rewrite the goal.
Keep reality in the loop.
A system can rewrite an answer, critique it and rewrite it again. The result may become smoother without becoming more accurate. If it keeps drawing from its own outputs and evaluating them through the same assumptions, repetition can start to look similar to confirmation.
Businesses have their own version of this problem. Internal presentations cite other internal presentations, and eventually, everyone agrees—until a customer, competitor or operational failure supplies information the organization was not looking for.
The same applies to AI. Outside results catch failures that internal checks miss. People don’t need to approve every action, but they must have a way to catch errors and correct course.
AI-generated material can still be useful. The risk grows when it is reused without fresh information or independent checks, allowing mistakes to carry from one round of work to the next.
The aim should be less unnecessary human involvement, not less contact with reality.
Build the capacity to judge.
The evaluation must be the core investment and not just a final check squeezed in before deployment.
Begin with the business outcome that matters. Constraints are as important as the metrics used to measure progress. Lower operating costs, for instance, are not a win if service quality drops below the agreed standard.
Then, separate the generation from verification. AI endorsing a change should not be the only basis for trust. Use independent tests and review proportionate to the consequences of failure. Keep some tests outside the optimization process so the system is not rewarded only on challenges it has repeatedly practiced.
Finally, make changes reversible by saving versions and defining when to roll back. A process that can change fast also needs a suitable way to stop changing when performance deteriorates.
Someone must own evaluation, with resources to test claims and authority to challenge a rollout when the evidence does not support it.
The next competitive advantage may belong not simply to the companies generating the most ideas, but to those that can most reliably distinguish useful improvements from convincing mistakes.
The human role changes, not disappears.
Self-improving AI should change how leaders think about oversight. Neither supervising every action nor stepping away entirely is a meaningful approach.
Instead, use AI’s speed to explore within clear boundaries defined by people who remain accountable for purpose, standards and consequences.
The loop is real. The leadership challenge is not to close it at any cost. It is to make sure every turn still connects to reality.
