# Autonomous Mind | Are data centers the new AI bogeyman?

Colorado Politics · Thelma Grimes · 2026-10-28

Autonomous Mind | Are data centers the new AI bogeyman?
Editor’s note: Artificial intelligence is advancing at a pace that lawmakers, regulators and even some of its creators struggle to keep up. While Congress debates oversight frameworks and ethical standards, leading technology companies are deploying increasingly powerful systems capable of learning, adapting and improving with minimal human intervention. This series examines the growing divide between the speed of innovation and the pace of governance, exploring the risks, opportunities and unanswered questions surrounding a technology that is rapidly becoming embedded in every aspect of modern life.
Scroll through social media, make a video call, check a bank balance or ask a smartphone to find the fastest route home. In an emergency, call 911.

Each action may take only seconds. But behind that seemingly effortless experience is a vast network of physical infrastructure, notably data centers that store, process and move the information upon which modern life depends.
Colorado currently has 68 data centers, 47 in the Denver metro area, with 17 more proposed or under development.
Now, as artificial intelligence races forward, some of that infrastructure is drawing both heightened criticism and support.
Across Colorado, critics have raised questions about electricity demand, water consumption, air quality, noise, economic incentives and the effects of industrial development on neighboring communities. Supporters, meanwhile, have pointed to jobs, revenue, economic well-being and national security, worried that, if the U.S. shirks from the AI race, China will fill that void.
Denver’s decision to temporarily pause certain data center development has added to the broader debate over how quickly to build the facilities and what safeguards should come first. After Denver’s moratorium on new data center construction, other Colorado cities and counties have followed suit, though other communities have also embraced, with some even offering tax incentives.
Adding to the national debate, states like Texas and New York have signed executive orders halting data center development to audit its impact on electricity and water infrastructure.
The debate raises a larger question: Are data centers becoming the fall guy for public anxiety about artificial intelligence — a technology whose rapid advancement has left many people uncertain about what comes next?
In fact, data centers are essential to the digital economy and the infrastructure at the center of the debate has supported everyday digital life for decades. Yet their growing size and resource demands have raised worries about how to distribute their costs and benefits.
“Data centers have become the physical manifestation of AI,” said Megan Ruskowski, vice president of marketing for CoreSite, a Denver-based data center operator. She said public concern about AI, including fears that it could eliminate jobs, has shaped how people view the facilities.
Ruskowski’s observation points to a disconnect: People can interact with artificial intelligence through a screen, but the physical infrastructure that enables it is largely out of sight. When that infrastructure becomes a proposed industrial building in a neighborhood, she said, the abstract concerns about technology now suddenly have a physical address.
CoreSite representatives stressed that treating all data centers as interchangeable is neither accurate nor fair, given how different they can be, from location to the businesses they serve to the amount of land, water and electricity they need to operate.
The infrastructure behind the smartphone
During a recent tour of CoreSite’s downtown Denver facility, the building looked more like an ordinary urban property than the sprawling industrial complexes often associated with the data center debate.
Its significance, however, extends well beyond its appearance.
CoreSite’s facility houses hundreds of companies and connects networks, cloud providers, content platforms and other businesses that exchange data. Company representatives described it as a central hub for digital communications across the Rocky Mountain region.
The facility supports a range of services, from online banking and streaming video to healthcare systems and emergency communications. Its tenants also include companies involved in 911 services and public safety.
Yvonne Ng, CoreSite’s general manager for the central region, said the building’s interconnected ecosystem includes more than 230 companies, 150-plus networks and eight major cloud providers.
CoreSite representatives stressed that digital services don’t simply materialize on a screen. They depend on equipment, electricity, cooling systems and connections that move information between networks and users.
“If you use the internet, you’re using a data center,” Ruskowski said.
The most recent public debate about data centers often focuses on the newest, largest facilities proposed to accommodate AI-accelerated advances. Those projects can be dramatically different from the interconnected facilities that support everyday digital services, such as the one in downtown Denver.
CoreSite operates multitenant or co-location, data centers. Multiple customers share a facility’s space, power and cooling infrastructure, while using its connections to exchange information.
Other facilities are built primarily for a single customer, including large technology companies seeking enormous computing capacity to train AI models. The two models serve different purposes and can have very different resource requirements.
Benjamin Xie, an assistant Professor of Computer Science at the University of Denver and former postdoctoral fellow at Stanford University’s Human-Centered AI Institute, maintained that worries about data centers are not simply a misunderstanding of AI or a reaction to unfamiliar technology.
Data centers vary widely in size: some are small, like CoreSite’s Denver operations, which run at 6 to 10 megawatts, while others are hyperscale, consuming 100 megawatts or more. That energy could power some 105,000 Colorado households.
Colorado currently has no operational hyperscale data centers, but five are proposed or under construction. According to a report co-authored by Xie, these five hyperscale data centers could consume as much electricity as 84% of all Colorado households and as much water as all households in Fort Collins and Grand Junction combined.
As data centers face criticism over water consumption, many companies, including Microsoft, have begun moving toward “zero-water” cooling techniques that use closed-loop systems and engineered fluids, reducing reliance on local resources.
Mark Robb, CoreSite’s senior manager for two of its Denver facilities, compared the company’s multi-tenant model to a cruise ship, where many customers share a common infrastructure, versus a luxury yacht built for one owner.
The analogy illustrates differences in scale and use, although actual energy, water and environmental demands depend on the individual facility.
For consumers, the distinction can be easy to miss. A data center used to train an AI model and a facility that connects a hospital, a wireless network and an online service may all fall under the same broad label.
But the consequences of building them are not necessarily the same.
AI changes the equation
Artificial intelligence has intensified demand for computing capacity, creating a new reason to build more data centers and expand existing ones.
Xie said AI creates demand both to train models and to run them when people use AI-powered services.
Those are separate stages, and both require computing resources.
Xie compared a conventional Google search without an AI summary to a query sent to an AI chatbot. As a rough rule of thumb, he said, the chatbot query can require about 10 times as much energy.
Still, as AI services become more widely used, the computing power they require can increase demand for data centers.
That growth is occurring alongside the expansion of other digital services. High-definition video streaming, cloud computing, connected devices and business networks already require substantial infrastructure.
The result is a collision between two realities: Consumers increasingly expect digital services to work instantly, while some are asking how infrastructure needed to deliver those services is being built — or, more precisely, where it should be built.
Xie said oversight and regulations in states such as Colorado have not kept pace with the rapid growth of data centers. In many cases, land can be zoned simply as industrial without specifying a particular use. As a result, properties purchased five to 10 years ago may have already received zoning approvals and permits under an industrial designation, with no added disclosures necessary for plans to eventually build a data center on the site.
Others have cautioned against being too quick to regulate, worried that government interventions would hinder, rather than facilitate or shepherd, the growth of a fast-growing economic engine. Business groups have also expressed worries about Colorado and its local economies losing out, given the demand in some quarters to slow or even halt data center development altogether.
In his recent report, commissioned by a member of the Denver City Council, Xie said a lack of transparency around today’s proposed centers is a central problem, creating a major public trust issue, with recent surveys showing seven in 10 Colorado residents oppose building a data center near them.
Xie said the newer generation of proposed facilities can be substantially larger than the data centers that have historically operated in urban areas.
Speaking independently of his university role, he pointed to a proposed 750-megawatt facility in Weld County as an example of the scale being discussed in Colorado. He contrasted that proposal with the much smaller downtown Denver facility.
A facility that supports a network of businesses in an established urban building is not the same undertaking as a massive campus designed around extraordinary computing demand.
Yet both can be described as data centers — and both are increasingly caught up in the same debate.
Familiar complaints: Electricity, water, noise
As AI technology accelerates, companies are expected to invest up to $7 trillion in the industry by 2030 globally, according to a report produced by technology, media and telecommunications company McKinsey & Company.
The report said that 40% of those dollars will be invested in the U.S. The question, of course, is where in America — and whether Colorado will be a part of that mix.
Xie said several questions remain unanswered, particularly about the health effects of low-frequency noise from data centers. He said his work with researchers and community organizations aims to measure those impacts rather than assume they are fully understood. Xie stressed that the concerns are especially significant when industrial facilities are proposed near homes, parks, recreation centers and healthcare facilities.
Electricity and water use also remain central issues.
Large facilities can require substantial new utility infrastructure, and policymakers and others said they want to know whether those investments will ultimately benefit existing customers or expose them to additional costs.
Experts have said access to energy is the real problem — water matters, but much less at scale.
“I think it makes sense to look at the numbers in order of magnitude,” Colorado Energy Office Director Will Toor earlier said. “The energy use from AI is a meaningful share, and it’s going to be a significant share of the overall electric demand in the state. The water use is not.”
Western Resource Advocates, a nonprofit environmental law and policy outfit, recently modeled data center energy and water use across the West, Toor noted.
“If you look at their numbers, they project that, by 10 years from now, there might be about 6,000 acre-feet of water used for data center cooling in Colorado,” Toor said. “Right now, we use about 6 million acre-feet a year for consumptive use.”
Meanwhile, CoreSite representatives said the company pays for its electricity and, when necessary, the transmission upgrades required to deliver power to its facilities. They described years of coordination with utilities to determine whether sufficient capacity exists before construction begins.
The company also said it designed its planned Denver facility in consultation with local utilities and would fund the infrastructure needed to meet its power requirements.
Competing perspectives help explain why data centers have become a flashpoint.
For some governments, it could mean more tax revenue from construction and long-term operations, notably jobs for constituents, not to mention a boost to the local economy. For other governments, it is a debate over how to allocate resources.
For operators, these facilities are essential infrastructure that supports services consumers and businesses already rely on. For advocates, rapid development raises questions about environmental impacts, transparency and whether local governments have enough information to evaluate projects before they are approved.
The latter two perspectives point to a need for better public understanding, Xie said, but he maintained that education alone will not resolve the disagreements.
