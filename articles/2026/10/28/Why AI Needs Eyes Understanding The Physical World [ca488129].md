# Why AI Needs Eyes: Understanding The Physical World

Forbes · Jeevan Kalanithi, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-10-28

Jeevan Kalanithi is co-founder and CEO of OpenSpace, a Visual Intelligence Platform helping builders turn site reality into action.
Artificial intelligence has become remarkably good at navigating the digital world. It can read documents, analyze spreadsheets, write code and reason across enormous amounts of information. But much of the economy does not operate in a digital world.
Construction sites, factories, warehouses and energy facilities are constantly changing. Equipment moves, materials arrive, work gets installed, conditions change and much of what happens never makes its way into a database or report. That creates a fundamental limitation for AI, as an agent is only as useful as the data it can access. When some of the most important information exists in the physical world, AI needs a way to perceive that world.
In other words, AI needs eyes.
Why Agents Need Reality, Not Just Intent
A lot of the excitement around agentic AI focuses on what agents can do: analyze information, generate reports, recommend actions and increasingly execute tasks on behalf of users. But every one of those capabilities depends on input.
In construction, schedules, contracts, BIM models, drawings and specifications tell us what is supposed to happen. They are incredibly valuable, but they largely describe intent. The physical site tells us what actually happened. Has a wall been installed? Has work progressed according to schedule? Was an issue fixed?
For an AI system to reason effectively about real-world operations, it needs access to both sides of that equation: the plan and the reality.
That applies far beyond construction. Manufacturers have production plans. Utilities have engineering models. Logistics companies have routing systems. In every case, digital systems describe an expected state of the world, while physical operations determine the actual state. The more effectively AI can connect the two, the more useful it can become.
A Shift Toward Visual Input
Amid this shift, cameras and sensors have become smaller, cheaper and nearly ubiquitous. Smartphones, drones, fixed cameras, wearables and smart glasses are producing an enormous amount of real-world information. Historically, that information was difficult to use at scale. A photograph might document a moment, but someone still had to interpret what it showed and why it mattered.
Computer vision and spatial AI are changing that. The opportunity is no longer simply to capture more images but to transform them into structured intelligence about what happened, where it happened and how an environment changed over time.
That is an important transition because industries have traditionally relied heavily on text-first documentation: Someone sees something important and later translates that observation into a note, checklist or report. In that process, information can get lost, misrepresented or mislabeled. Visual-first systems can preserve more of the original context while allowing AI to interpret it later.
Why Seeing Is Not Enough
Giving AI access to images is only the beginning. An AI agent does not simply need to recognize that a pipe, wall or piece of equipment appears in a photograph. It needs context: Where is it located? When was the image captured? What was supposed to be there? What changed since the last observation? Is the work on schedule? Does something require action?
Spatial context helps turn isolated images into an evolving model of a physical environment. When visual reality can be aligned with drawings, schedules, models and other operational data, AI can answer more valuable questions. Instead of “What is in this photo?” we can ask, “Does what is installed here match the plan?” 
That is the difference between visual recognition and operational intelligence.
Closing The Loop Between Reality And Action
At its core, physical work is a feedback loop. We observe reality, compare it with what we expected, take action and then observe the result. Humans already work this way, but that process can be slow.
Collecting, organizing and communicating field information takes time. Someone may identify an issue in the morning, document it later and resolve it days afterward.
The combination of visual and spatial information could compress that cycle by reducing the time between observation, interpretation and action. An agent could compare current site conditions with plans, schedules and prior observations; surface discrepancies; and help route issues to the appropriate team. Subsequent site captures could then provide a way to verify whether the issue was resolved.
The value is not simply that AI can detect a problem. It’s that connecting observation to context and follow-up could shorten the feedback loop between identifying an issue and determining what happened next.
AI That Workers Barely Notice
AI adoption in physical-first industries will not happen simply because the technology has become more powerful. It has to fit the way people actually work.
Most field workers don’t have time to learn complicated AI interfaces. This means new applications should fit seamlessly into existing workflows. People should be able to work naturally—walking a site, talking, taking pictures—while AI handles more of the documentation and administrative work in the background.
The goal is not to replace human expertise but to let people spend more time on judgment, coordination and physical execution.
Building Toward Physical-World AI
Technology leaders preparing for this next phase should think about four foundations:
1. Reality Data: Do your systems capture what is actually happening, not merely what was planned?
2. Context: Is that information tied to location, time and relevant business data?
3. Interoperability: Can AI systems access that data and take action across the technology stack?
4. Human Oversight: Which actions can be automated, and where should humans remain in the loop?
These questions will become increasingly important as AI moves beyond screens and documents and into the environments where physical work happens.
The first era of AI taught machines to work with digital information. The next will connect AI to the physical environments where we build, manufacture, transport goods and operate infrastructure.
Giving AI “eyes” is about more than computer vision. It means giving AI enough awareness of reality to understand what is happening, compare it with what should be happening and help humans determine what should happen next. That is when AI stops simply understanding our information and starts helping us understand our world.
