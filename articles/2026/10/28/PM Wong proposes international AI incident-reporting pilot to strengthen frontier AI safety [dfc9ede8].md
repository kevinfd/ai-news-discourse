# PM Wong proposes international AI incident-reporting pilot to strengthen frontier AI safety

AsiaOne · 2026-10-28

Lessons learnt from serious artificial intelligence (AI) incidents should not be confined to one country or company, but shared more systematically so that others can learn from failures and emerging risks, said Singapore Prime Minister Lawrence Wong.
Speaking at a virtual leaders' meeting on frontier AI safety co-hosted by Norway and Finland on Wednesday (Oct 28), PM Wong proposed an international AI incident-reporting pilot, drawing parallels with international aviation, where incidents and near misses are studied to continually improve safety standards.
Along with the European Commission and 21 other countries, Singapore had in September signed a joint declaration calling for the control of frontier AI models at the United Nations General Assembly.
The declaration emphasised the importance of international cooperation to strengthen safeguards against the risks of increasingly powerful AI systems, while ensuring that AI remains under human control and oversight.
The Wednesday meeting discussed international cooperation on risk management, building global trust in the safety and security of frontier AI models, and developing common rules on AI safety and governance.
Speaking during his turn, PM Wong suggested drawing lessons from international aviation and developing a similar culture of learning and prevention for AI.
"We should be able to learn from what went wrong, identify the emerging risks, and take steps to prevent similar incidents happening elsewhere," he said.
The prime minister also pointed out that present knowledge about the safety of frontier models comes from their developers, even though businesses, governments, and other organisations are using such models.
"And as users, we ourselves encounter serious failures or near misses that reveal significant risks, even if no actual harm has occurred," PM Wong said.
He said that while these cases may not trigger existing reporting requirements, they can still offer valuable lessons for everyone.
As a first step, PM Wong suggested that interested parties and countries come together to launch a pilot programme around AI incident reporting.
This can be done by agreeing on a common set of incident categories and information to be shared.
"We can explore how participating countries can collect and exchange this information securely. And this will allow us to test what works, identify the practical difficulties, and build confidence before considering a wider framework."
At the meeting, the prime minister also suggested for signatories to adopt a practical and flexible approach, organising cooperation around specific workstreams, with smaller cross-regional groups of interested countries taking the lead in developing practical tools and frameworks.
Parties should also build on and leverage existing efforts, rather than duplicate what is already being done.
"So by connecting these different efforts, we can strengthen their collective impact and give our cooperation a wider reach," he explained.
About the joint declaration
The declaration came about amid concerns that the rapid development of frontier AI models poses serious risks to safety and security if they are not appropriately managed and go "rogue".
This, despite AI's potential to improve lives, advance science and boost economies.
When an AI agent "goes rogue", it means the AI has taken action beyond the task it was asked to perform. Both AI firms Anthropic and OpenAI said in July that their AI models had succeeded in acting on their own.
On Sept 16, OpenAI said it was introducing a new framework for tracking, probing and disclosing AI model misalignment instances, such as new ways for the models to act without authorisation, coordinate with other models or evade oversight.
This came as it disclosed six reports of "unexpected or concerning" behaviour in AI models.
Such warnings from within the AI industry itself have revived a long-running debate over whether advanced AI could escape human control and ultimately threaten humanity's survival, and whether the companies developing the technology are doing enough to prevent such a scenario.
