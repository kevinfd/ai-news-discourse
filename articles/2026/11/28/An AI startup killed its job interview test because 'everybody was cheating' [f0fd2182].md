# An AI startup killed its job interview test because 'everybody was cheating'

Business Insider · Tim Paradis, Tim Paradis (tparadis@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-28

Warp now conducts live technical interviews after job candidates' use of AI on take-home assessments made their responses look too similar. Courtesy of Warp staff Warp scrapped take-home tests after candidates' answers began looking "absolutely identical," the COO said. Now, the startup requires candidates to use AI in live exercises to show how they solve problems. Warp also interviews finalists in person , a move its COO says has helped it land more candidates. Last year, job candidates at the AI startup Warp had to complete a technical assessment on their own time. Now, AI has made those tests essentially worthless, said Gabriella Garcia, the company's chief operating officer and chief people officer. "We've killed the take-home because everybody was cheating on it," she said. Garcia said it became clear when test-takers' results began looking "absolutely identical" that the exam she'd championed only a year prior was no longer cutting it. The New York firm, which uses AI to automate payroll, compliance, and benefits, now opts for live technical exercises and in-person interviews for most open roles. Rather than watching as candidates use AI on the sly , the company now requires them to use it during the technicals to demonstrate their fluency. Candidates tackle an open-ended question, choosing their own tools, language, and approach, Garcia said. The AI requirement reflects how engineers work at Warp, where employees are expected to use the tech on the job. As AI changes how software engineers work, some employers are incorporating the technology into interviews to better understand how candidates use it to solve problems and make decisions. Testing AI judgment Warp began the year with 20 employees and expects to close 2026 with more than 100, she said. The exercises are meant to see how people think, especially when using Cursor and Claude Code. For software engineers , this means seeing whether a candidate starts with a plan. If they move right into coding, even with an AI agent , "that's a pretty big red flag," Garcia said. That's because, she said, the No. 1 thing AI lets engineers do is focus on planning and design, as well as asking questions, rather than simply cranking out code. Gabriella Garcia is Warp's chief operating officer and chief people officer. Courtesy of Warp Garcia, who previously held engineering roles at Meta, Apple, and Google, said Warp wants to see how someone prompts. "Is it concise? Are they using context? Are they using guardrails? How naturally do they spin up the code editor and those prompts?" she said. In some ways, Garcia said, the shift is a return to form for the technical interview . Years ago, a prospective employer might have given her and her fellow engineers "a really hard algorithms problem," she said. Her first step might have been to hover around a whiteboard and sketch out a plan. Over time, interviews morphed into exercises in which candidates would jump right into coding, finding what she called "little bottlenecks and edge cases and creating really bad code." Garcia said AI has revived the importance of computer science fundamentals: planning, designing architecture, and asking questions. Warp's engineering tests now run 75 minutes. The company uses technical exercises for engineers as well as marketing, sales, account management, and customer service roles. The specifics vary, she said, though the goal is the same: to see how someone solves a problem, learns, plans, and asks questions with an AI agent. A big investment The interview process is time-intensive, especially for Warp's 15-person engineering team, which doubled in size within a 10-day period earlier this year, Garcia said. She's considering ways to reduce the burden on engineers' calendars who take part in the interview process. One reason the company puts so much time into the process, she said, is that AI has boosted what a small engineering team can accomplish. Garcia estimated that, before AI, it would have taken 150 engineers to do the work her team is doing. She attributed the difference to a combination of the technology and the quality of Warp's hires. "The needs on engineering capacity are very different. And so what that requires is that we need to heavily filter our entire interview process," she said. Warp can end a technical interview early if a candidate is struggling, Garcia said. Or, if someone is crushing it, the interviewer can suspend the live technical and move on to answering the candidate's questions about the company. Final interviews are in person, so applicants can spend time with the team . Garcia said that also reduces the risk that someone on a video call could rely on AI-generated answers. Covering travel expenses for candidates outside New York adds about $1,500 to $2,000 to the cost of each interview for Warp, Garcia said, though she considers it worthwhile because it has "dramatically" improved the rate at which candidates accept roles. When the company lands a hard-to-get candidate, Garcia celebrates by ringing a dragon-emblazoned gong that she acquired for the startup's office. Warp employees ring it for other wins, too, including signing new customers. She said Warp looks for "high-slope" people who don't necessarily have fancy résumés yet show potential to learn and grow quickly. Two of the company's top engineers didn't complete high school, Garcia said. One is a 17-year-old who works remotely from Brazil. "He has lapped our staff engineers," she said.
