# Anthropic now has a price. But who puts a price on the risk?

Fast Company · Enrique Dans · 2026-11-28

The company warning about catastrophe just went public: on Thursday, November 19, the largest pure-play AI company, maker of the Claude line of AI models, went public, and its shares jumped almost 39% above its IPO price. The race slowed a bit to close at $97.17, up 17% from its IPO price, and that gave the company a fully diluted valuation of $1.76 trillion, if you factor in all options, restricted stock units, and convertible notes that might one day become shares. Considering that the company is just five years old, not bad at all.
What weirds me out about the whole operation is that we are talking about a company so irresponsible as to say in October 2023 that “there’s a chance that something goes really quite catastrophically wrong on the scale of human civilization might be somewhere between 10 per cent and 25 percent.” Then, in September 2025, its founder, Dario Amodei, said: “I think there’s a 25% chance that things go really, really badly.” Later, in January 2026, he said: “We are considerably closer to real danger in 2026 than we were in 2023.” What type of company are we talking about?
Declaring yourself extremely dangerous shouldn’t be an excuse to raise more money
All these estimations come not from some demented analyst, but from the founder and CEO of the company himself. They are subjective assessments of broad AI risks, including misuse and societal failures. They are neither measured Claude failure rates nor evidence of a steadily increasing numerical estimate. What obligations follow from taking those warnings seriously?
To make things even worse, Anthropic is not alone. OpenAI said in February 2015 that they “consider the development of superhuman machine intelligence the greatest threat to the continued existence of humanity”, in June 2015 that “AI will likely lead to the end of the world”, and in January 2023 that “the bad case is, like, lights out for all of us.”
What obligations should follow when an industry’s leaders publicly assign such extraordinary probabilities to catastrophic outcomes? I would expect independently verified safeguards, binding conditions on deployment, and clear accountability for harm. Instead, we appear remarkably comfortable treating these warnings as another part of the investment story.
Is there any other industry on Earth that is allowed to operate with a self-declared one–in-four chance of causing a catastrophe that could wipe out humanity as a whole?
