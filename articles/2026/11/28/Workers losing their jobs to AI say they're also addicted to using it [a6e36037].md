# Workers losing their jobs to AI say they're also addicted to using it

ABC News Australia · Allyson Horn and Kat Tse · 2026-11-28

AI micro dramas turn China's entertainment industry on its head
Until a few months ago, Oksana had her pick of acting jobs in China.
The 26-year-old was booked back-to-back to work on short-drama projects, usually bite-sized serialised videos made specifically for mobile viewing.
But this year, that work has all but disappeared because of the rise of videos produced with artificial intelligence (AI).
"Many of the companies I used to know and work with have switched to AI and no longer shoot live-action dramas," she said.
"Now getting a role is very hard.
"If I wanted to, I previously could have kept shooting until I dropped dead.
"This year, that's no longer the case. The short drama industry is completely different."
How fast has the shift happened?
In 2026, more than 90 per cent of China's new micro dramas were generated by AI, including the actors, voices and scenes.
They were nearly non-existent the year before.
Government figures show about 1,600 new AI micro dramas launched every day for the first half of 2026.
The AI videos have become so popular that even someone like Oksana, who has lost her job to the technology, is "obsessed" with them.
"These AI dramas really are good, truly good," she said.
"With those two or 300-episode short dramas, once I click in, I forget about getting out.
"Even when I go to sleep each night, I leave the sound on and fall asleep listening.
"My head is a bit of a mess every day now, and I can't focus on anything else."
Micro dramas for macro audiences
The micro drama episodes often focus on fantasy, underdog-style or romantic storylines alongside time-travelling and urban dramas.
The episodes are usually free and end on cliffhangers designed to keep people watching, with viewers having to pay to see the rest of the series.
One of China's biggest hits of 2026, drawing 6.08 billion views, is a series where a woman wakes up in the Tang dynasty and gains power by slaying demons that roam the city.
AI-generated videos of pets and anthropomorphic fruit are also finding large audiences.
AI micro drama developer Zhu Zhili said the technology allowed companies to produce entertainment content quicker, cheaper and with more advanced storylines.
"Before, we needed to hire real actors, build sets, go into studios, and so on," he said.
"Now, with AI, we only need a very small team working together on computers in a company or studio to produce a short drama that previously would have taken enormous manpower and resources to shoot.
"We can just use AI to do the pictures, storyboard generation, generative audio, generative music, and generative voiceover.
"So the efficiency is very, very high now."
AI creators have reported dramas may cost about one-tenth of traditional live-action shoots.
But Mr Zhu said despite the large volume of new dramas being made, turning a profit could still be difficult.
"In the first half of this year, China produced a total of 220,000 AI short dramas, which is an enormous number," he said.
"But on the other hand, only about 1,000 of them truly broke out, meaning they succeeded, passed 100 million views, or made money, a share of less than 0.4 per cent."
China has ambitious goals to have AI agents, devices and applications reach 90 per cent penetration in key sectors by 2030.
The government's current five-year plan also lists micro dramas as an emerging form of "cultural business" to be actively developed.
The AI entertainment industry's development has moved at breakneck speed since the release of powerful new AI video software, like tech group ByteDance's Seedance 2.0 model.
But Chinese social media is full of stories and complaints by former actors who are now out of work.
"Then this year, after AI became widespread, nearly all my peers stopped getting call sheets. I've been at home without work for several months too," said user Liu Yuzhan, who now works as a delivery driver.
Oksana says it's a similar story among her industry friends, but she believes there will always be an appetite for real human acting in longer-form content.
"Many of my peers have switched to livestreaming because of the current environment and can no longer work as professional actors," she said.
"But AI content gets tiresome if you watch it for long.
"It can never replace live actors, or the imperfections in their acting and their subtle emotions.
"AI is formulaic and can't convey emotion."
