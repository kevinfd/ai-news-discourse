# Breakfast News: AI Agents’ Opportunities and Risks

The Motley Fool · TMF Breakfast News · 2026-11-28

Breakfast News: AI Agents’ Opportunities and Risks
Team Rule Breakers on Meta’s Muse, and who can protect us from AI agents.
By TMF Breakfast News –
All of us on Team Rule Breakers are optimists. We believe in the future, and we like to invest in it before everyone else does. Right now, artificial intelligence (AI) agents are the clearest test case.
An AI agent is software that does a job for you. You tell it what you want, and it keeps trying different ways to get it done. Ordinary software follows a fixed set of steps. An agent works out its own.
Nikesh Arora, CEO of Palo Alto Networks (PANW +2.98%), thinks agents will be the next big driver of network traffic, as well as the next big security headache. He points to the roughly $5 trillion that companies could spend on computing power over the next five years. Humans can’t use all that data, he says, so agents will. Agent traffic on Palo Alto’s network security platform is already up ninefold.
The industry that protects agents, Arora says, “still has to be built.” Palo Alto’s own AI security product, Prisma AIRS, has reached about $100 million in revenue. It’s an early sign of a market that barely exists yet.
Consumers are getting their first real taste of agents through Muse, the new AI agent from Meta Platforms (META +3.13%). Muse can read your email, manage your calendar, and send messages for you.
We like a good story, but we check it against the numbers. So we asked our analysts what AI agents mean for investors. They didn’t agree on everything. But on one question, they mostly agreed.
The Muse Debate
Our analysts are split on Muse. Two use it and like it. Two have doubts. One questions whether most people will want it. The other questions whether it’s safe.
Jason Hall: “It Just Figures it Out”
Jason H. is the team’s most enthusiastic Muse user.
I use Muse daily and it’s been great. Using it for managing my calendar mainly, and it saves me a ton of time. Simple things like my kid’s multiple sports teams, games, practices and tournaments. I can just tell Chooch (what I named my Muse) to put all the events on my calendar and invite my wife, and it does it.
So most of the time I don’t even have to give it the information. It just figures it out. And now it mostly does it proactively without me prompting it.
Nick Sciple: Meta’s Networks Are Its Edge
Nick uses Muse for his personal email. It flags important messages, suggests next steps, and sends replies once he approves them. But he thinks the bigger prize is still ahead.
Long-run, the conversation is moving toward agents talking to other agents. I think Meta is well positioned to win there by leveraging their existing networks (WhatsApp/IG/FB).
Toby Bordelon: Curiosity Isn’t the Same as Demand
Toby has seen this pattern from Meta before.
I think there are clear parallels with other Meta projects — Oculus, metaverse, glasses — they are good at sparking interest and curiosity, but ultimately with all these Meta hasn’t been able to make a compelling case to a mass market consumer that they need this product/service in their life.
Tim Beyers: A Single Point of Failure
Tim is holding back on giving Muse too much access.
The idea that Muse becomes a single point of failure that leads to unmanageable identity theft is … spinning in my head. Probably unfair.
The Agent Security Opportunity
Our analysts agree more on security. Everyone who spoke up sees a big opportunity in protecting agents.
Toby asks the question many businesses will soon face.
How many organizations will trust Anthropic to protect them against Claude intrusions? It seems pretty obvious that there will be a large opportunity for many security players in the coming years though. Both in the enterprise and consumer space.
For Tim, the same risk creates an opportunity.
I can imagine there being a massive opportunity around agent security and management. Surely someone is going to figure that out.
“Who watches the Watchmen?” — yeah, it’s an opportunity.
Jason Moser: Bet on an Impartial Observer
Jason M. has a clear answer to Toby’s question. He backs it with his own money.
Do you trust the ones delivering the tech to protect you from their tech? I’m not sure. I mean, we do that to a degree already today, of course. But this stuff is next-level. I tend to support the notion of a third party (cyber co.) to take that on as opposed to someone like Anthropic or OpenAI. An impartial observer, so to speak.
I remain very long [Palo Alto, CrowdStrike (CRWD +3.77%), Zscaler (ZS +3.74%), and Cloudflare (NET +4.76%).
Bigger in Five Years (and Beating the S&P 500)?
So where does that leave investors?
Consumer agents show promise. Muse already saves our analysts real time, and Meta’s networks could help agents collaborate. But lasting demand and trust remain unproven. The more an agent can do, the more damage it can cause if something goes wrong.
That’s one reason several of our analysts are keeping a close eye on security – things like network traffic checks, device protection, and login controls. Companies working in these areas include Palo Alto s, CrowdStrike, Zscaler, Cloudflare, and Okta (OKTA +2.82%).
Still, a booming market doesn't guarantee a good stock pick. Also, OpenAI and Anthropic may build their own security tools. Technology changes quickly, so today’s leaders could fall behind. And Nikesh Arora, whose forecasts opened this article, runs Palo Alto, so his views aren’t neutral.
We’ll watch three things. Can agent traffic keep growing at anything like the ninefold jump Palo Alto reported? Can agent-security products like Prisma AIRS scale? And do businesses buy security from the AI labs?
For Team Rule Breakers, one question matters most: Can this company be bigger in five years (and beat the market)? For the companies that keep agents safe, many of our analysts think so.
