# Andrew Garfield on Becoming Sam Altman in ‘Artificial’: ‘I’m Dying to Understand This Person’

CNET · Abrar Al-Heeti · 2026-11-22

It’s a tall order for an actor to embody any living person, but especially someone whom audiences may already have negative feelings about.
Rather than daunting, that challenge was “exciting” to actor Andrew Garfield, who plays OpenAI’s polarizing CEO Sam Altman in the film Artificial, slated for an initial Dec. 25 release. The biopic follows the rise of OpenAI, focusing on Altman’s relationships — particularly with his cofounder Ilya Sutskever (Yura Borisov) — as he relentlessly pursues success amid Silicon Valley’s artificial intelligence power struggle.
Garfield’s perspective on Altman morphed as he worked to better understand him, the actor told me ahead of an early screening hosted by SFFilm in the heart of the city where the story is set. His mission as an actor is to get to the “central truths” of who a person is, he said, “and try to merge my soul with theirs. And like all of us, Sam has so many facets.”

That includes physical traits. Garfield says he had to lose weight and change his gait and voice to try to match Altman’s. The semblance is striking. From his bouncy stride to his clipped, gravelly speech, Garfield effortlessly emulates the Altman we’ve seen in interviews and Congressional testimonies. It’s a remarkable transformation — down to the sly, coaxing glint in his eyes — showcasing the breadth of Garfield’s acting talent.
But it goes beyond physicality. Garfield had to dig deep internally to find elements “that felt like they were a match” with Altman, he said. The result is an astonishingly believable portrayal of a multidimensional figure, at once pensive and inscrutable.
“There is a responsibility,” Garfield told me. “I take it very, very seriously to play any character, but particularly someone who’s alive, someone who’s pretty much my age. But also, maybe the greater responsibility is making sure that we interrogate these human beings that are leading our world into the next chapter, it seems.”
“And it feels like our job as storytellers to reflect the times we’re in,” he added, “and no better way of doing that than by looking at the human beings — their personal relationships, their souls, all the diamond facets of their souls — and interrogate the people that have the power over our culture right now.”
Artificial explores OpenAI’s rise through a rather bleak lens, with questions of moral responsibility and safety looming over the protagonists as they navigate the company’s success. The film is laced with dark satire, which offers moments of levity amid otherwise weighty meditations on the technology’s long-term impact.
In one scene, when Sutskever is asked about the difference between AI and artificial general intelligence, which can theoretically match or even surpass human ability, he plainly responds, “It’s like how rocket ships are different from scooters.” He goes on to tell his cohorts that this is achievable “in your lifetime — well, maybe not yours or yours or yours,” as he points to the less youthful figures in the room. Moments like these elicited generous laughter from the audience.
Director Luca Guadagnino, known for deeply character-driven stories, such as Challengers and Call Me by Your Name, said he was following the news about Sam Altman’s temporary ousting from OpenAI in 2023 and was fascinated by the “Shakespearean feud” leading to that moment. Guadagnino applies his signature character-centric approach to OpenAI’s story, relying on slow, measured beats, piercing close-ups and suspenseful music to relay the stakes of each pivotal moment.
“It’s never about the object; it’s always about the people,” Guadagnino said during a panel discussion after the screening. “It’s not about AGI or AI. It’s about the conflict between worldview and way of being, embodied by people that clash or fight for the vision they have.”
He also said, “The point for me was more about trying to understand: How did the possibility of an incredible technology that can help all of us turn into the opportunity to get power in your hands and to become self-entitled?”
Garfield is no stranger to portraying prominent figures in the tech world, having played Eduardo Saverin, Facebook’s co-founder and former chief financial officer, in 2010’s The Social Network. On the surface, Artificial seems like a spiritual successor, recounting another defining moment in the tech industry. But the films differ in both tone and approach, with Artificial trading rapid-fire drama for a darker, more existential theme that occasionally ventures into more technical territory. What the films do share is a focus on bold, forceful personalities.
“From that experience, I suppose, I was very curious about what it takes to win in this particular late-stage capitalist world that we’re in with these emerging technologies,” Garfield said during the panel discussion. “What does it take to hold Sauron’s Ring of Power and not be completely burnt up by it? Is it possible to not completely lose yourself to that overwhelming, exponentially growing possibility and power?”
Comic relief also arrives in the form of Ike Barinholtz playing OpenAI cofounder and tech mogul Elon Musk. By portraying him as a brutish, aloof figure who saunters in and out of rooms on a whim, he effectively underscores the absurdity of perceived importance and status. Almost every on-screen moment generated a murmur of laughter in the audience.
“I think we’re all fascinated by these cultural, iconic figures who are leading us into this next stage of culture and society,” Garfield told the crowd. “They’re kind of kids. They’re kind of slightly stuck in an early developmental place, and that felt like a really important part of Luca’s telling of this story.”
In fact, he added, “[Guadagnino] would say to us, ‘This is a John Hughes movie. This is a high school, screwball comedy, just with slightly higher stakes — mixed in with a little bit of Invasion of the Body Snatchers.’ A very important part of this is that these are all kind of adolescent, high-school-minded people.”
Even the way Garfield portrays Altman flippantly perching on couches and sitting cross-legged on tabletops while donning sunglasses — including during serious conversations about the future of OpenAI — was deeply researched.
“We had amazing consultants and researchers that work with us that gave us lots of information that’s not quite in the public arena about our characters,” Garfield said. “And Sam is someone who apparently would always sit like he was crisscross applesauce at play school, nursery school [or] kindergarten.”
Ultimately, Artificial poses an open question about where society is headed and whether those at the helm are equipped to lead the charge. Amid weighty explorations of AI’s ramifications, the film paints a haunting, revelatory portrait of the cunning, mercurial figures making decisions that shape our world.
For Garfield, this presented an opportunity to dive deep into a figure like Altman, who, despite his prominence, remains largely elusive in the public eye.
“I just wanted to tell this story and actually uncover for myself, excavate for myself, who is this person?” Garfield told me. “I’m dying to understand this person, and I get to try to understand this person, maybe more than anyone else has ever really tried to. And it feels like a gift to me.”
