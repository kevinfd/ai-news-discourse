# Will fragile bipartisanship on AI survive the silly season, or is parliament heading for a holiday meltdown? | Josh Butler

The Guardian · Josh Butler · 2026-11-22

The final four days of parliament for 2026 begin on Monday, and the government has a clutch of key reforms, budget hangovers and savings measures it wants to pass. But how many, if any, will get over the line by Friday is anyone’s guess.
But over the top of the usual last-week-of-school feeling of parliament at this time of year, between the Christmas parties, frenzied negotiations and the prospect of Senate all-nighters, hangs the protean multi-headed beast of AI. An eagerly anticipated parliamentary report will drop this week, bringing us one step closer to Labor’s long-awaited legislation on datacentres, energy use and copyright – just as the green shoots of a bipartisan consensus on AI threatens to split.
Welcome to Canberra’s silly season.
Several key policies remain in Senate limbo: the domestic gas reserve, changes to trusts and capital gains tax, the digital duty of care bill, and changes to private health insurance. Labor sources are confident some will get up this week, and the re-entry of Anthony Albanese into the domestic political fray after a week in Manila and Shenzhen is expected to intensify deal-making.
Labor sources expect they are closer to a deal with the Coalition on gas, while the Greens are the more likely partner on the rest of the slate. But the Greens and Coalition are in no mood to give Labor any quick wins. One Greens source says they would be surprised if much passed at all this week.
The Liberal deputy leader, Jane Hume, told me on the Australian Politics podcast on Friday that she was happy to see “movement” on gas, as Labor promised a carveout for Western Australia – a key Coalition demand – but said was “more they could do”, particularly around long-term supply. Talk of the government entertaining a gas phaseout, as pushed by the Greens, was seen by both the Coalition and Greens as just a tactic to pressure Angus Taylor and frighten business groups; at this stage, don’t expect to see a Greens-Labor deal on fossil fuels.
On digital duty of care, the Treasury bills and private health, though, the minor party is closer.
Both Labor and the Greens have publicly maintained that negotiations remain up in the air, each are telling the other that the ball is in their court. Greens sources have for some time said they don’t feel any pressure to cave on CGT and trusts, and are relatively content with the status quo on private health, so might hold out for the amendments they want.
Digital duty of care, however, is likely to find some agreement. The parliamentary report handed down on Friday, chaired by Labor, only recommended the government make one of the key amendments requested by the Greens, to allow a private right of legal action for citizens to take big tech to court. The Greens also want bigger fines, an ombudsman for big tech and further restrictions on gambling. Labor is keen to get this done and say they are open to amendments.
It may not get the biggest headlines this week but potentially the most interesting development may come as soon as Monday. That’s when the joint select committee on artificial intelligence is expected to release a report that could signal where Labor will go on the thorniest issues, such as datacentres and how Australian creatives should be paid for the AI theft of their work.
The report will also signal whether AI policy will remain a relatively bipartisan endeavour, as some Liberals have recently proposed, or whether the major party alliance will fracture.
The creative industries were flabbergasted last week when reports about Labor considering a carveout for music in a permit system for AI companies landed the morning after the Aria awards – where Elton John, Tim Minchin and other well-loved musicians lined up behind the Not AI’s To Take campaign.
Many creatives say they strongly doubt that the government was seriously considering such a proposal. But what’s not in doubt is senior Labor figures have begun more forcefully claiming there are shortfalls in copyright law – despite major rightsholder groups rubbishing the need for change. From Richard Marles to Tim Ayres and Michelle Rowland, a rejection of the “status quo” has bubbled up, potentially pointing to a direction of travel.
Liberals Jonathon Duniam and Aaron Violi just a fortnight ago said AI was “too important to become an unnecessarily partisan political contest”. But the shadow attorney general, Michaelia Cash, labelled the potential music carveout a “ham-fisted tactic”, saying property should not be “stolen”. Influential Liberal MP Garth Hamilton suggested AI was “just good old-fashioned theft”, proposing companies be subject to a levy. The former Nationals leader David Littleproud said on social media: “I reckon Anthropic can afford to pay everyone for copyright now. Don’t let big AI bully us!”
The foreign affairs minister, Penny Wong, will on Tuesday make a major speech to parliament to raise AI as an issue of foreign policy and national security. According to advance notes, she will say the development of AI must not “reward speed over prudence” and urge a “shared, global endeavour” that Australia wants to help shape.
“That means building trust between countries, cooperating when things go wrong and making sure the world’s response matches the power of the technology,” Wong will say.
But, at least for this week, all eyes will be on how the federal parliament’s response matches up.
- Josh Butler is a political reporter and the Guardian’s Canberra bureau chief of staff
