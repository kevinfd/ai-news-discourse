# CrowdStrike CEO: Companies are choosing between getting hacked and shutting down

Fast Company · Robert Safian · 2026-11-22

Microsoft systems down. Air traffic systems down. Problems at Chase, United, and Apple. After a week of outages across big systems, the AI conspiracy theories are in full swing. CrowdStrike founder and CEO George Kurtz describes how AI agents can turn a patch into a working exploit within an hour, and how CEOs are now taking services offline rather than risk being hacked. He also discusses CrowdStrike’s new AI Kill Switch, and why slowing down the frontier labs won’t slow down the attackers.
This is an abridged transcript of an interview from Rapid Response, hosted by former Fast Company editor-in-chief Robert Safian. From the team behind the Masters of Scale podcast, Rapid Response features candid conversations with today’s top business leaders navigating real-time challenges. Subscribe to Rapid Response wherever you get your podcasts to ensure you never miss an episode.
So what is going on? Over the past week, we’ve seen Microsoft systems go down. Air traffic systems go down. . . . The AI conspiracy theories are everywhere. Is all this just a coincidence?
I think a few of my friends and colleagues might have their tinfoil hats on as well. The reality is it’s the bill for the Mythos moment. Nine months ago, when Mythos came out, it really set in motion everyone looking for these vulnerabilities, right? And the whole industry has been racing to find as many vulnerabilities as it can and create as many patches as it can. But what we’ve done now is hit the sound barrier of actually being able to roll out all these patches. So when you look at what’s really taking place, and you look at all the issues that were found in Black October, you’ve got Darker November.
This is like the PHP code, and now Java, that people are scrambling to find and plug these gaps in.
Yeah, exactly. I think the Java one is actually going to be worse. There are already working exploits for all of that. But what people are doing now is rushing to roll out all of these patches, and they’re making the decision: Breach or break. That is, are we going to get breached because we can’t patch fast enough, or are we just going to break something and hopefully fix it if we roll it out? That’s kind of a new norm now because the industry can’t keep up.
Technology is messy. Things happen, and we’re going to get better and better as an industry at dealing with this sort of AI speed that we’re facing. When you think about all the legacy systems that are out there, and you think about even banking-type systems, there’s a lot of legacy-type code out there, and it’s not as easy just to create a fix while everything is in midstream. So it’s not always clear, and when things are melting down, you get on these 24-hour bridges, and people are trying to figure out what actually happened. It’s a bit of fog of war.
