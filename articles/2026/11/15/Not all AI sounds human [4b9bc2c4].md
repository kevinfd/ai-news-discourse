# Not all AI sounds human.

Gal Ratner · galratner · 2026-11-15

Not all AI sounds human.
Some AI models don’t generate tokens or try to sound like your best friend.
On September 15, Diogo Almeida, a former OpenAI researcher who worked on InstructGPT and ChatGPT, took TypeSafe AI out of stealth with a $40 million seed round led by DCVC and a model that can’t write a sentence. The model is called Jev. It charges $0.042 per million tokens of input. Output is free, because Jev doesn’t write any.
Twenty-four days later, Bloomberg reported that Andreessen Horowitz had led a round of about $870 million in TypeSafe at a $7.5 billion valuation, with Sequoia and DCVC along for the ride. TypeSafe, which styled the round a “Series AI,” says about a third of the Fortune 500 uses Jev. Its lead investor’s announcement, posted the same day, says 25% have integrated it. Bloomberg says TypeSafe wouldn’t name one.
In between, OpenAI and Microsoft shipped their own versions. OpenAI opened a Decisions API to developers in public beta on October 6, charging $0.10 per million input tokens and nothing for output. On October 9, Microsoft added Microsoft-Decision-1 to its Foundry catalog of AI models at exactly Jev’s price.
Then, in November, Mark Russinovich published one too. Russinovich is the CTO of Microsoft Azure, the company’s cloud business, and he put Venn on his personal GitHub account, the site where developers publish code, under his own name rather than Microsoft’s. The code is free under the MIT license. The part that makes the decisions is 3.15 megabytes, about the size of a photo from your phone. It runs on the ordinary processor inside an office laptop, and it works from data that most companies with an AI search system have already paid to create. It has eight stars, GitHub’s version of a like.
The easy read on all of this is that decision models are chatbots with the personality removed, and that anyone already paying for ChatGPT owns one. Within hours of OpenAI’s announcement, a commenter on Hacker News, the tech industry’s favorite message board, called it “a bit silly since OpenAI LLMs already can output structured data.” He’s right that a chatbot can fill in a form. He’s wrong about what that costs.
AI companies bill by the token, a chunk of text roughly three-quarters of a word long. A chatbot runs two meters. One counts what you send in. The other counts what the model writes back, and by TypeSafe’s comparison the writing meter usually costs about five times as much per token. Asking a chatbot to answer in a form doesn’t switch that second meter off, because the chatbot still writes its answer one token at a time and bills you for every one. A decision model doesn’t write anything. You give it the situation, a question and the list of answers you’ll accept, and it reads all of it once and puts a probability on every answer at the same moment. One is paying someone to write you a memo that ends with the word “billing.” The other is handing them a ballot.
Most of the AI work inside a business is multiple choice
Jev takes two things. The first is what TypeSafe calls the state, meaning whatever you want judged, whether that’s a customer email, a support ticket, an insurance claim or a row out of a spreadsheet. The second is a set of questions with the answers defined in advance. A question can ask Jev to pick one option from a list, to rate something on a scale you describe, or to say how likely a statement is to be true, which TypeSafe calls a Noul. Since October it can also find where something is in an image. TypeSafe’s quick-start example sends one support ticket with three questions attached, asking which team should handle it, how frustrated the customer is and whether it’s urgent. Jev answers all three in a single response, each with a percentage, and TypeSafe says adding more questions barely changes the response time.
That percentage is the product. Microsoft describes its goal for Decision-1 in plain terms. A 90% answer should be right about nine times out of ten. Archer Hume, who probed Jev from the outside and published what he found, saw that kind of honesty even where Jev was bad at the task. On two-step math word problems, Jev was right only 32% of the time, and its average confidence on those problems was 30%. It was wrong a lot, and it knew it. That’s what lets software set a rule, like acting on its own above 95% and sending everything below to a person. Try writing that rule against a paragraph of prose.
Venn asks the same three kinds of questions, in the same request format, and gets its answers a different way. It works on embeddings. An embedding is a long list of numbers that captures what a piece of text means, so that “I was charged twice” and “you billed me double” come out with very similar numbers even though they share almost no words. Most companies that have built an AI search over their documents, or a chatbot that answers from them, have already computed embeddings for everything they’ve indexed and stored them in a database. Venn decides from those numbers directly.
It can decide three ways. It can compare an item with plain-English descriptions of the options, which needs no history at all. It can look up the most similar cases in your own records and pick the option whose similar cases turned out best, and when it does, it hands back the IDs of those past cases, so a manager can see exactly which precedents drove the answer. Or it can train a small custom predictor on your labeled examples, which takes seconds to minutes on one machine. It also works on whole collections. It can sweep a backlog with a new question, pick out the item that doesn’t belong in a set, or flag that this month’s incoming requests don’t look like last month’s.
One reads, the other remembers
TypeSafe hasn’t published how Jev is built. Its launch post mentions a new architecture and a training method it calls Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions, which, going by the name, rewards the model when its percentages hold up, and it leaves it there. Hume published a detailed reconstruction. He concludes that Jev is a large language model underneath, the same kind of engine that runs a chatbot, rebuilt to read the situation once, let each question consult that reading without seeing the other questions, and score the allowed answers directly instead of writing. He found that the way Jev chops text into tokens matches none of the 192 public versions he tested, and he clocked it working through about 30,000 tokens in roughly 160 milliseconds. He also calls the reconstruction speculative, which is the right word for any account of a model whose maker hasn’t described it.
Microsoft was more forthcoming about Decision-1. It’s Alibaba’s nine-billion-parameter Qwen model, retrained to score answers in a single pass, and Microsoft says it’ll rebase it on its own models and on OpenAI’s. Parameters are the adjustable numbers inside a model, and their count is a rough measure of its size. The open-source alternatives to Jev follow the same recipe. Kev, from developer Jared Palmer, retrains Qwen models at four sizes running from 0.8 billion to 27 billion parameters.
Venn is built differently. Its reader, the piece that turns text into numbers, is EmbeddingGemma 2, a free Google model of about 270 million parameters, roughly a third the size of Kev’s smallest. The reader is frozen, meaning nobody retrains it. On top of it sits a decision layer of 787,457 parameters, trained on six public datasets of customer requests, questions, news stories and encyclopedia entries, whose main trick is a learned “none of these” option. That’s the 3.15 megabytes. Everything else Venn learns, it learns from your records, and you own those.
The Doom demo shows what that means in practice. Venn plays a research version of the old shooter from screenshots alone. It looks at the screen, finds the most similar moments from earlier games, and picks whichever move turned out best in those moments. When a game ends, the results go back into the memory. Nothing inside the model changes. After enough games, one more step compresses the memory into a decision maker of 49,153 parameters. Playing alone with the memory switched off, that small model averaged 8.4 kills a game over 250 games. The memory it was built from averaged 6.2. It’s a video game. It’s also a working example of software getting better from its own track record without anyone retraining a model.
The bill is small because nothing gets written
A chatbot’s cost and delay pile up with every token it writes, one after another. A decision model makes one pass over the input and stops, so the second meter has nothing to count. Hume argues that the output tokens Jev reports are a billing figure worked out after the fact, and no sign that anything was generated. TypeSafe prices accordingly. At $0.042 per million input tokens, Jev costs roughly five to 238 times less than the $0.20 to $10 per million that TypeSafe says chatbots charge for input alone, before their output meter even starts. TypeSafe also says Jev answers in 70 to 500 milliseconds.
Speed is the part I care about. I’ve said before that language models don’t belong anywhere a decision has to be made fast. You don’t bet a horse race on a model that needs several seconds to finish its sentence. An answer that comes back in a fraction of a second, with a percentage attached and a rule in code deciding what to do with it, is the first kind of AI I’d let near that loop.
The most useful price check is tucked into Venn’s documentation. To compare the two on a public set of banking support tickets, the Venn project ran all 3,080 test tickets through Jev. The bill was about 21 cents.
TypeSafe deserves credit for admitting in its launch post that it hasn’t proven its prices are sustainable. It deserves less for the post’s headline numbers. It claims workflows that ran 193.6 times faster and 444.6 times cheaper than with chatbots, while conceding it expects those to sit near the high end of what customers will see. It says Jev can’t hallucinate, and in the same post describes the zero in its hallucination comparison as “not empirical.” What TypeSafe means is that Jev can only answer from the list you gave it. A wrong answer from your list is still a wrong answer.
Venn is cheaper again, for a different reason. Its expensive step is reading, turning text into those lists of numbers. Venn assumes you paid for that step when you built your search, so a new decision is arithmetic on numbers you already have, and that arithmetic runs on a regular processor with nobody sending you a bill. The wine example makes the point. With 65,701 wine reviews already converted to embeddings, Venn learned to predict prices and then priced every bottle in about seven seconds, with no usage charge. Asking Jev directly took about 30 minutes and cost $1.26, with roughly the same accuracy on the test set. In the help-desk example, Venn swept a backlog of 13,083 tickets with a new question in a third of a second on an eight-core processor.
None of it is free. Hardware and electricity cost money, and the reading still has to happen once. Converting all 65,701 reviews took a 76-second pass on a data-center graphics chip, and the wine example’s own timings show a server’s ordinary processor needing 83 seconds for just 2,000 reviews. Venn’s responses even include a cost field. It always reads zero, and the documentation notes that nothing is measuring it. TypeSafe’s hallucination rate and Venn’s cost field have that in common.
Remembering history beats a smarter model on personal decisions
Netflix’s own researchers wrote in 2015 that its recommendations influence about 80% of the hours members stream, and they estimated the system was worth more than $1 billion a year in subscribers who didn’t cancel. Netflix didn’t get there with a smarter model. In 2009 it paid $1 million to the team that made its rating predictions 10% more accurate. Three years later, its engineers wrote that the winning entry’s extra accuracy wasn’t worth the engineering effort to put it into production. By then members were streaming, and Netflix could see what they actually played, what they finished and what they scrolled past, which told it more than any star rating. In 2017 it scrapped the stars for a thumbs up or down, after tests showed the thumbs drew 200% more ratings. Netflix turned its customers into a yes-or-no question. TypeSafe would call it a Noul.
When the question is about you, what you did beats what a smart model thinks. The Venn project’s tests against Jev say the same thing at a smaller scale. On a public dataset of 13,083 banking questions sorted into 77 support queues, Jev routed new tickets correctly 79.7% of the time by reading them against the queue names. Venn, matching each new ticket against 10,003 closed ones, got 93.6%, and it was already ahead with just five closed tickets per queue. On a second public dataset Venn won again, 94.3% to 91.1%, once it had 50 past examples per category.
History also answers what the item can’t. A consumer complaint can ask for a refund, but it can’t tell you whether the company will pay one. On complaints filed with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, Venn ranked new complaints by how similar past complaints against the same company had turned out, and scored 0.768 on a scale where 0.5 is a coin toss and 1.0 is perfect. A method built on Jev’s labels scored 0.702. Combining Jev’s answers with the history did best of all, at 0.816.
Sometimes the history isn’t words at all. Given recordings of actors reading identical sentences in different emotions, Venn’s experimental audio support named the emotion 56.5% of the time on voices it had never heard. Jev, which doesn’t take audio, got the transcripts and managed 14.6%. The words were the same. The voices weren’t.
None of this means history always wins. Jev routed Firefox bug reports more accurately than a Venn model trained on past routing, 64.1% to 58.3%, and it was better at naming grape varieties from tasting notes. On the public portion of JevBench, a benchmark for this kind of model, Venn’s own documentation puts it at 55% against 82% to 84%. Bad records poison the well, too. When a fifth of the closed tickets were filed under the wrong queue, Venn’s closest-match method fell below Jev, though letting the 11 closest tickets vote held accuracy at 93.7%. Venn’s documentation says all of this out loud. Its first no-history score on the bank data was 92.1%, until the project traced it to Venn recognizing the dataset’s queue names, which are all over AI research, and replaced it with 69.0% using fresh descriptions, below Jev. It also records a hot dog detector, inspired by HBO’s Silicon Valley, that went 20 for 20 on its test photos and then called a hamburger a hot dog with 100% confidence. The “not hot dog” photos it learned from were bananas. That’s what bad history looks like.
Neither model can run Netflix’s recommendation engine. That engine learns from the behavior of hundreds of millions of members, and Netflix is choosing among thousands of titles, while a single Jev question tops out at 255 possible answers before it needs a two-step workaround. What Jev can do is the grunt work inside a system like that, tagging every title in a catalog by mood or re-ranking a shortlist that another system produced, in a fraction of a second. TypeSafe publishes a guide for exactly that. Venn is closer in spirit, because its best trick is history. Record what one viewer finished and what they bailed on after ten minutes, and its memory of past cases will pick the next title from a shortlist by what kept that viewer watching in similar moments before. That’s a recommendation engine for one, running on a laptop. Nobody has published a test of it.
Tinder ran into the same lesson from the other direction. In 2016, its CEO, Sean Rad, confirmed to Fast Company that the app gave every user a secret desirability rating, an “Elo score” borrowed from chess rankings. In 2019, Tinder announced that “Elo is old news at Tinder” and said its matching now leans on how people actually behave on the app, who they like and who they pass on. Tinder threw out the score and kept the history. Jev can look at photos now, so you could hand it a profile and ask whether a particular person would swipe right. It would answer, with a percentage, and it would be guessing the way a stranger guesses your type from your bio. Venn could do what Tinder does at the scale of one person. Turn the profiles someone has already swiped on into embeddings, record each swipe as the outcome, and predict the next one from the profiles that look most like it, the same way it learned Doom from old games. Your type turns out to be a cluster of numbers sitting close together. Nobody has tested Venn on this, its image support is experimental, and it would only know one person’s history, where Tinder learns from everyone’s. Anything that scores people from their photos also needs a hard look for bias before it goes near a real user.
TikTok’s version of the lesson leaked. In December 2021, The New York Times obtained “TikTok Algo 101,” an internal explainer that TikTok’s engineers in Beijing wrote for non-technical staff, and TikTok confirmed it was authentic. It names adding daily users as the “ultimate goal” and says the feed is tuned for two things, whether you come back and how long you stay. It even includes a rough scoring equation that takes the predicted chances you’ll like a video, comment on it and play it, plus how long you’re expected to watch, and multiplies each by what it’s worth to TikTok. Those predicted chances are exactly the kind of answer a decision model returns. Will this person like this video? That’s a Noul. TikTok’s edge isn’t the math. It’s the history behind every guess, billions of swipes and replays telling it what someone like you did a second ago.
Venn’s memory of past cases runs the same loop, small enough to read. It shows something, records how it turned out, and next time picks whatever worked best in similar moments. In the Doom demo the outcome was kills. Swap kills for seconds watched and you’ve built the business model of the modern feed. Venn’s documentation is blunt about who decides what counts as a win. “Your application supplies what ‘good’ means.” It also has a dial for exploration, a set chance that it deliberately tries something it isn’t sure about. On a feed, that’s the video you didn’t ask for, slipped in to see if it hooks you. TikTok’s document makes the same point from the other side, warning that a user who keeps getting the same kind of video gets bored and closes the app.
In 2017, Reed Hastings told Wall Street analysts that Netflix was “competing with sleep, on the margin.” A version of that loop now runs on a laptop, and the code is free.
Neither one thinks but they are both still AI
The Venn documentation puts the core limitation in five words. “Embedding similarity is not reasoning.” Its own small stress test shows Venn getting two of four simple number comparisons right before anyone tried to trick it. In the help-desk demo, a customer writes that he was NOT charged twice, and that his statement just shows two lines. Venn’s precedents say duplicate charge, because the ticket looks like every duplicate-charge ticket before it. A model that reads the text catches the “not.”
Jev doesn’t reason either, and TypeSafe says so on a documentation page titled Model jaggedness. Jev takes questions literally. It miscounts. It handles dates as text, so it can’t reliably tell you which of two came first. It stumbles over double negatives, leans toward whichever option you list first, and can be steered by instructions planted in the very text it’s judging. Its long-document mode, which raises the limit to 262,000 tokens, has to be switched on with a request header named TypeSafe-Danger-Zone-Long-Context. TypeSafe says it’s slower and less accurate, too.
Microsoft’s catalog page for Decision-1 carries the warning that matters most to anyone in hiring, lending or insurance, which is that the model isn’t designed or evaluated to be the only decision-maker on consequential decisions about people. Venn’s documentation says nothing in one of its answers authorizes an action on its own. These models sort the work. People still sign.
The workable setup uses both, and the Venn project spells it out. Route a ticket with Venn when a past case is a clear match. When nothing in the history comes close, hand it to Jev or to a person, and write every resolution back to the history so tomorrow’s clear matches include today’s hard cases. Because Venn speaks Jev’s question format, that hand-off is cheap to build.
How to get them
Jev is a hosted service. You can sign up with TypeSafe directly, which ran a waitlist at launch, or skip the line through OpenRouter, a service that resells access to hundreds of AI models under one account. Cloudflare and Vercel, two platforms a lot of websites already run on, carry it too. TypeSafe publishes toolkits for Python and JavaScript, and anything that can send a web request can call it. Your data goes to TypeSafe’s servers. TypeSafe says it doesn’t train Jev on customer requests or responses.
If you want the same idea on your own hardware, there are free open-source alternatives. Kev’s largest model came within a point of Jev on questions from sources it had never trained on, 85.1% to 85.7%, but it wants a data-center graphics card. Jevos, from two independent developers, runs on a laptop processor and answers in 28 to 130 milliseconds, and its own benchmarks show it trailing Jev on every task they tested.
Venn is a free download from Russinovich’s GitHub page, and you’ll need a developer to set it up. It isn’t in Python’s public package catalog, where the package actually named venn is an unrelated diagram library. It needs version 3.12 of Python, the programming language most AI tools are written in, and it runs on Linux or Windows, including the ARM chips in newer Windows laptops. The quick-start demo takes about a minute and downloads nothing. Real text questions need Google’s reader, about 1.5 gigabytes, and the 3-megabyte decision layer from Hugging Face, the site where AI models are shared. Venn’s built-in server has no password and by default only answers requests from the same machine. Keep it that way.
There’s a catch for Microsoft shops. Venn can read embeddings out of six kinds of databases, including Microsoft’s Azure AI Search. SQL Server, Microsoft’s flagship database, isn’t one of them. If your embeddings live there, budget a developer for the connector. It boils down to finding the closest past cases with a couple of filters applied, which SQL Server 2025 already knows how to do. Neither project ships a toolkit for .NET, the Microsoft platform a lot of corporate software is written in, but both answer ordinary web requests, so .NET teams can call them without one.
Read the license before you build a product on it. Venn’s code is MIT-licensed, and Google’s reader is under the similarly permissive Apache 2.0. The 3-megabyte decision layer comes with its own permission to redistribute it, and Venn’s license page says that permission doesn’t extend to commercial-use rights it doesn’t spell out. Two of the six datasets it was trained on have no clear license, and the description of one says research or noncommercial use. Venn’s own license audit says all of that. Russinovich calls Venn a research preview, and a research preview on one person’s GitHub account comes with no support contract. I’m not a lawyer. Have one read it.
The numbers decide
TypeSafe’s launch announcement says Jev is named for Jevons paradox, the observation from William Stanley Jevons’s 1865 book The Coal Question that when steam engines got more efficient, Britain burned more coal. Satya Nadella reached for the same paradox in January 2025, the week DeepSeek’s cheap model knocked Microsoft’s stock down. “Jevons paradox strikes again!” he posted. The point, as economists read it at the time, was that cheaper AI wouldn’t shrink demand for AI computing.
Jevons may well be right about decisions. Make them cheap enough and software will ask for millions of them a day. The paradox doesn’t say where they have to run. Jev’s answers come from TypeSafe’s servers, OpenAI’s from OpenAI’s, and Decision-1’s from Azure. Venn’s come from the processor already sitting in your office, using numbers your company already paid to compute, and they arrive with the past cases that justify them.
I build AI agents for a living, software that does work on its own instead of just chatting, and a lot of what happens inside them isn’t conversation. Cara, the shopping assistant I built for ShopSnap storefronts, sits on a semantic router from Microsoft’s AI libraries, which means the meaning of a shopper’s message decides where it goes before any language model writes a word back. That’s a decision made from an embedding. The RAG pipelines I build, the systems that let an AI answer from a company’s own documents, keep their embeddings in SQL Server 2025, which stores them natively. Those numbers exist because someone already paid to compute them for search. Venn’s argument, stripped down, is that they can answer questions too, on hardware the company already owns.
When the question is about how your company does things, your own records beat the smarter model in most of the Venn project’s tests. They lost on the Firefox bug reports, and they lose whenever the records themselves are wrong. That’s not a technology insight. It’s an accounting one. Before the next AI invoice comes in, ask your team how many of your AI calls are really multiple-choice questions dressed up as conversations, what you pay per answer, and where your embeddings live. If the answers come back “most of them,” “too much” and “in a database nobody’s using for this,” you’ve found the money.
The CTO of Azure just published AI that doesn’t need Azure. Only eight people noticed.
