# A philandering chief is not Nvidia’s worst problem. It’s the legal chip smuggling mess that comes next.

Digital Trends · Rachit Agarwal · 2026-11-15

Jensen Huang is trending for his affairs, with new affair details coming out seemingly every other day. Amidst the affair allegations, Huang has stepped down as CEO, but believe it or not, the affairs are not Nvidia’s word problem, and it may be the least important part of this story.
The woman involved, Alice Huang, was an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company that had become one of Nvidia’s bigger customers. According to The Washington Post, American officials suspected her of working as a Chinese intelligence source, and they suspected her employer of moving restricted AI chips into China.
In June 2025, Singapore took her passport on the same day US export control officers turned up at a company site. Nvidia stopped selling to Megaspeed that month, and spokesperson John Rizzo says the block has not been lifted since.
Chris Miller, the Tufts professor who wrote “Chip War,” thinks the warning signs were there for anyone willing to look. “Megaspeed appeared out of nowhere,” he said, pointing out that it was not a serious data center operator and that Singapore already had questions.
How does a banned chip end up in China?
Just after the Biden administration’s restricted chip export in 2022, Nvidia’s China revenue dropped from $7.1 billion in 2021 to $5.8 billion in 2022. During the same time, Nvidia’s Singapore revenue climbed from $2.3 billion in 2022 to $23.7 billion in 2024, yet chips physically arriving in the country accounted for roughly one percent of the company’s revenue.
This exponential rise in revenue in Singapore triggered concerns that chips being sold in Singapore were somehow diverted to China. A Justice Department indictment describes warehouse workers taking a hair dryer to server boxes to swap labels and serial numbers before showing the repackaged shipment to an inspector, while the real contents were already on their way to China.
The uncomfortable part is that a lot of this never needed smuggling at all. Middleman firms around Asia buy Nvidia hardware legally and then rent out access to it over the internet, so a Chinese company that cannot buy the chip can simply log in and use one. But now the US government is also focusing on closing this loophole, which would increase the need for smuggling even more.
Why should anyone outside the chip business care?
Because the rules are about to change for everyone who buys compute. The House has passed a bill blocking cloud providers from renting restricted chips to Chinese firms, and another proposal would force chipmakers to build tracking into their products. Huang told a Fox News podcast last November that “People talk about smuggling, but it’s incredibly hard to do.” Washington no longer seems convinced.
