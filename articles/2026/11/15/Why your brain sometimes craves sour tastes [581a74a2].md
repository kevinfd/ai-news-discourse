# Why your brain sometimes craves sour tastes

Scientific American · Tom Metcalfe · 2026-11-15

Adding a dash of lemon juice to a dish is among a good cook’s many tricks, but exactly why sour tickles the taste buds has long been a mystery. Now scientists have uncovered the precise neurochemistry behind this age-old culinary tip. They’ve found that sourness triggers the release of the chemical serotonin—and drowns out the signals of some other tastes, such as sweetness.
“This has been known by chefs for ages, and what we did is use that to actually work out how things occur within the taste bud,” says neurobiologist Stephen Roper of the University of Miami Miller School of Medicine.
In a new study published in the Journal of Neuroscience, Roper and his team explore how taste signals make it from the tongues of mice to their brains. The researchers show that “sour” is sensed by specific cells in taste buds that then produce the neurotransmitter serotonin, which suppresses the ability of nearby cells to signal their detection of certain other tastes, including sweetness and bitterness. In turn, the “sour” signals travel to the brain via a neurological pathway dedicated to aversive tastes—in other words, tastes that cause a strong sense of dislike—while pleasant tastes such as “sweet” are sent by another pathway.

It’s unclear how these two taste pathways interact, Roper says. “Hopefully my work can lead to identifying certain molecular markers that characterize these two different streams,” he says. “Once you have a molecular marker, then you can chase down those neurons.”
The new work gives a new scientific understanding of the neurochemical processes of taste: “There is now quite a bit of mystery surrounding the question of whether sour is even a taste,” says neuroscientist Emily Liman of the University of Southern California, who wasn't involved in the new study—or if the sour-sensing cells are reacting to something else, such as the ammonium given off by cellular decay, that disrupts their pH balance in the same way.
Neuroscientists think the experience of taste is centered in the mammalian brain's gustatory cortex, while the limbic system—a more primitive, emotional center in the brain—assesses pleasure and aversion. Meanwhile the brain stem can trigger a reflex that causes us to immediately spit out spoiled food. But while sour tastes can indicate spoilage—in milk, for example—they don’t always do so, and many toxins don’t taste sour. Some sour flavors are appealing, such as the citrusy zing of lemonade. These conflicts are why it is such a mystery that the “sour” taste is prioritized, Roper says.
The study shows that taste buds act like a preprocessor that can modify taste signals before they are sent to the brain. In other words, taste buds are not passive actors but shape the signals they send. Adult humans have between 2,000 and 8,000 taste buds, mostly on their tongues yet also scattered around other parts of the mouth and throat. Each taste bud has dozens of taste-sensing cells, which typically responds to just one particular taste. When the sour-sensing cells secrete serotonin, however, they overwhelm nearby cells that detect sweet, salty and umami—a basic savory taste found in mushrooms and soy sauce.
Roper says researchers will next map the neurological pathways that transmit the modified taste signals from the tongue to the brain to understand how the brain makes sense of all the competing tastes that can exist in a single mouthful of food.
Neuroscientist Sue Kinnamon of the University of Colorado Anschutz School of Medicine says the new study helps to resolve long-standing questions about signaling between taste cells. A key finding is that serotonin is the main neurotransmitter involved in the process rather than another neurotransmitter such as gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), which is also secreted by sour-sensing taste cells, she says.
“The taste bud is an exciting organ,” says Kinnamon, who was not involved in the new study. “They’re all in close proximity to each other, so it’s not surprising that they talk to one another, and that influences what we taste.”
