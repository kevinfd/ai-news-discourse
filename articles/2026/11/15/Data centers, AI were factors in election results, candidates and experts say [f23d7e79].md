# Data centers, AI were factors in election results, candidates and experts say

Chicago Tribune · Doug Ross, Maya Wilkins · 2026-11-15

For all the talk of this month’s election being about a referendum on President Donald Trump, voters had other considerations on their mind, too.
Data centers and artificial intelligence had a “huge impact, huge impact,” state Rep.-elect Ryan Kominakis, D-Valparaiso, said.
More than 500 state legislative seats were flipped nationwide, he said.
“I’ve been in this for a while, and I’ve never seen anything like this ever,” state Rep. Chuck Moseley, D-Portage, said.
“I find it hard to believe that that many people could be wrong,” his wife told him.
Utilities and data centers were the main topic that came up when Kominakis was knocking on doors in his campaign to unseat incumbent Republican Ed Soliday.
“A lot of people are frustrated with the data centers,” Kominakis said, with voters citing the one that had been proposed near Wheeler High School in Union Township and another whether Valparaiso had initially proposed a sports complex.
“All these horror stories,” he said, and no regulations made voters wary.
Adding to that is the $655 million and counting in tax revenue loss from state economic development giveaways to data center developers. “We’ve got to repeal that law or at bare minimum amend it,” Kominakis said.
He has a Dec. 11 deadline to give the Legislative Services Agency, which drafts legislation, his proposal to repeal those tax breaks, require project labor agreements for union wages for data centers that receive scaled-back tax breaks, limit their locations so they can’t be near schools or neighborhoods, and require environmental protection.
Kominakis, a Democrat, plans to seek his caucus leadership for advice on the direction his legislation takes.
“I want to make sure we’re doing the right thing, making the correct strategic moves,” he said.
He credits his Nov. 3 victory to Republicans staying home or switching sides as well as Democrats showing up.
“Voters talked about it at the door. Not every door I knocked talked about data centers, but some did,” Moseley said. The Democratic incumbent won re-election.
Voters’ concerns ranged from extending big money for tax credits and other incentives to questions about the noise data centers generate. “Everything, everything. It’s just a wide swath with people with different concerns about the things,” Moseley said.
“As much as 70% of folks in Indiana are opposed to data centers for various reasons,” Moseley said. “There’s more than likely going to be some discussion about that in the next session, and we’re going to have to see where that goes.”
People want realistic answers to their concerns, along with what guardrails to put in place, if any, he said.
“I think we’re going to have a good, honest conversation and get everybody at the table,” Moseley said. “We need to be fair and honest.”
He said the state needs a task force to look at various concerns surrounding data centers.
State Sen.-elect Scott Houldieson, D-Highland, he’s still exploring what’s the purpose of data center development and where it’s going.
“What’s the endgame here?” Houldieson said. “These developments are supporting an industry that has produced a lot of wealth inequality in our country, and we should not offer any kind of tax incentives to them.”
Houldieson said he’s concerned that its effect on utility rates has yet to be seen, and it’ll take a burden on infrastructure. He also said he’s against non-disclosure agreements.
Houldieson previously told the Post-Tribune that he wants to focus on utility affordability at the statehouse. He’s unsure what legislation he’ll support or draft yet.
“I’m thinking about all of that,” Houldieson said. “I’m still learning the ropes of the legislature, and I probably will be learning a lot for a while, so I can’t say what legislation I’m going to be putting forward.”
State Rep.-elect Nick Neal, D-Crown Point, said it’s important to him that residents’ data center concerns are heard and that utility customers are protected as developments come in.
Neal wants the state leaders to look at regulating data centers more closely.
“It’s hard for me to say what I’m going to support,” Neal said. “I’m supporting things that are fair and reasonable for all parties involved.”
With electric affordability, Neal believes the state legislature should revamp the Indiana Utility Regulatory Commission, believing it should be a bipartisan group.
“We’ve got to do a better job of regulating downstate of those folks who are setting those standards,” Neal said.
State Sen.-elect Jay Starkey, R-Demotte, did not respond to a request for comment.
U.S. Rep. Frank Mrvan, D-Highland, said in a statement Friday that the rising cost of energy is because of “irresponsible policies” from state and federal governments that “have prioritized the interests of Big Tech and Big Coal.”
“Affordability was clearly an important issue in this election,” said Mrvan, who won re-election against Republican candidate Barb Regnitz. “The rising costs of household energy and fuel are forcing families to make difficult choices between paying their utility bills, purchasing necessary medications, putting food on the table, and visiting loved ones.”
Data center development decisions are made at state and local levels, Mrvan said, but he believes each project should be individually evaluated, looking at its energy demands, demand on natural resources, demands on our existing utility infrastructure and quality of life.
Mrvan said he’s concerned that the Trump administration has allowed “Big Tech to operate without meaningful accountability.” He plans to ensure corporations are held accountable and that development doesn’t come at the expense of the environment, economy, or safety and well-being of communities.
“When it comes to data centers, I support federal legislation requiring these companies to pay the full cost of the energy and infrastructure necessary to support their operations,” Mrvan said in his statement. “Residential ratepayers should never be forced to subsidize the enormous energy demands of some of the world’s wealthiest technology companies.”
Mrvan has focused on energy affordability, including recently introducing legislation to require the U.S. Department of Energy to produce a report detailing the financial impact ratepayers will feel of keeping coal plant facilities open past their retirement.
Kerwin Olson, executive director of the Citizens Action Coalition, said it was no surprise that data centers and energy costs were at the top of this election’s issues.
“I think it was a significant issue in Indiana,” Olson said. “We saw so many unexpected election results, some of which were located in areas with large groups fighting and pushing back on data centers. … You can certainly look at the results and say, ‘Wow, OK, yeah, I think data centers probably paid a role in a few, if not many of those.’”
Olson expects “a rather large number” of data center-related bills to be filed. He believes a lot will be focused on transparency, particularly with non-disclosure agreements, and tax incentives, including sales tax and local exemptions.
He also thinks at least one bill for a statewide moratorium will be introduced.
“We’ve seen many, many moratoriums passed in cities, towns and counties across the state,” Olson said. “For that reason, I think there’s a chance, when you’re seeing that kind of activity at the local level, one would hope that would send a signal to the general assembly.”
During the 2026 session, the state legislature passed House Enrolled Act 1002, which offered multiple solutions to high electric bills, including prohibiting utilities from disconnecting low-income customers’ services during extreme heat warnings and tying utility profits to performance metrics.
Since HEA 1002 passed this year, Olson believes the statehouse might wait longer to tackle electric affordability again. He thinks they’ll wait to see what the IURC does and go from there.
He believes Hoosiers might see more bills focused on private equity ownership of utilities, eliminating or lessening utility sales tax, sharing fuel costs between ratepayers and shareholders, or eliminating late fees and penalties.
“I’m not sure how much of an appetite there will be to revisit that stuff, but with affordability and energy prices almost being the top issue in the election, I don’t know how the general assembly doesn’t do something to improve outcomes,” Olson said.
Tony Sindone, director of the Center for Economic Education and Research at Indiana University Northwest, said the economy was a factor in the election results. “I think people just wanted change. People were just getting a little anxious with higher costs.”
Last year, the big concern was tariffs; this year, it’s energy costs, he said.
AI and data centers played a role, too, Sindone said.
“They’ve heard some good things about AI, some bad things about AI,” Sindone said. “I think it’s a communication problem.” Developers putting in data centers and public officials need to tell the story better, he said.
“When people think they don’t have the information they need to make a decision, they fill in the blanks, and the information they fill it in with generally isn’t good,” Sindone said.
He understands the economics of the land sales to Amazon and Microsoft, the two biggest players when it comes to Northwest Indiana data centers. “If I’m a farmer and someone’s offering a premium price for my land and I can make more money selling my land than farming it, what do you think I’m going to do?”
“Why do we need so many data centers,” he asked. “Why can’t they just make AI smarter so we don’t have to take up as much space with all these servers?”
“We can’t put the genie back in the bottle, so it’s happening,” Sindone said. Make sure it’s as efficient as possible, he urged.
Indiana Democratic Party Chair Karen Tallian, formerly a State Senator from Ogden Dunes, offered her take on it in a statement.
“The overarching theme of this election is that Hoosiers were struggling with high costs and tired of the corrupt, out-of-touch Republican supermajority legislature failing to listen to them,” she said. “Over the years, Republicans rushed through billions in tax breaks to trillion-dollar corporations. This was done at the expense of Hoosier public schools, roads, childcare and healthcare. Voters wanted a change in priorities and a legislature focused on affordability, from pre-K to healthcare to senior care. That’s why Hoosiers elected Democrats in record numbers.”
“It is clear that AI needs to be regulated, and it is likely that this will be done at the federal level by the incoming Democratic Congress, since Republicans have consistently refused to stand up to Big Tech,” she said.
“Democrats won all over Indiana and all over the country. This was about more than one issue; this wave was a widespread backlash against extreme and out-of-touch policies coming out of both Washington and Indianapolis,” Tallian said.
The Indiana Republican Party didn’t respond to a request for comment. Porter County Republican Chair Nathan Uldricks declined comment.
In Porter County, voter turnout was 45% in this month’s election. Of straight-party tickets, 53.6 went to Democrats compared to 46% for Republicans.
Porter County Democratic Chair Don Craft said his party has seen a surge of supporters over concerns about data centers and AI.
“Obviously, a lot of people have strong feelings about data centers. A lot of Democrats are worried about the environmental effects,” he said. Others are worried about them being too close to residential areas.
Travis Gearhart, political director for the Indiana Libertarian Party, ran unsuccessfully against Soliday and Kominakis. “Just look at my race. You had (Operating Engineers Local) 150 endorse the Republican, Soliday, and they also pulled their endorsement from Dawn Miller on the basis that she was very vocal against data centers,” Gearhart said. Miller, a Republican, ran unsuccessfully for Porter County Council District 3.
Operating Engineers Local 150 represents construction workers who would benefit from the jobs created from building data centers.
“I think (data centers) played a huge role” in the election, Gearhart said.
In his own race, Soliday “was very heavily in league with a lot of people who were pro-data center,” Gearhart said.
“If they’re that great, why do we need tax cuts for them in the first place,” Gearhart asked. “I’m not saying there has to be some nefarious anything, but where there’s smoke, there’s fire.”
In Hobart, data center opponents have been vocal.
“They just had a sit-down with the data center people that they had some things they wanted them to consider,” Gearhart said. With the developer making a lot of promises, “all we want to do is make you hear these concerns,” he said, which he hopes can be addressed.
Matt Reardon, founding partner with MCR Partners and a longtime economic development proponent, is accustomed to hearing complaints and concerns.
“This is the same type of challenge that development projects have had forever related to rural areas, no matter what it is, he said. With distribution centers and lifestyle centers, residents worried about truck traffic, air pollution, noise, wear and tear on roads and other concerns.
“There’s always been the battle of land rights vs. other people telling people what to do,” Reardon said.
“I don’t think it’s different. I just think there’s been more activity around this particular industry in the last four years,” he said. “At some point, that activity will dissipate. Communities that have managed to land project will reap the benefits, and communities that have chosen for whatever reasons not to do them will be where they’re at.”
“It’s really more mathematical than emotional,” Reardon said.
“Communities that prepared, communities that have done their ordinance work and followed up and understand an industry, like they should be doing, will attract investment, like anything else,” he said.
With data centers, “the spend is a thousand times more. The investment is a thousand times more, and there’s a lot of fear and uncertainty about what it is,” Reardon said.
“Indirectly, you’re going to have the haves and the have-nots created by the decisions made by local officials and bodies,” he said.
Doug Ross is a freelance reporter for the Post-Tribune.
