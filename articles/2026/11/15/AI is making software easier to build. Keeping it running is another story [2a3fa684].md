# AI is making software easier to build. Keeping it running is another story

Fast Company · Kolawole Samuel Adebayo · 2026-11-15

These days, it almost feels like old news: Developers can now use AI tools to generate code, build features, and make changes in a fraction of the time they once needed. The problem now surfacing across businesses is that once software goes into production, teams still need to monitor, maintain, and fix it. A feature that takes minutes to build can introduce problems that take engineers hours to investigate.
Google’s 2025 DORA report, based on responses from nearly 5,000 technology professionals, found that greater AI adoption was associated with faster software delivery but reduced stability. While the researchers didn’t establish that AI-generated code directly caused more outages, their findings suggest that companies can produce software faster without necessarily becoming better at maintaining it.
That raises a critical question for companies investing heavily in AI coding tools: If development keeps getting faster, how much of the work involved in running software can also be automated? A growing group of companies is working to find out, developing AI systems that can investigate failures, identify possible causes, and, in some cases, take corrective action without waiting for an engineer.
MORE SOFTWARE, MORE TO MANAGE
Ishay Yaari, cofounder and CEO of DataAgent, a company developing autonomous remediation software, says the operational side of software development hasn’t advanced at the same pace as coding.
“More code, more frequent deployments, and more infrastructure changes naturally create more potential failure points,” he tells Fast Company.
For site reliability engineering (SRE) teams, that means managing applications and infrastructure that may be changing more frequently. When something fails, engineers must figure out what changed, identify the cause, and restore service.
Consider an application that begins crashing after an update. The cause could be faulty code, an incorrect configuration, insufficient computing resources, or a problem with another service. While monitoring tools may detect the failure immediately, engineers still need to examine logs, review recent changes, and investigate several possibilities before deciding what to fix.
