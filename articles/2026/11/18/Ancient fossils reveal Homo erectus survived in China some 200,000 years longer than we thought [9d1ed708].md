# Ancient fossils reveal Homo erectus survived in China some 200,000 years longer than we thought

Scientific American · Jacek Krywko · 2026-11-18

The ancient human Homo erectus emerged nearly two million years ago and spread from Africa to as far as the Indonesian island of Java. Now fossils discovered in two caves in southern China are helping to color in parts of this ancient human ancestor’s journey. By analyzing the fossils, researchers have found that H. erectus survived on the Asian mainland for around 200,000 years longer than we previously thought. And it did so in dense rainforest, long considered the particular domain of Homo sapiens.
The conclusions, published in two new studies in Nature, run contrary to assumptions, based on past fossil finds, that H. erectus was a specialist species that preferred grass and woodlands and that struggled to adapt to different habitats. Before these new discoveries, scientists had found no securely dated fossils younger than about 400,000 years on the Asian mainland. The last known members of the species lived on Java until about 110,000 years ago, and encroaching rainforests have been blamed for their extinction.
“The idea never really sat very well with me,” says Kira Westaway, a geochronologist at Macquarie University in Australia, who co-led one of the studies. H. erectus, she explains, survived for a really long time, which made her doubt the species lacked the flexibility to live in different environments.

Westaway’s team dated the fossil-bearing layers of China’s Naxian Cave and Baxian Cave with five independent methods, placing the H. erectus fossils there sometime between 324,000 and 184,000 years ago. Carbon isotopes in the enamel of animal teeth at the sites showed that animals in the caves fed exclusively in the closed-canopy forest that once surround them. And H. erectus teeth that were also found there carried similar signatures. This suggests these ancient people were not just passing through the rainforest but lived there.
H. erectus also appears to have been a keen traveler. The teeth contain strontium, an element taken up from food and water whose chemical signature reflects the underlying rock. In the H. erectus teeth, the signature didn’t match the local limestone, suggesting these individuals traveled a lot, possibly from as far as northern Vietnam, some 250 kilometers away.
“This emphasizes that behavioral flexibility was likely a key to Homo erectus success, both in terms of the long duration of the species and its broad dispersal,” says Susan Antón, a paleoanthropologist at New York University, who was not involved in the new research.
In the other Nature study, a second team extracted ancient proteins from the tooth enamel.
“Proteins, especially skeletal proteins, fall apart inherently slower than ancient DNA does,” says Frido Welker, a molecular anthropologist at the University of Copenhagen and senior author of the study. Four teeth carried a version of the enamel protein ameloblastin, which had only been seen in older H. erectus fossils found in China, but they lacked another variant those fossils share. Welker’s team interpreted that as a clue that the newly analyzed teeth belonged to a previously unknown lineage of H. erectus.
“I appreciated how Welker and his colleagues came to the conclusion that they were looking at a novel Homo erectus lineage,” says Sheela Athreya, a paleoanthropologist at Texas A&M University, who was not involved in either study. “I would not be surprised if these results are confirmed by other studies.”
One unsolved mystery from the new research is that another hominin tooth found in the Baxian cave fit neither H. erectus nor Neanderthals nor modern humans. “I think Denisovans would be the first option to consider,” Welker says. He cautions, however, that no comparable Denisovan tooth exists to check against and that proteins hold far less concrete information than DNA.
“This is important because these hominins could have been related to Denisovans that already inhabited Asia or even Neanderthals in Europe,” says Karen Baab, a paleoanthropologist at Midwestern University in Arizona, who was not involved in the new research. “I hope there will be future fossils from southern China that can help confirm this species diagnosis.”
The search for such new fossils, Westaway says, is already ongoing. Her team returns to China every year, with cavers exploring four or five caves a day. “There’s a lot to be done,” she says.
