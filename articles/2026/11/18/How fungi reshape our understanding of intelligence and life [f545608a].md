# How fungi reshape our understanding of intelligence and life

Scientific American · Brianne Kane, Adam Kovac, Sarah Lewin Frasier, Jennifer Hackett, Fonda Mwangi, Alex Sugiura · 2026-11-18

Rachel Feltman: For Scientific American’s Science Quickly, I’m Rachel Feltman.
For today’s episode, I’m handing the mic over to our books and culture lead Bri Kane. She’s here to kick off a monthly series where SciAm staffers discuss a book they all read together. We’re hoping you’ll want to join us for these virtual book clubs, not just by listening to our chats but by checking out each month’s selection for yourself.
This month the SciAm staff has been digging into the far-out world of fungus with Merlin Sheldrake’s Entangled Life.

Bri has brought along news editor Sarah Lewin Frasier, breaking news editor Adam Kovac and copy editor Jenni Hacket to discuss this month’s buddy read. Here’s their conversation.
Bri Kane: Hi, everyone. I’ve been reading Entangled Life, by Merlin Sheldrake, for weeks now, and my brain has gone all mushy with mushroom facts. I can’t stop talking about them. My friends are getting worried about the fungus in my life. So I need to talk to my favorite science nerds about all of my favorite parts about this book.
So this is Entangled Life, by Merlin Sheldrake. It’s our November buddy read pick for Scientific American: 227 pages, not including an additional 125 pages or so of notes and index in the back. The sources in this bad boy are nothing to be skipped over, I will say. This is the study of mycology, which is the study of fungus, but we’re gonna get into all the different fields of science that mycology actually branches into, which is freaky and fun.
But today on the pod, I have Sarah, news editor and reader of whatever pops up on the family’s shared Kindle account; Adam, our breaking news reporter and serious nonfiction reader; and Jeni, copy editor, who will read just about anything from physics to fairies. So hey, everyone. Can you just say hi with your name so that our listeners can, kind of, tell us all apart?
Adam Kovac: Hey, this is Adam.
Sarah Lewin Frasier: Hi, I’m Sarah.
Jennifer Hackett: Hi, I’m Jeni.
Kane: Awesome. All right, well, first up: pop quiz. Adam, what was your favorite freaky fungus fact from this book?
Kovac: So the thing that I can’t get out of my head is the relationship between fungus and plants and how, like, they actually trade nutrients and carbon, and like, they have this real, kind of, barter system that’s going on. I did not know that. It’s kind of blowing my mind. And, like, that there’s some funguses that will, like, pull carbon from other plants and then trade it with, like, this other tree. Like, they have, this whole marketplace that’s going on, like, right under our feet, and now I can’t go outside without thinking about that.
Kane: I know, and they talk about in the book, like, Merlin explains that these analogies to, like, marketplaces or family dynamics, passing down carbon, mother trees, all of this really informs our understanding of these fungus but also kind of precolor our assumptions about it, which is really interesting. Is it a free market? Are they sharing in a socialist kind of way, or are they getting something out of it in a capitalist kind of way?
Kovac: Like, I kind of wonder if, you know, some fungus accuse others of, like, being too woke and like, you know, they’re having political disagreements about how they trade carbon. I’d like to imagine that that’s the case
Kane: Yeah, I don’t know what debates the fungus and the plants are getting into, but honestly, I just wanna sit in on that conversation.
Jeni, I know that you are a big science fan, but I know you read a lot of fantasy and science fiction. So what’s the freaky fungus fact that jumped out to your particular brain?
Hackett: Well, to me, every fungus fact is freaky. I am the resident one who’s terrified of mushrooms. But what really stood out to me while reading this is the question of what makes something intelligent, that, sort of, throughline through the book. I was fascinated by the discussion of how we determine intelligence and how fungi can show intelligence, can navigate, can communicate. I don’t like knowing it is so smart, and that is one of the reasons why I fear it. So I just feel more validated in my fear now.
Kane: It’s disturbing. They think without brains. They seem to speak without mouths and can hear information from other fungus families without having ears, and they’re also everywhere. I mean, I think that’s something else about this book. It really reminds you: Guys, there’s so much fungus in the room with us right now. [Laughs.]
Hackett: There’s a quote where he says, “Fungus is all around you and inside you.” And I’m like, “I know that already, and I don’t need to be reminded because I hate it.”
Kane: I can really relate to that, Jeni. I think it’s really interesting, but also I can’t think about it for too long because then I need to start washing my hands 100 times in a row.
Okay, Sarah, I know that you’re a total speed reader, so what is it about this book that you have devoured in the last, like, four days?
Lewin Frasier: Well, so one fact that stuck with me was that there’s this specific species of split gill mushroom. The fungus that grows it has more than 23,000 mating types, like 23,000 sexes, most of which can mate with each other but not all of them. Each is compatible with only about 98 percent of the population. It seems like it would be, like, an interesting dating app to put together.
Kane: I’ll say, that was definitely one of the things that I’ve been talking to a lot of my friends about this book, is mushrooms are queer as hell. The natural kingdom of life is so much more diverse than XX and XY. There’s so much going on with mushrooms that we can learn from and be able to maybe expand our understanding of mating types and sexual relationships.
I mean, this also reminded me of a book that we read last year that I really liked, [The] Sexual Evolution, from Nathan Lents, talks about how queer the entire natural kingdom is. And I think mushrooms is a really freaky but accurate example of just how weird things can get. The fun fact that I keep telling people about: So we all know penicillin, right? Penicillin is based on a fungus. It was very important. This is a fact that a lot of people know off the top of their head. But did you know that we couldn’t make penicillin at an industrial scale for all the people that needed it for many, many years until lab assistant Mary Hunt saw a “pretty golden mold”, and she realized that that could completely change our penicillin production. And so I just have to say: shout out to Mary Hunt forever. She is the reason why penicillin is in hospitals every single day this year and is saving lives. So I need us all to remember Mary’s name a little bit more than everyone already is.
So one thing that all book clubs do, and all buddies who read books together do, is we have to have a final score. Was it hot or not? Is this a five-star read that you’re gonna tell everyone they have to read forever? Is this a book you’re gonna forget that you read this time last year and forget all of these fungus facts? Adam, what do you think? How many stars is it?
Kovac: I am going to give this seven slices of mushroom pizza out of eight. I found it a little bit repetitive at times, but just the content of it is so interesting, and it opens up this whole world that we largely ignore and is invisible. Uh, so that I really, really enjoyed. It’s a little new universe that’s just right below our feet.
Kane: Absolutely. Seven out of eight mushroom slices of pizza, that feels right to me. Sarah, what about you? What’s your final score?
Lewin Frasier: I give it, like, four stars. I think it’s a really fun exercise to try and wrap your mind around things like this, and I have to give him a full five stars for commitment. He does say at the end that he’s going to dampen a copy of the book, grow mushrooms on it, eat those mushrooms, dampen a second copy, grow mushrooms, brew those into beer and drink the beer. Assuming he actually followed through with that, I think that’s true dedication to consuming the book yourself before others consume it.
Kane: I can say I found the YouTube video of him eating those mushrooms, and it is so amazing and freaky, and Merlin needs to come sit next to me. I have so many questions. Okay, Jeni, what’s your final score?
Hackett: I’m in awe of this man. My final score is: I agree; I’ll do four out of five of those red-and-white cottagecore mushrooms. I was fascinated. I also listened to the audiobook for this one, and I found it an excellent audiobook. It is read by the author, and I think that helped a bit with the repetitiveness that Adam mentioned. I didn’t notice it as much ’cause it just felt like I was listening to my new best mushroom buddy tell me a story about the weirdest thing that he loves, and it was a great time and terrifying.
Kane: I completely agree. I think it can be repetitive. Merlin definitely uses a rhetorical device of asking a question and then answering it across the chapter and maybe asking another question that’s pretty similar halfway through that chapter. I forgive him, honestly. I am going to do some radical chemistry and give this a million umpteen thousand stars because it is one giant organism, if you think about it. It is just one mushroom that has a million hyphae reaching out to everything else on the forest floor, and if you think about it, it is both a million things and one thing at the same time. It’s a mushroom.
Thank you guys so much for being my buddy this month. I love reading with you all. So thank you so much for hanging out with me this month, everyone.
Kovac: Bri, this was so much fun. Thanks for having me read this book and coming on to talk about it.
Lewin Frasier: Thanks, Bri, for letting me say some funky facts.
