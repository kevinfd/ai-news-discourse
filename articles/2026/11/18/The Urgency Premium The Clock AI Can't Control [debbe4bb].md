# The Urgency Premium: The Clock AI Can't Control

Forbes · Adam Thier, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-18

Adam Thier is the Chief Product and Technology Officer at Anaplan, a leading AI-driven scenario planning and analysis platform.
One of the most hyped benefits of AI is that it will speed up decision making from weeks to minutes. It’s a good story, and parts of it are already true.
But as any technology leader who’s tried to operationalize AI knows, moving fast means nothing if the rest of the world doesn’t move as fast as you do. 
Here’s a simple example that has nothing to do with AI and everything to do with why “faster decisions” alone won’t fix the problem. 
You have a document that needs to arrive at your client’s office on Friday. If you send it a week ahead of time, it costs $0.82, the price of a first-class stamp. Wait until two days before, and it costs maybe $12. The day before? The cost of express delivery can jump to over $30. 
In every version, the mail truck didn’t get faster because you decided faster—all that changed was how much of its schedule you had left to use. 
Most leaders write this off as a rounding error, a one-time tax on poor planning. But the same dynamic plays out across hiring, procurement, sales and product launches. In each, waiting can destroy significant value.
I call this the “urgency premium,” the avoidable value lost to insufficient lead time. 
There’s a tendency for leaders to treat lateness as binary: you either hit your deadline, or you didn’t. But value deteriorates long before the deadline arrives, whether or not anyone notices. 
Stop targeting the last possible date.
Most organizations plan backward from the last possible date an action can be taken.
My take: That’s the wrong target. By that point, your highest-value options are likely already gone. Instead, leaders should track option cliffs, or the points where a preferred resource, path or choice stops being available. 
Let’s go back to the product launch example. The launch date is one deadline, but there are many others hiding in the months leading up to it.
There is a date when your preferred implementation partner can no longer guarantee availability, and a point when compressing testing or security review begins to compromise quality. Those are the cliffs leaders need to identify before work begins. 
To find them, ask three questions for all major decisions:
1. How fast do costs escalate as time runs out?
2. How fast does execution quality decline?
3. When do your best options actually expire? 
Of the three questions, the third is especially important. If a preferred option disappears weeks before a launch, managing to the launch date means managing to the wrong deadline. 
Know when waiting is worth it.
Not every decision should be made as early as possible. Sometimes waiting has real value. Requirements may become clearer, markets may shift and new information can lead to better decisions.
Make that trade-off explicit: what you gain by waiting has to be worth more than the options you’ll lose, the costs you’ll incur and the execution quality you’ll sacrifice. 
AI may make decisions dramatically faster, but that only makes timing more important.
