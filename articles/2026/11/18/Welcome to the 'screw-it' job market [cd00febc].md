# Welcome to the 'screw-it' job market

Business Insider · Ana Altchek, Ana Altchek (aaltchek@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-18

Getty Images; Alyssa powell/BI Amanda McDormand's cover letter opened with a bold pitch. "This isn't a job application—it's an idea, and I'll get straight to it. I'm a corporate fixer. If you've watched Scandal , think Olivia Pope. If you're more of a Ray Donovan person, that works too. And if you're into music, I like to consider myself the Taylor Swift of business." It's one of several Hail Mary strategies 43-year-old McDormand tried when entering an unfamiliar landscape after being laid off from Comcast in December. In 2019, the New-Hampshire based senior leader had been turning down offers while exploring new job opportunities. This time, her LinkedIn feed was inundated with posts about job seekers who'd sent hundreds of applications that went unanswered over years-long searches. Still, she wasn't prepared for her inbox to be flooded with auto rejections . With a few months of severance left, McDormand decided she had some time to experiment — and not much to lose. She tried the direct route with an "I am not AI" bit in her cover letter. In another, she wove in golf references for a hiring manager who liked the sport. Her experiments, indeed, led to more callbacks, but her first big win — which resulted in a fractional role before her current full-time job — was a cover letter laced with Taylor Swift lyrics. She described "identifying the blank space" within an organization, told the employer they've "seen this film before, and it's time to change the ending," and closed by saying, "This could be our era, just say 'yes'. As AI slop crowds the job market, applicants are taking drastic measures to show hiring managers they’re human — and nothing is off the table. Beyond cover-letter riddles , recruiters say they’ve seen a resurgence of résumé interest sections with odd details and more candid personal statements. Some include fun-fact infographics. Others may be purposefully including punctuation or spelling errors to signal they wrote it themselves. Carly Martinetti, cofounder of PR and AI visibility firm Notably, said that one job seeker mentioned in her LinkedIn reach-out that she's "been an in-house girly my whole career." That's "totally not something AI would say," Martinetti said, adding that she found the message down-to-Earth. Among the entries recruiters and candidates shared with me: "buffalo chicken wraps," the legal drama "Suits," "vampire and Robert Pattinson movies," "clowning," "mermaiding," "lucid dreaming," "love of french fries," "superhero," and "movie theme dinner party thrower." Candidates are left wondering: "How do I signal effectively to people that I'm not a machine?" Marc Cenedella, founder and CEO of Ladders The effort comes at a time when over 84% of job seekers use AI to write or edit their résumés, according to recent survey data from Ladders. The report also found that 76% of 237 surveyed job seekers have taken at least one deliberate step to sound less like AI. Before AI, Ladders' founder and CEO Marc Cenedella says he "rarely saw people adding personal anecdotes to their résumés. It was almost a faux pas." Nowadays, Cenedella says, candidates are left wondering: "How do I signal effectively to people that I'm not a machine?" Job seekers have long found a way to flex on their résumés by including marathon training, freelance work, or volunteering under a section labeled "hobbies," "skills," or "extracurriculars." Cover letters similarly gave applicants space to expand on accomplishments, explain a career pivot, or reveal a bit more about themselves. In a crowded job market, those extra details are showing up in stranger places — and sometimes taking on a more unconventional, and cringey form. Applicants who have spent months sending out résumés with little response said they eventually adopted a "screw it" mentality. Some have written sob-story cover letters and posted them on social media to share their frustration. One Reddit user shared a cover letter in a post titled "The cover letter I finally had the balls to send, because I have nothing to lose," which opened with recounting their various unanswered applications to the company. If a niche confession catches a hiring manager's eye, some applicants figure it may be worth a little embarrassment. The move is also a response to the sheer volume of applications recruiters now have to sort through. A 2026 Greenhouse analysis found that companies receive an average of 194 applications per hire. AI has made the process of sifting through hundreds of résumés even more monotonous. Many recruiters are growing tired of reading perfectly tailored résumés that pull exact language from the job description, and 300-word AI-polished notes that all sound vaguely similar. "When you've been looking at résumés for an hour, you start to get really picky," said Kristin Raiford, an executive recruiter who previously led executive search for Amazon ads and finance. "You're like, what's different about this person from the last person? Why do I pick them and not them?" The same Greenhouse report said that 3.2% of applications reach a first interview. Listing college football as a hobby may not be enough to set you apart from the masses these days. However, claiming to know the first 100 digits of pi may be just enough to make a hiring manager pause. Kelli Hrivnak, a tech and marketing recruiter, still remembers a résumé submitted years ago from a candidate who claimed just that. The person wasn't hired, and the fact didn't change her view of their qualifications — but it stuck with her. McDormand recently received 220 applications for a role she posted just days earlier. The candidates getting her attention are the ones who give a recruiter something to remember, weird or not. "After you've seen it like a dozen times, you're like, 'I don't know who this person is. This person is not giving me anything,'" McDormand said, adding that she appreciates the unconventional approach. Sharing a quirky interest or hobby offers a two-for-one advantage for some applicants. It can jolt a hiring manager out of résumé fatigue — and also give the candidate an easy transition into talking about their skill set in an interview. Kelsey Anderson, a graduate student now applying for jobs, lists "Julia Child" in her résumé's interest section. It can lead to a straightforward conversation about cooking — or help her transition to a deeper topic. For example, she could discuss American food dynamics, Child's love of travel, her late-career pivot, or her feminist advocacy. For others, hobbies reflect their skill set. Paige Maly, who works in marketing, said she got more callbacks than ever after adding visual scale of five of her wildest adventures to her résumé. Paige Maly said she received more callbacks than ever after adding a visual scale of adventures she's taken to her résumé. Paige Maly She said that section became a conversation starter in every interview. For Maly, it was more than a stunt. It was proof of the qualities she believes marketing leaders want: independence, decisiveness, and a willingness to take risks. "I'm OK with making moves," Maly said. All of these tactics are ultimately a gamble, even if they pay off. While a bold move could result in a job offer, it could also turn off the recruiter. Some hiring managers may appreciate the added flair, but others prefer résumés to remain strictly professional . Erica Keswin, a former executive recruiter and bestselling workplace strategy and human leadership author, said that including wild details could "really backfire" with the wrong crowd. Creative industries might be more receptive to this approach — but old-school law firms or consulting giants might not be as keen on eccentric confessions. Keswin said that including a unique hobby can be a smart move to humanize your application, but she wouldn't recommend tacking on an especially odd confession just because it worked out for someone else. "I wouldn't always say, 'plug and play,'" Keswin said. "Because if it doesn't feel like you, and someone asks you about it, it's going to have the opposite effect." For McDormand, a self-described "Swiftie for life," it was genuine, although she said she researched the companies before sending applications to decide whether to go the traditional or creative route . McDormand said weaving song lyrics into her application gave her an opening in an interview to spend 10 minutes discussing Swift's marketing playbook and how she used similar tactics to set herself apart. She said the ability to connect pop culture into meaningful strategy was both effective and memorable. Those conversations are what made the biggest impact in actually landing a job, she said. "It's a technique to get in the door," McDormand says, "But once you get in the door, you have to have the wherewithal to be able to back up the conversation."
