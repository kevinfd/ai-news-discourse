# Australia has a reputation for being the most secretive democracy in the world. New laws could make it worse | Tom Sulston

The Guardian · Tom Sulston · 2026-11-18

In 2019, the New York Times described Australia as “the world’s most secretive democracy”. Since then, a succession of defence, anti-terror and surveillance bills over consecutive governments have stripped away parliamentary power in favour of executive power
In this tradition, the government has been quiet about the accelerating capability and protecting bases bill that’s currently being rushed through parliament. This bill would allow the minister to grant exemptions from Australian laws to defence officials, the definition of which is to be expanded to include third-party contractors.
Equality under the law is not only a fundamental human rights principle but also underpins core Australian values of fairness and mateship. We might accept that there are valid reasons for defence officials to be granted additional legal protection during the course of the work – soldiers can carry automatic weapons that are not legal for the rest of us. However, the bill also expands the definition of “defence official” to include commercial contractors working for the department of defence. That would allow the minister to permit mercenaries working for private military contractors to operate in Australia with legal impunity.
At the same time, tech corporations in the United States are expanding their customer base to include national defence departments. AI hyperscalers like OpenAI and Anthropic are now defence contractors, as are cloud providers like Google and Microsoft, as are defence data specialists like Palantir. This move to military work is especially concerning, given that these tech companies operate on the internet – core civilian infrastructure on which Australian civic life depends.
Last week, the government announced that OpenAI would not face criminal charges over their recent Medicare data breach. Despite flagging changes to Australia’s criminal law, the government seems willing to accept criminal behaviour by AI companies in their desperate scramble for productivity.
In future, the Australian defence minister would have discretion to declare staff working for these corporations as “defence officials” and grant them pre-emptive exemptions from Australian laws. Naturally, this would be subject to national security provisions to restrict public and parliamentary scrutiny, with hefty punishments for whistleblowers.
AI corporations need to host their website-hacking agents somewhere. The proposed bill would roll out the red carpet with “defence security zones”. This part of the bill allows the defence minister to define both military and civilian infrastructure as deserving of special treatment – places where the public are allowed neither access nor transparency. The US military listening station at Pine Gap is one such place, where Australians are not allowed to walk the perimeter or take photographs. Fair enough, it’s a military installation and the Americans are pretty sensitive. But AI corporations also now create military products: when Anthropic released its “Fable” model, the US government treated it like defence materiel and prohibited foreign nationals from using it.
AI corporations are rushing to construct datacentres in suburban Australia to host their products. But local residents typically don’t want to have a massive datacentre at the end of their road, taking their data, taking their electricity, taking their jobs. They especially don’t want a commercially operated mini Pine Gap that would constitute a military target. Which is why we’re seeing such strong opposition to datacentre construction across the country. But with this bill, the federal government can restrict protests and civil disobedience against datacentres by designating them “defence security zones”, with heavy jail terms on offer for those who would disrupt the important AI business of making deepfake nudes and targeting missile strikes in Gaza.
All of this is scary. Those of us who remember the internet as the world’s town square are grieving – coming to terms with military corporations transforming it into a theatre of war. Instead of granting special dispensation to harm us, our government needs to strengthen legal protection for civilians. Australia’s lack of a human rights act is an urgent issue. Without legally recognised and enforceable human rights, we’re all living in a dystopian world built for corporate AI profit and blessed by our government.
