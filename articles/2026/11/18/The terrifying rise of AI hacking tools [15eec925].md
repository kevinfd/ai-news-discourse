# The terrifying rise of AI hacking tools

Axios · Sam Sabin · 2026-11-18

A new breed of widely available AI tools is helping hackers launch cyberattacks around the clock , breaching corporate networks in a matter of hours and overwhelming defenders. Why it matters: AI-enabled hacking is still in its infancy, but open-source tools — which are free to download — are fundamentally changing the economics of cybercrime. Hackers can launch self-adjusting attack plans, allowing agents to find vulnerabilities and steal credentials, sometimes before human defenders realize their systems are being probed. Driving the news: Recent cyberattacks involving publicly available "AI agent frameworks" are drawing warnings about how quickly hackers can infiltrate corporate networks. The FBI warned Friday that hackers have used an AI framework called Artex to attack organizations worldwide, with some intrusions progressing from initial targeting to privileged access in less than three hours. In a separate report Friday , Amazon said hackers used Artex to steal AI model credentials, then used those stolen resources to power additional attacks. Last week, developer platform Railway and blockchain infrastructure provider Chainstack also reported attacks involving Artex. Palo Alto Networks said Thursday that Chinese-speaking hackers used another open-source framework, Cairn, to conduct 25 operations against at least 19 organizations between August and September. In a separate report Monday, Google said hackers used Cairn during an intrusion into the U.S. aerospace sector. CrowdStrike also saw one group using Artex to target more than 200 organizations worldwide over 11 days last month. Reality check : Cyberattacks are still scary and tech resiliency is still built on a faulty internet foundation, making mishaps possible even without clear evidence of AI-assisted hacks. A litany of popular services — including Microsoft , Apple , Chase , United , Workday , the Federal Aviation Administration , the National Security Agency — have all reported outages in the last week. An unknown group infected about 178 major websites with crypto-stealing malware through the Optimizely Web experimentation platform in less than a day, according to Report URI . Affected websites included Zoom, the BBC, CNN, Salesforce, Gitlab and Ikea, and AI doesn't seem to be involved as of now. What they're saying: "We're seeing more active attacks than ever right now," Sam Rubin, senior vice president of Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 threat intelligence and consulting unit, told Axios. "The open-weight models are getting close to frontier-level capabilities," and when combined with tools like Artex and Cairn, they are "really hitting organizations hard," he added. Incident response firms worldwide are stretched to capacity, with some turning away cases because they lack resources, John Hultquist, chief analyst at Google's threat intelligence group, told Axios. How it works: Artex and Cairn use AI agents to automate tasks hackers traditionally performed themselves. A hacker gives the framework a target and an objective, such as breaking into a company's network. AI agents develop attack plans, search for vulnerabilities and use hacking tools to test possible entry points. The agents share findings and adjust tactics when something fails, allowing them to operate around the clock without constant human supervision. Between the lines: Hackers are also stealing the computing resources needed to sustain their AI-powered attacks. Amazon's findings show how attackers can steal access to AI models and use that access to find more credentials, expanding their operations without paying for the computing power. The intrigue: These frameworks aren't necessarily good at hiding their activity, but that might not matter. Hultquist said autonomous attacks generate substantial suspicious activity, potentially making them easier to detect. But financially motivated hackers may only need to steal valuable information before defenders stop them. He also warned that state-backed hackers could use these capabilities to disrupt critical systems before defenders can intervene. What to watch: Security teams may need to fundamentally change their defenses to keep pace with automated attacks.
