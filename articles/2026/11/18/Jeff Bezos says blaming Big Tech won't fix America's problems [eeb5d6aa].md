# Jeff Bezos says blaming Big Tech won't fix America's problems

Business Insider · Katherine Li, Katherine Li (katherineli@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-18

Jeff Bezos says politicians are making Big Tech the latest villain as voters focus on affordability. Kimberly White/Getty Images for Semafor Jeff Bezos says politicians are making Big Tech the latest villain following Wall Street. Bezos says "most people in tech just want to stay heads down doing the work." Data centers, job concerns, and AI surveillance tools have all fueled resistance against Big Tech. Jeff Bezos says Big Tech has become America's new favorite villain. Speaking with Semafor's Liz Hoffman at Semafor's Silicon Valley & The World conference, the Amazon founder said on Tuesday that politicians have a habit of picking villains rather than addressing the root causes of economic problems. "That is the age-old political technique, which is pick a villain," Bezos said. "And pick a villain is a good political technique, but it's a terrible technique for solving problems." Bezos said public perceptions of powerful industries have shifted over time, from lawyers to Wall Street to landlords, and now it's Big Tech's turn. "From time to time, landlords are the villains. And now, Big Tech is the villain. Tech is the villain," Bezos said. "That is the picture that is being painted. It's not correct. It's not a true picture." "I think most people in tech just want to stay heads down doing the work. I don't think there's a lot of villainy here," Bezos added. Bezos's comments come as Big Tech faces growing public scrutiny over its expanding footprint in everyday life, from data centers that can drive up local utility costs to AI surveillance tools such as Flock's network of license-plate-reading cameras. Concerns about privacy, energy costs, and the impact of AI on jobs have fueled resistance to the industry's growing influence. Those concerns have intersected with a broader affordability backlash. Higher household costs, including war-related pressure on fuel and some consumer prices, and persistently high housing costs weighed on voters in the 2026 midterm elections. In ABC News' election polling, 89% of voters said the cost of living was a crucial or major factor in their vote. Affordability concerns have become a defining political issue, helping Democrats flip key seats and win majorities in both chambers of Congress in the latest election. Economic anxiety cut across party lines. Some Democrats who won, including Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan and James Talarico in Texas, made data-center development and AI's potential effects on workers part of their campaigns.
