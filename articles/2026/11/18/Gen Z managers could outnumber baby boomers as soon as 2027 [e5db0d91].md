# Gen Z managers could outnumber baby boomers as soon as 2027

Business Insider · Juliana Kaplan,Madison Hoff, Juliana Kaplan,Madison Hoff (jkaplan@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-18

Twelve percent of managers are Gen Zers, and that is expected to tick up next year. ZenSaBi/Getty Images Gen Zers could overtake baby boomers as managers as soon as next year. As boomers retire, Gen Zers are moving up in their careers and feeling positive about the workplace. They'll bring their own style of leadership, including a desire for mentorship and in-person work. For years, Gen Z has been moving up from their first jobs. But in boardrooms and cubicles across the country, the not-so-entry-level-anymore generation is preparing to become bosses. A new report from job sites Indeed and Glassdoor finds that Gen Z now makes up just under a quarter of the labor force, and 12% of managers. The job sites expect that if trends continue, Gen Z will surpass the now-retiring baby boomers in management as soon as next year and Gen X in 2033. That might come as a surprise to any employers who think Gen Z are still teenagers just getting into the job market, Laura Ullrich, director of economic research in North America at the Indeed Hiring Lab, said. But the oldest Gen Zers turn 30 next year, marking a new generational shift. And, despite the challenges facing younger workers — like fading entry-level opportunities — Gen Z is still showing up at work. On the whole, the oldest Gen Zers and youngest millennials are more likely to be employed than the rest of the labor force. As the Gen Z takeover kicks off, they're bringing their own spin on management. "They are quite positive and even more positive than the millennials were a few years ago when they were the same age, which I think is remarkable given everything that we've been through in the past few years," Ullrich said. An optimistic generation Despite the historically strange labor market circumstances of a pandemic and the subsequent rise of AI shaping Gen Z's entry into the workforce, the generation is still feeling good. The report showed that Gen Zers in a survey run by Indeed were more optimistic about getting ahead and being more successful in their careers over the next five years than their older peers. Gen Z's older generational sibling, millennials, are also a formidable force in the workplace. For now, millennials still make up 38% of managers, with their managerial market share only set to grow. And those generations can be at odds over what's important to them: According to the Indeed survey, millennials are more likely to care about remote work options, with over a third of millennials saying that they prefer primarily remote arrangements, while 29% of Gen Zers said so. The new report said 58% of Gen Zers want a mentor but only a quarter are provided one from their employer. "They're very eager to be mentored. They're very eager to engage when we ask them what type of skills they want. They want more communication skills," Ullrich said. "I think they know that's a shortcoming of their generation, partially because of what happened during the COVID pandemic." Ben Whybrow, a 22-year-old in the UK, experienced being thrown into the deep end of management firsthand. Whybrow works in college apprenticeship administration; about a year ago, he followed an old manager to a new workplace, where Whybrow himself ended up in management. His direct reports were all older than him, and Whybrow — who moved away from his old job and home — quickly found that he wasn't ready to step into management without that support network. "I went, 'No, I can't do this. I'm not old enough yet. I haven't got enough life experience,' and basically begged my old line manager for my job back," he said. He returned to a non-managerial role. Whybrow said he still finds Gen Z managers to be rare; he doesn't feel like there's much of a support network for those younger managers. But in a few years, and with more support, he'd like to try managing again. "I've made it quite clear that I would like to become a manager, and have that additional responsibility of not just being low-level support — and actually being able to go and delegate and learn how to be a proper manager," Whybrow said.
