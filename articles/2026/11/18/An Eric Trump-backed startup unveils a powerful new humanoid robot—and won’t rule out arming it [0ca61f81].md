# An Eric Trump-backed startup unveils a powerful new humanoid robot—and won’t rule out arming it

Fast Company · Chris Stokel-Walker · 2026-11-18

Foundation Future Industries, a startup backed by Eric Trump, has unveiled a humanoid robot that can carry 200 pounds while walking through rain, snow, and dust. The company wants its new Phantom MKII to work on construction sites and in factories—but the CEO isn’t ruling out putting weapons in its hands.
Sankaet Pathak, founder and CEO of Foundation, tells Fast Company his company would consider arming its machines if asked to by the U.S. military, arguing that deploying robots on the ground could reduce reliance on drones and air strikes.
“We’re not anti-weaponization and warfare,” he says. “That’s not something we have a blanket policy of just saying we’re not going to do.”
Phantom MKII is a second-generation humanoid designed for heavy industry and defense, unveiled today alongside Cortex II, the AI system that controls it. Foundation, which counts Eric Trump among its investors and advisers, has been particularly open about military applications—at times drawing controversy.
Earlier this month, the Financial Times reported that Foundation’s cofounder and president, Mike LeBlanc, was in direct talks with the Israel Defense Forces to supply up to 1,000 of its first-generation Phantom MKI robots for operations in tunnels beneath Gaza. Pathak disputes that characterization. “We’re not in talks with the IDF in any official capacity on deploying robots there,” he says. “What we have are very early exploration conversations.” When pressed about LeBlanc’s comments, Pathak says there is “literally nothing actively going on,” and there are no conversations progressing toward a contract.
Previously, Foundation said its machines had been tested with Ukrainian forces, while LeBlanc has described potential uses for them in detecting booby traps and trip wires.
For now, Pathak says most of Foundation’s defense work focuses on logistics rather than combat—though he doesn’t rule out deploying his machines on the battlefield. “Any time DOW [Department of War] will come to us and say, ‘Well, we need these robots more on the active battlefield with a weapon,’ to me that indicates that means less drones, less air strikes, more precision,” he says.
