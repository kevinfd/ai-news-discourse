# Data Residency Was The Easy Part

Forbes · Nick Burling, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-18

Nick Burling, Chief Product Officer at Nasuni.
A company can keep every sensitive file within the required geographic boundaries and still lose control over where its information goes. An AI application processing that information through a service operating in another jurisdiction may be enough to introduce sovereignty risks.
Enterprise data sovereignty, or an organization’s control over how its data is used and which laws govern it, has traditionally been approached through data residency policies and regional infrastructure. But those safeguards can only go so far when applications process that information across national borders, potentially subjecting it to different legal requirements.
When an AI workload processes enterprise information, that data may pass through services operating beyond the jurisdiction where the original file resides. The source data can remain within its required geographic boundaries while information derived from it is processed elsewhere.
If an enterprise wants to scale AI without compromising sovereignty, it must be able to deploy workloads wherever they deliver the most value while maintaining demonstrable control over the information those workloads use.
Why AI Sovereignty Requires Workload Flexibility
Keeping every AI workload within the same jurisdiction as its source data may seem like the safest approach. For a hospital processing sensitive patient information, local execution may be necessary. A manufacturer analyzing internal production data, however, could have greater flexibility in using specialized models from external providers.
Applying identical restrictions across these use cases can unnecessarily limit AI adoption. Organizations should be able to choose where each workload runs based on its regulatory requirements while retaining the flexibility to use specialized AI capabilities where appropriate. That may mean keeping sensitive workloads within private infrastructure and allowing others to use external services under established safeguards.
Hybrid architectures provide that flexibility, provided governance extends across the environments involved. This becomes especially important as organizations move from AI assistants toward autonomous agents.
Autonomous AI agents make these decisions more complex. Unlike applications that respond to individual user requests, agents can independently access information and interact with services across different environments. Each interaction can introduce another jurisdiction into the workflow, making it harder for organizations to maintain consistent control over sensitive data.
The Importance Of Demonstrable Control
The practical priority is ensuring that sovereignty policies follow information throughout an AI workflow. That starts with understanding the information an application can access and the restrictions attached to it. A document containing regulated personal information may require different processing controls from an internal knowledge article. Those distinctions need to inform which services can access the material and where processing can occur.
Those policies must extend to the information AI generates, which can carry the same sensitivity as the original documents. Organizations need to control how that information is retained and who can access it, with visibility into the providers handling it. Sensitive portions of a workflow can then remain within approved infrastructure while other functions run elsewhere.
Finally, organizations need evidence that these controls are working. An audit trail that records who accessed a source file provides limited visibility into what happened afterward. AI governance increasingly requires tracing subsequent processing steps, including which service handled the information and, where relevant, where outputs were retained.
This level of accountability can also support adoption. When enterprises can demonstrate that an AI workload operates within established boundaries, they have a stronger basis for approving it for production use.
A company may have complete records of where its sensitive files are stored and still struggle to explain what happened to that information once AI accessed it. Without that visibility, sovereignty becomes difficult to enforce and even harder to prove.
You can’t prove sovereignty for what you can’t trace. Organizations that can provide that evidence will have greater freedom to adopt AI without compromising control.
