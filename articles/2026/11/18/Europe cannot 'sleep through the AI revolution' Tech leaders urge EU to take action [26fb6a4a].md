# Europe cannot 'sleep through the AI revolution': Tech leaders urge EU to take action

Euronews · Pascale Davies · 2026-11-18

Tech leaders, including Yann LeCun, Nobel laureate Philippe Aghion and the CEOs of ElevenLabs and Lovable, call on the EU to secure a dedicated AI allocation in its 2028-2034 budget.
Dozens of European tech founders and scientists are warning that the window to compete in artificial intelligence is closing and the European Union’s next long-term budget will be crucial to deciding the continent’s place in the future.
The open letter, signed by 59 leaders, including Nobel laureate and economist Philippe Aghion, one of AI’s so-called godfathers Yann LeCun, and the heads of DeepL, OVHcloud, ElevenLabs and Lovable, argues Europe risks losing its technological sovereignty and competitiveness.
“We share a deep belief in Europe’s potential, but also a growing sense of alarm: the world is undergoing a technological revolution, and Europe is competing in it with a fraction of the capital, the compute, and the scaling capacity of its rivals. The gap is widening every quarter,” the letter reads.
China and the United States are battling to take the lead on the technology that promises to transform healthcare, manufacturing, and global economies. Countries that lead in AI will not only define global power but also grow their economies.
Titled ‘An Urgent Call: Europe Cannot Sleep Through the AI Revolution,’ the letter released on Wednesday says the talent and ambition already exist in Europe, but what is missing is the political will and capital to match them.
They warn negotiations on the EU budget for 2028-2034 are under way, and what is not secured during the talks will not exist before 2035.
The five AI demands
The letter sets out five demands. The first is simplification: the authors say Europe is right to protect safety and fundamental rights, but that this should not bury innovators in bureaucracy.
They welcome the first Digital Omnibus, a legislative package introduced to simplify, streamline, and reduce administrative burdens across the EU’s digital rulebook. However, they say it must not be the last step, and they want the ongoing review of the digital rulebook to produce binding simplification.
They also call on the EU to create a true 28th regime, so that a company can incorporate once and operate under one set of rules in every member state.
Scale and data
The second demand is to help European tech scale. Europe, the letter argues, is good at producing ideas but poor at capturing their benefits, because too many founders start companies in the United States or relocate there during later funding rounds.
They call for the channelling of Europe's large pool of private savings through pension funds and insurers into scale-up funds, completing the single market for capital, and having governments act as anchor customers for European technology.
The letter also calls for broad access to data, which they say matters as much as compute or capital.
Investment
The third ask is a reality check on investment. While there are investments in the EU’s AI "Gigafactories", they say AI needs more investment, as American companies alone are putting hundreds of billions of dollars into AI infrastructure this year.
To fund this, they call on European leaders to secure a dedicated and ambitious AI allocation within the European Competitiveness Fund and the next research framework programme.
Talent
The fourth ask is to retain Europe’s talent and calls for institutions for talent.
'Ambitious strategy'
The final demand is a change of mindset in Europe, saying that the continent can no longer be content with regulating technologies invented elsewhere; it must move from defence to offence and deliberately back European champions with the scale, capital and market access to compete globally.
“Europe can no longer be satisfied with simply regulating the technologies invented by others. We need a far more ambitious strategy for technological competition with the world.
"We must shift our mindset from playing defence to playing offence, and eventually to win,” the letter reads.
Euronews has reached out to the European Commission for comment.
