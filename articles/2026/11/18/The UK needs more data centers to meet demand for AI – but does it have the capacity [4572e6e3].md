# The UK needs more data centers to meet demand for AI – but does it have the capacity?

Northeastern Global News · Jonathan Chadwick · 2026-11-18

The UK needs more data centers to meet demand for AI – but does it have the capacity?
The British government is calling for more data centers to be built, but in terms of space and energy capacity, the country is more limited than AI rivals like the U.S. and China.
LONDON – The green belt, a vast area of countryside set up decades ago to control urban sprawl, wraps around London like a protective ring.
It offers millions of people access to green space and acts as a haven for native English wildlife, including deer, foxes, sparrows and kestrels.
The area, more than 1.2 million acres now faces the possible addition of data centers, the often massive facilities associated with the computing needs of artificial intelligence.
Data centers packed with computer servers that store, send and receive data have generated concerns about the environment and costs in communities worldwide, including in the United States where an explosion of data centers is fueling a political backlash even as the economy depends on them.
“The green belt serves a number of purposes – to check urban sprawl, keep towns separate, safeguard countryside, preserve historic character and assist recycling of derelict land,” Ian Pirie, coordinator at environmental network Friends of the Earth’s Havering branch, told Northeastern Global News. “I do not see data centers fulfilling any of these functions.”
In his recent Draft London Plan, London Mayor Sadiq Khan outlined ways to allow the construction of data centers on the green belt for the first time. According to Khan’s plan, parts of the green belt area that local planning authorities consider degraded or of lower quality and “with little aesthetic or environmental value” have been re-classified “grey belt”.
Data centers could be built on the grey belt as soon as 2028 as long as they meet energy efficiency and sustainability criteria, according to the plan.
One data center proposed for about 494 acres in East Havering would be built on green belt land currently used to grow crops including wheat, barley, rapeseed, beans and peas.
Protest group No to East Havering Data Center has questioned whether a new data center should involve the destruction of “214 football pitches” worth of farmland.
In Britain, data centers are “as fundamental to our economy as the railways and electricity networks”, the government’s Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade said in a recent Draft National Policy Statement. The government wants to see more built to “strengthen British supply chains and help new industries grow,” according to the draft policy statement. A policy paper from the country’s treasury department last month called for “faster and more certain delivery of the data centers the country needs.”
“As AI adoption grows, demand for that infrastructure will grow with it,” the policy paper said.
There has been growing public opposition to data centers, which place huge demands on electricity and water resources and could raise the cost of these commodities for local residents, said environmental charity Global Action Plan.
During November, people across Europe are taking part in coordinated protests, including a group in Amsterdam who broke into a data center construction site. In Scotland last weekend, thousands marched towards Edinburgh’s Scottish Parliament building, many holding placards objecting to a data center proposal in the nearby town of Larbert.
The U.S. has seen similar public opposition, which in some states has triggered a pause of construction of data centers. In July, New York Governor Kathy Hochul, put a one-year moratorium on all new construction of data centers in the state , while as many as 15 states, including Maine, are also considering such a move, according to the National Conference of State Legislatures.
Based on the growing demand for AI and digital services, the government anticipates Britain will need a nationwide network of data centers that would consume up to 6 billion watts of electricity when running at full power. Britain’s data center electrical capacity stands at around 2.4 billion watts.
Waleed Iqbal, assistant professor in data science at Northeastern University London, said the main problem with the government’s target is not space, but electrical capacity. Britain hosts more than 520 data centers, making it the largest market in western Europe, according to the government.
According to the House of Commons and advisory firm Oxford Economics, the U.K.’s data centers are located mostly near the country’s big towns and cities and range from about 2.5 acres to nearly 50 acres. Some proposed data centers, including Elsham Tech Park in North Lincolnshire, could span hundreds of acres.
To get new data centers up and running, the British government wants to connect them to the National Grid – the country’s vast electricity transmission network – but some face nearly a decade’s wait for this to happen.
“Space is the smaller problem,” Iqbal said. “Electricity is the binding constraint.”
In London, the market for data center space is competitive. To try and meet demand for AI computing power, tech companies and property developers are squeezing new data centers into available spaces in and around the capital.
Plans were approved during the summer by Secretary of State for Housing Angela Rayner to put a new data center in the Truman brewery, a historic and cherished site dating to the 17th century on London’s Brick Lane. Stellanor London North is a data center in the capital occupying a former post-war gin distillery. East India Docks data center uses parts of former docklands from the historic East India Company.
Slough, about 20 miles west of central London, is home to Europe’s highest concentration of data centers, according to SERGO, owner of Slough Trading Estate. Residents say excessive noise and heat from the facilities make daily life too loud and hot.
“The noise is like a busy motorway, sometimes louder,” Nicola Jones, a Slough resident, told NGN.
A large data center site can require between 1,000 and 10,000 construction workers when it’s being built, but once the facility is operational, the long-term workforce may be closer to 50 to 400 permanent employees, according to analytics firm Lightcast.
“Communities should get a real share of the benefits, and a genuine say early in planning,” Iqbal said. “If a data center proposal is done badly, it means a village carries the costs of national AI ambitions while getting little in return.”
The government’s data center target is “achievable on paper,” but delivery will be “very tight,” Iqbal said. Much depends on the public’s appetite for AI and digital services, which shows no signs of slowing, he said.
“Developers want [to be] near fiber optics, near existing grid connections and close to London,” Iqbal said.
