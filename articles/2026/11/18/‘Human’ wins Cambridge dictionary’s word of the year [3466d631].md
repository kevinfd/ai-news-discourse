# ‘Human’ wins Cambridge dictionary’s word of the year

The Guardian · Dan Milmo Global technology editor · 2026-11-18

In a world where the line between people and machines is more blurred than ever, Cambridge dictionary has sided with natural intelligence for its word of the year: human.
Breakthroughs in artificial intelligence – led by the arrival of ChatGPT three years ago – have made the question of what it means to be human as vexed as ever, but the dictionary has drawn a firm line with its choice of the word of 2026, defining “human” as “having the qualities, faults, and feelings that people have, as opposed to gods, animals, or machines”.
AI advances have spurred questions about whether computers can achieve consciousness, with the evolutionary biologist Richard Dawkins among those who answered in the affirmative, saying he was “left with the overwhelming feeling that they are human”. Sceptics, on the other hand, argue that there is a vast difference between how a human brain evolves and how an AI system is built.
Cambridge dictionary said the meaning and use of “human” had become more important in the age of the chatbot. Colin McIntosh, an editor of the dictionary, said AI was “changing the way we talk about being human” and had led to the word of the year being deployed to distinguish our work from that of advanced computer systems.
 
“Fears about what humans may lose when we outsource our thinking has made us reconsider the extent to which we rely on AI: from top mathematicians to teachers of young children,” he said. “A recent increase in compounds such as ‘human-made’ highlight how the word has come to distinguish works or qualities of people, as distinct from AI.”
Prof Rachel Adams, an expert on AI governance and human rights at the University of Cambridge, said: “AI brings us back to questions that we’ve asked for millennia: who are we, what does it mean to be human? The answer to these questions has never been fixed or stable, but the act of asking it is something we have in common.”
Other tech-related words were on the shortlist: “agent”, the term for an AI system that can operate – sometimes alarmingly – without human control, and “analogues”, relating to non-digital activities. A non-tech contender was “wholesome”, a word to describe something which is likely to improve your life either physically, morally, or emotionally.
Earlier this month, “mogging” – meaning to outperform or outshine someone or something – was named the Collins word of the year.
Tech-adjacent words also stood out among the new additions to the Cambridge dictionary. “Looksmaxxing”, describing the act of making oneself as physically attractive as possible, which has been associated with the online manosphere, and “aura farming”, another term often encountered on social media, which refers to “the activity of doing things with the intention of showing a personality that people admire and think is attractive and interesting”.
The former UK prime minister Keir Starmer made the news late last year when some social media users wondered “why he was aura farming” with his TikToks; Andy Burnham also appears to be attempting to master the art after becoming prime minister.
