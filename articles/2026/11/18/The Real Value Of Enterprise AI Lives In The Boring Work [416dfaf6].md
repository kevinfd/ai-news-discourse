# The Real Value Of Enterprise AI Lives In The Boring Work

Forbes · Rajat Bhargava, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-18

Rajat Bhargava is an entrepreneur, investor, author and currently CEO and cofounder of JumpCloud.
The technology industry is racing toward bigger AI breakthroughs, from autonomous agents to increasingly sophisticated models. Inside most organizations, however, the most valuable opportunities are less exciting. They sit in repetitive tasks such as preparing customer handoffs, reviewing sales opportunities, compiling reports and managing routine IT requests. These activities rarely make headlines, but they consume time employees could spend solving problems or building customer relationships. For business leaders, the question isn’t how much AI an organization can deploy but how much better it operates because of AI.
Getting there requires more than adding another tool. Organizations need to understand where employees lose time, improve the processes behind that work and define what success looks like. They also need to recognize that AI agents acting on employees’ behalf create new responsibilities around access and oversight. Moving quickly matters, but deploying AI everywhere is not a strategy. The organizations that gain the most will start with everyday problems, test their assumptions and expand what works.
Find The Friction Before Choosing The Technology
A common mistake is starting with technology rather than a business problem. Leaders may pursue ambitious AI projects while overlooking smaller workflows that could deliver value sooner. Consider a sales representative preparing for a customer meeting by gathering account details, reviewing past conversations and summarizing open issues across several systems. AI could handle much of that preparation, leaving more time to understand customer needs. The goal isn’t necessarily to replace a process; it’s to remove the administrative work slowing it down.
A useful starting point is to ask employees which repetitive tasks consume the most time without requiring much judgment. Rather than launch a company-wide initiative, leaders can evaluate promising workflows against three practical criteria:
• Find Recurring Bottlenecks: Prioritize tasks that happen frequently and slow down employees or customers.
• Set A Baseline: Record how long each task takes, how often errors occur and what delays cost.
• Test Before Expanding: Pilot one defined workflow, then compare speed, accuracy and employee feedback with the original process.
Even a promising workflow may not be ready for automation. When teams follow different procedures or store information in disconnected systems, AI can reproduce confusion at greater speed. Automating customer handoffs, for example, won’t help if sales and customer success disagree on which details must be shared. Before deployment, organizations should clarify responsibilities, standardize the process and agree on a successful outcome. Fixing these gaps first prevents teams from spending money automating inefficiency.
Measure Business Outcomes, Not AI Activity
AI makes it easier to produce content, process information and complete routine tasks, but more output doesn’t automatically mean better performance. Thousands of AI-generated sales summaries won’t matter if they don’t improve customer conversations. Weekly reports add little value if nobody uses them to make decisions. Counting prompts, agents or completed tasks therefore offers an incomplete picture of progress. What matters is whether AI improves the quality, speed or cost of a meaningful business outcome.
Each initiative needs a baseline and a result that can be checked after deployment. Customer support leaders might measure resolution time alongside customer satisfaction, while IT teams could track how quickly routine requests are handled without weakening security. Sales teams could examine whether automated preparation creates more time for conversations that move opportunities forward. Measurements should include the effort spent checking AI output and correcting mistakes. If automation saves 30 minutes but requires 20 minutes of rework, the real gain is only 10 minutes.
It is essential to distinguish time saved from value created. Giving employees five hours back each week sounds promising, but the benefit depends on what happens next. That time might support faster customer service, more thoughtful decisions or stronger relationships, none of which is captured by an automation dashboard alone. Small pilots help teams test those connections before committing to larger investments. Scaling then becomes a decision grounded in results, not excitement about the technology.
Make Governance Part Of The Workflow, Not An Afterthought
An assistant summarizing a document presents different risks from an agent updating customer records or acting inside business systems. As AI moves from answering questions to taking action, organizations need clearer rules about who and what can access sensitive information. A recent report our company conducted found that 72% of surveyed organizations reported a moderate-to-large gap between what AI was permitted to access and what they could prove it accessed. That gap shows why productivity and oversight cannot be treated as separate projects. Agents need clear ownership, limited permissions and records of their activity from the start.
Before deploying an agent, IT and security teams should define its task, permitted systems, approval requirements and accountable owner. An agent preparing a sales report might need to read CRM data but not modify customer records or open unrelated financial files. Access should be restricted to what’s necessary and removed when the task or agent is no longer active. Activity logs should help teams investigate mistakes and identify unusual behavior. These safeguards make it easier to expand automation without losing visibility or control.
Governance cannot end when a pilot goes live. Business needs change, employees encounter exceptions and automated workflows can drift away from their original purpose. Teams should regularly review accuracy, outcomes, permissions and whether an agent is still needed. Frontline employees should remain involved because they often spot problems before a dashboard does. The real advantage in enterprise AI will come from improving ordinary work, measuring what changes and keeping control as automation grows.
