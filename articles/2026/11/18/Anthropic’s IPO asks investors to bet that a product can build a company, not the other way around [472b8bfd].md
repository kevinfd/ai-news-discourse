# Anthropic’s IPO asks investors to bet that a product can build a company, not the other way around

Fast Company · Chris Stokel-Walker · 2026-11-18

When Anthropic launches its initial public offering this Thursday, it will be another test of how much further the AI industry’s seemingly unchecked growth can go. The company is seeking a valuation of $1.4 trillion, asking investors to buy into the idea that artificial intelligence will become a major contributor to the economy.
But the IPO also asks investors to make a more unusual bet: on a company whose own product could eventually do much of the work of building the business itself.
In its amended S-1 filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission, released last week, Anthropic says that “each new version of a Claude model directly accelerates development of the next.” Claude already helps run experiments, generate training data, and test future models, the company says. As evidence, Anthropic notes that Claude wrote more than 90% of contributions to its code base in September 2026. In the three-month period through September, the daily volume of code contributed per engineer was more than 45 times its 2024 average.
Traditionally, companies go public asking investors to bankroll employees who will build and sell better products. Anthropic is pitching something different: a business whose products increasingly help develop their own successors—and, in the process, help build the company itself.
There is some precedent for a company using its own technology to improve it. When the software company GitLab went public in 2021, its prospectus described how employees used GitLab’s development platform to identify problems and determine what to build next, a practice known as “dogfooding.” But that was software helping human developers do their jobs, rather than AI increasingly performing the work itself.
“It may be the first IPO in which this type of thing is happening,” says Sinan Aral, a professor at MIT Sloan School of Management.
Still, Aral cautions against confusing AI’s growing role in developing products with the ability to decide which products should exist in the first place. Claude may be writing code, but humans remain responsible for the more consequential research decisions. “What to do, what goals to have and what to optimize, is generally being decided by human beings,” he says.
