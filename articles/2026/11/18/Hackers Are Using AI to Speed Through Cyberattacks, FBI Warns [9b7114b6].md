# Hackers Are Using AI to Speed Through Cyberattacks, FBI Warns

CNET · Nelson Aguilar · 2026-11-18

An open-source AI tool designed to find security flaws is helping hackers launch cyberattacks. The FBI says hackers are using Artex, an AI-powered security testing tool, to break into organizations worldwide. In an advisory on Friday, the agency said some attacks took less than 3 hours from selecting a target to gaining high-level access to its systems. Attackers have used Artex since at least August against banks, government agencies, technology companies and healthcare providers.
The advisory was issued the same day communications problems prompted a nationwide Federal Aviation Administration ground stop and a day after a major Microsoft 365 outage began. Microsoft attributed its disruption to technical problems, and the FAA says it has found no indication of a cyberattack.
An FBI official told ABC News that Artex doesn’t appear to be connected to either incident or to other recent disruptions, including banking outages involving JPMorgan Chase.
More from CNET
The warning adds to concerns about the security risks posed by AI agents, which can carry out tasks with little human supervision.
Artex automates security tests that organizations use to uncover weaknesses. According to Microsoft’s analysis, its agents can examine defenses, exploit flaws and extract data. Attackers can use that automation to try several ways into a network simultaneously.
The FBI cited attacks against South Korean financial institutions that exposed the personal information of approximately 67,000 people. Microsoft warns that attackers breached over 2,250 organizations’ Microsoft 365 systems in a separate campaign, using stolen ConnectWise backup credentials alongside the Artex tool.
Because customers had already authorized the backup software, attackers could use its access without logging in as an individual user. Microsoft cautioned that a successful sign-in doesn’t necessarily mean data was accessed or stolen. The automated attacks could trigger security alerts, but their speed left defenders little time to respond.
Amazon Web Services’ Nov. 13 research described another scheme: Stealing credentials for AI services, and then using that access to hunt for more credentials at someone else’s expense. Amazon Threat Intelligence uncovered the activity through decoy systems posing as AI services. It linked the campaigns to Artex with moderate confidence but couldn’t independently verify results the agents reported on systems outside its monitoring.
Amazon said the attacks relied on default passwords, exposed login details and outdated software, all of which hackers can find and exploit faster with AI.
Artex’s developer removed the original GitHub repository in October after reports of abuse, but copies remain available, the FBI said. The agency recommends limiting access to sensitive systems, reviewing software permissions, replacing exposed credentials, and monitoring unusual account activity or large data transfers.
