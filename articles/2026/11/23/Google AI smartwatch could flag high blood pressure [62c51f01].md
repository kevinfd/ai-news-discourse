# Google AI smartwatch could flag high blood pressure

Fox News · Kurt Knutsson, CyberGuy Report · 2026-11-23

When was the last time you checked your blood pressure? If you're like a lot of people, it probably happened during your last doctor's appointment. And if you feel perfectly healthy, you may not give it another thought. That's what makes high blood pressure so concerning. You can have it for years without noticing anything wrong.
Now, Google researchers are exploring a way to uncover that hidden danger using something many of us already wear on our wrists. In a new study recently published in Nature, researchers demonstrated an artificial intelligence system that can identify signs of hypertension using standard smartwatch sensors. It works in the background without requiring calibration with a traditional blood pressure cuff.
The technology uses sensors that already exist in consumer smartwatches. That could make early detection much more accessible. However, the research also reveals a limitation that anyone relying on a smartwatch for health information should understand.

How Google AI detects high blood pressure using a smartwatch
Your smartwatch already knows quite a bit about your body. It can track your heart rate throughout the day. Depending on the model, it may also detect irregular heart rhythms.
Google's new AI takes the information collected by those sensors and looks for signs of something harder to detect: ongoing high blood pressure. According to Google Research, the system analyzes two types of sensor data.
Your smartwatch can pick up signals you cannot feel
The first sensor uses light to detect changes in blood volume beneath your skin. It's the same basic technology many watches use to monitor your pulse. The second sensor measures movement. Researchers found that it can also capture incredibly small vibrations caused by your heart pumping blood. You wouldn't notice those vibrations. However, the AI can analyze them alongside your pulse patterns to look for signs associated with hypertension.
The system collects information over multiple days. It then combines those patterns with your height to estimate the likelihood of sustained high blood pressure. Unlike a traditional monitor, the smartwatch doesn't produce a reading such as 120 over 80. Instead, it looks for signs that your blood pressure may be consistently elevated. What's interesting is that you wouldn't have to do anything differently. You could go about your day while your smartwatch quietly looks for potential warning signs.
Google's smartwatch AI study reveals promising results
Google worked with researchers from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and Harvard Medical School to develop and test the system. First, researchers trained the underlying AI using nearly 500,000 hours of smartwatch sensor data collected from 2,109 consenting participants. They then evaluated the hypertension detection system in a clinical study involving 196 participants across five U.S. locations. None of those participants had known cardiovascular disease or were taking blood pressure medication.
During the study, participants wore a consumer smartwatch for seven days. Researchers compared the AI's findings with results from 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring. That medical test uses a cuff to measure blood pressure repeatedly throughout the day and night. Doctors use it to help confirm hypertension.
How accurate was Google's smartwatch AI?
During initial screening, the results showed:
- 65.8% sensitivity: The AI correctly identified nearly two-thirds of participants who had hypertension.
- 90% specificity: The system correctly classified 90% of participants who did not have hypertension.
- 80.6% positive predictive value: Among participants the AI flagged as having hypertension, about four out of five actually had the condition.
For comparison, initial blood pressure screenings performed in medical offices identified 55.3% of participants with hypertension in the study. That's an encouraging result. However, the difference between the smartwatch and office screening was not statistically significant, according to a Nature Portfolio summary of the findings. There's another number worth paying attention to.
The AI missed roughly one in three participants who had hypertension during initial screening.
That's a number worth paying attention to. After all, we're talking about a condition that can lead to a heart attack or stroke, often without any warning signs.
Researchers also looked at a smaller group of 48 people who had elevated blood pressure readings during their initial doctor's visit. In that group, the AI correctly identified 78.4% of those with ongoing hypertension. It also correctly ruled out the condition in 90.9% of those who didn't have it. Those results are encouraging, but there's still work to do. Google needs to test the technology on more people before we can know how reliably it can detect high blood pressure.
MICROSOFT AI BEATS DOCTORS IN SIMULATED DIAGNOSIS TEST
Can your Pixel Watch detect high blood pressure today?
Here's where things get interesting for people who already own a Google smartwatch. Google introduced its Health Guardian features in September 2026. Among them is a blood pressure trends feature available through the Google Health app.
According to Google's September announcement, blood pressure trends are available at no additional subscription cost on eligible Pixel Watch 3, Pixel Watch 4 and Pixel Watch 5 devices. Availability depends on your region.
The feature analyzes pulse and movement patterns over time. Then, it provides a monthly assessment of whether your blood pressure trend appears high. Google says these existing Health Guardian capabilities are informed by clinical research, including work connected to its newly published findings. However, there's an important difference between the experimental system described in Nature and the feature available on your watch today.
Google's current blood pressure feature has limitations
The existing Pixel Watch feature is intended for general wellness. Google specifically says it cannot diagnose hypertension or serve as a medical screening tool. It also cannot tell you your actual systolic or diastolic blood pressure numbers. According to Google's support documentation, the feature provides a monthly trend assessment such as "High" or "Not high."
And a "Not high" result doesn't guarantee your blood pressure is normal. Google also warns users against relying on these estimates to change medications or treatment plans. Meanwhile, the newly published research remains exploratory. Google has not announced that the experimental hypertension screening system will become a commercial diagnostic feature.
Apple Watch already offers hypertension alerts
Google has some competition in this area. Apple has already introduced FDA-cleared hypertension notifications for compatible Apple Watch models. As we previously reported in Apple Watch gets FDA-approved hypertension alerts, the feature looks for patterns associated with chronic high blood pressure. It uses information gathered by the watch's optical heart sensor over 30-day periods.
The feature supports Apple Watch Series 9 and later models, along with Apple Watch Ultra 2 and later models, subject to software and regional availability. If the watch detects concerning patterns, it can notify you. However, Apple also makes clear that the feature cannot diagnose hypertension. And it may miss cases. Apple recommends checking your blood pressure with a traditional cuff and discussing the results with a healthcare professional if you receive an alert.
So, while Apple and Google are approaching this technology differently, the current guidance remains clear. A smartwatch alert can give you useful information, but you still need an accurate blood pressure measurement.
Why detecting high blood pressure earlier could save lives
High blood pressure has earned the nickname "silent killer" for a reason. According to the World Health Organization, more than one billion adults worldwide live with hypertension. Many have no idea they have it.
Meanwhile, untreated high blood pressure can damage blood vessels and increase the risk of a heart attack or stroke. Younger adults can be particularly vulnerable to missed diagnoses because they may feel healthy and see little reason to get checked. Now, consider what could happen if a smartwatch noticed concerning patterns before someone developed symptoms.
That person might decide to schedule an appointment and have their blood pressure checked. If doctors confirm hypertension, earlier treatment could help reduce the risk of complications. Of course, researchers still need to determine whether smartwatch-based screening actually improves diagnosis rates and long-term health outcomes.

What should you do if your smartwatch flags high blood pressure?
If your smartwatch warns you about possible high blood pressure, don't ignore it. But don't assume the watch has diagnosed you, either. The next step is to check your blood pressure using a validated upper-arm cuff.
The American Heart Association recommends automatic upper-arm monitors for home measurements because they provide more reliable readings than typical wrist or finger monitors. If you're using Google's blood pressure trends feature and receive a "High" result, Google recommends checking your blood pressure twice a day for seven days and sharing those readings with your doctor.
Make sure you follow your monitor's instructions and use the correct cuff size. Also, keep an eye on how you're feeling. If you experience emergency warning signs such as chest pain or difficulty breathing, seek immediate medical attention rather than waiting for another smartwatch assessment.
And remember, even if your watch never sends an alert, you should continue getting your blood pressure checked. For another way to track your readings, see our article on how to monitor blood pressure with your Apple Watch using a compatible blood pressure monitor.
The biggest challenge Google's smartwatch AI still faces
One of my biggest questions about this technology comes down to how much confidence people will place in the results. Let's say your smartwatch tells you everything looks normal. Would you still bother checking your blood pressure? That's where the research raises an important concern.
In Google's initial screening study, the AI missed a meaningful number of people who actually had hypertension. And while the results compared favorably with initial office screening, the study was relatively small. Researchers will need more evidence before the technology can become a dependable part of routine medical screening.
Google's findings suggest that everyday wearables could eventually help identify people who might otherwise go undiagnosed. For now, though, a reliable blood pressure cuff and a conversation with your doctor remain essential.
