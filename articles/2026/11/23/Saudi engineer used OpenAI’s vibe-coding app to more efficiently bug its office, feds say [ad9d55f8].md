# Saudi engineer used OpenAI’s vibe-coding app to more efficiently bug its office, feds say

Business Insider · Jacob Shamsian,Stephen Council, Jacob Shamsian,Stephen Council (jshamsian@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-23

Abdulrahman Alfozan and his lawyer David Rizk speak to Judge Peter Kang in a courtroom sketch from Friday, November 3, 2026. Vicki Ellen Behringer Abdulrahman Alfozan used Codex to handle his recordings of OpenAI's office, prosecutors say. The Saudi engineer worked at OpenAI before his arrest and firing in mid-October. He's pleaded not guilty and offered to pay a $1 million bond. When a Saudi OpenAI engineer wanted to vibe-code a better method for secretly recording meetings at the AI company, he turned to the familiar tool Codex, according to prosecutors. Abdulrahman Alfozan used OpenAI's own popular coding product to modify a recording device that was the same kind OpenAI found tucked behind a TV in a meeting room, prosecutors said in a filing Monday, citing OpenAI records. They say he prompted OpenAI's AI coding tool, Codex, to add a voice-detection skill and used it to organize his surreptitious recordings in Dropbox. A Monday motion by US attorneys disclosed these details, part of a back-and-forth over whether to keep Alfozan detained while he awaits trial. The Justice Department arrested Alfozan on October 9 and charged him with using a device to intercept oral communications. He made one bid for release, about a week later. A magistrate judge deemed him a flight risk and ordered that he stay detained. He has pleaded not guilty. Alfozan's lawyer puts pressure on the feds' investigation On Friday, Alfozan's defense lawyer, David Rizk , asked a federal judge overseeing the case to allow him to be released on a $1 million bond. He said that prosecutors haven't yet presented evidence to support more severe charges, such as espionage or theft of trade secrets. Four of his friends, all US citizens, agreed to put up money for bond, he wrote. Rizk also wrote that Alfozan's wife flew to Saudi Arabia with their child — evidence that a judge cited to rule he should be detained — because her student visa was set to expire. "Mr. Alfozan's wife and child remain in Saudi Arabia because she was so deeply traumatized by the violent FBI raid that woke her last month and then the firestorm of publicity following the government's false insinuations that her husband was a spy," he wrote. Prosecutors hope to keep Alfozan detained In the Monday filing, prosecutors said the investigation is ongoing. There was already evidence, they wrote, that Alfozan would try to flee the country if he were released from jail. Prosecutors said Alfozan exchanged WhatsApp messages with a member of the Saudi royal family indicating he might apply for a job at Echelon, a Riyadh-based AI company, "after the IPO" — an apparent reference to OpenAI's expected public offering next year. They also shed light on the material they allege Alfozan gathered from OpenAI's offices. According to Monday's filing, law enforcement officials found photos on his cellphone of a document marked "CONFIDENTIAL" detailing a "strategic partnership" between OpenAI and another company. They also found audio files on his personal computer of "dozens of recordings of conversations" that took place at OpenAI throughout September of this year, including meetings that "involved discussions of what appears likely to be confidential trade secret material," the filing says. Alfozan used his wife's account to prompt the coding tool Codex to help with the recordings, the filing says. At a hearing last week, US Assistant Attorney Nick Parker said that the prosecutors' ongoing investigation includes the recording device, five devices taken from the couple, and both records and surveillance footage from OpenAI. Alfozan and Rizk will square off against the prosecution over the new motion for release in a hearing on Tuesday morning before Magistrate Judge Alex Tse, who rejected the original bid for release.
