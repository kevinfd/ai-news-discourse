# Scoping Review Finds Generative AI Nursing Evidence Lacks Proven Clinical Benefit

BIOENGINEER · 2026-11-23

A scoping review evaluating generative artificial intelligence in clinical nursing indicates that while models handle diverse information and decision-related tasks, existing research fails to demonstrate overall clinical care quality, safety benefits, or workload reduction. Published in BMC Nursing, the investigation synthesized empirical findings across international literature to assess how generative tools perform across healthcare environments. Although interest in deploying these technologies has expanded, current evidence regarding their direct application to bedside and community nursing care remains fragmented across operational settings and evaluated outcome domains.
To map the existing literature systematically, the research team implemented the Arksey and O’Malley methodological framework and reported findings in alignment with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. The investigators searched nine primary databases from inception through February 2026, seeking original application and evaluation studies published in English or Chinese journals. To ensure comprehensive coverage, two independent reviewers screened all identified records and charted study details, filtering 8,981 retrieved citations down to 266 full-text reports and selecting 23 eligible studies for final descriptive synthesis.
The included investigations revealed four overlapping functional forms through which generative artificial intelligence systems were deployed within clinical nursing contexts. Conversational question answering and counseling represented the most frequent functional category, evaluated across nine separate studies. An equal number of studies examined content generation and optimization alongside case-based decision support and automated assessment, each accounting for eight studies. In contrast, retrieval-augmented knowledge support represented the least explored functional mechanism, appearing in only one included investigation.
Across the reviewed corpus, researchers deployed these systems across six distinct nursing-related care scenarios. Patient education and health communication formed the primary focus of implementation, analyzed by eleven studies. Clinical assessment and decision support served as the setting for eight studies, while mental health assessment and emotional support, symptom management and rehabilitation guidance, and care planning, documentation, and management were each examined in three studies. Finally, two studies evaluated generative systems applied directly to nursing education and training.
Study methodologies evaluated model performance predominantly through localized surrogate metrics rather than real-world patient trajectories. Investigators relied heavily on expert ratings, task-specific accuracy calculations, agreement measures, readability indices, psychological scales, and user-experience questionnaires. A substantial proportion of the evidence derived from isolated model-output assessments, simulated clinical cases, and retrospective or observational comparisons rather than active interventions embedded in routine nursing workflows.
Alongside task-specific performance metrics, the review identified recurring technical and operational concerns documented across the literature. Content errors and variable task-specific accuracy emerged consistently across evaluations, raising substantial questions about reliability during clinical decision-making. Furthermore, multiple study participants and authors voiced explicit concerns regarding patient privacy protections and the potential for healthcare professionals to develop an uncritical overreliance on automated model outputs during clinical care delivery.
The review concluded that successful technical execution in simulated settings or isolated tasks cannot substitute for rigorous demonstrations of patient safety or clinical care improvement. Because current data do not confirm reductions in overall nursing workload, the authors emphasized that prospective investigations are critically needed to monitor patient outcomes, identify clinically consequential errors, and establish the practical requirements of safe implementation before these models can be responsibly integrated into routine clinical nursing.
