# How Caltech students brokered a truce in math's fight over AI

Business Insider · Truman Dickerson, Truman Dickerson (tdickerson@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-23

Caltech's mathathon could be a blueprint for a math community seeking to heal deep schisms in its ranks. Truman Dickerson Mathematicians everywhere are divided over how, if at all, to use AI in their work. Caltech students, worried about their futures and seeking to embrace AI, were caught in the middle. Their AI "mathathon" now symbolizes a way forward for a fractured field. There was a lot riding on the meeting. Caltech's "mathathon," a multi-day event during which math enthusiasts seeking to embrace AI planned to prompt chatbots to crack the world's hardest unsolved problems , was in peril. Hundreds of mathematical luminaries and graduate students were calling for its cancellation because, they said, it would promote AI "slop." During a video call on a Friday in early September, however, the event's undergraduate organizers reached a compromise with their naysayers: They would remake the competition's parameters, and in return, get much-needed support from the wider math community. "It felt like a miracle," said Mayla Ward, a Ph.D. student at Caltech who was present at the meeting. Ward initially called for the event's cancellation but later became involved with its organization. Taken together, the myriad changes (one of which involved parting ways with Anthropic as a sponsor) herald a possible future for the field of mathematics in which the use of AI is omnipresent, but measured, human-centered, and controlled by mathematicians. The event itself became a blueprint for those seeking to bridge deep schisms in math circles worldwide, whose ranks are split over how and if AI should be used in the discipline. The mathathon The competition drew participants to Caltech from Ph.D. programs across the world. Everyone was slightly geeky and hyped up about using AI to solve problems. "There's an opportunity here to get some ideas of what the new era of math might look like," said Jack McCarthy, a student mathematician and research collaborator at Meta. Jack McKoen and Jack McCarthy, two participants at Mathathon, grin for the camera. Truman Dickerson In many ways, it proved an amicable gathering reminiscent of days gone by — before AI companies started ransacking the field's long list of unsolved problems . Devoutly anti-AI advocates mingled politely with a cast of younger students enthusiastic about AI, some of whom were already shifting their focus toward computer science. Math geeks roamed around the university's picturesque campus in the foothills of the San Gabriel Mountains, picking up docile turtles when they weren't engrossed in their computers. "I think this unification is amazingly great," said Caltech mathematics professor Sergei Gukov. Deep fissures Ever since OpenAI first crashed into the math scene by solving the renowned Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize question in September, mathematicians at every level — from Fields medalists to freshmen undergrads — have been ensconced in an existential debate about the future of the field. Much of the debate centers on whether mathematicians should use AI and, if so, how they should use it responsibly. Many have objected to OpenAI romping through lists of open problems, taking every opportunity to advertise its name at the expense of human researchers. Gukov, who grew up in Russia when it was still the Soviet Union, said the battle lines fell between close friends and research collaborators, some of whom stopped working together. "Everything was so cohesive and united, and suddenly it's not," he said. Sergei Gukov makes proprietary AI models designed specifically for mathematicians. Truman Dickerson On Friday, an employee at Anthropic said he used Claude to solve what is arguably the field's most famous open problem . Hours later, another mathematician said he had also solved the problem — without using AI . Meanwhile, leading figures in the math world are racing to develop their own AI models to regain some control over AI-generated solutions. Before they are fully developed, though, many young mathematicians are turning to closed and open-source models to stay relevant in a rapidly changing discipline. A new way forward At the Caltech mathathon, nearly every participant used a cocktail of frontier models that ran nonstop in the background while they took brief breaks to guzzle Red Bull. The brainchild of a small group of undergrad math students, the mathathon was initially very different. The event took place on Caltech's picturesque campus in Pasadena. Truman Dickerson The organizers sought to emulate the big AI companies, which have focused on tackling the most famous unsolved problems and publishing lengthy, brackish proofs — giving human mathematicians the thankless task of gleaning useful information from them. In the video call, however, organizers were persuaded to take so-called "open problems" off the table, in favor of tasking participants with finding alternative ways to solve old problems. The organizers also introduced additional incentives for teams that could clearly explain their results, thereby cutting through reams of AI slop. And organizers negotiated with Anthropic, which had previously sponsored the event with $1 million in tokens, to sever the AI giant's sponsorship. OpenAI, which was originally a sponsor, pulled out after people began calling for its cancellation. "One of the big things us as mathematicians care about was mathematicians being in control of the research process," said Caiman Moreno-Earle, a sophomore organizer. "This means taking ownership of the models themselves." Whether the mathathon will live up to the aspirations of the undergrads who designed it remains an open question, though.
