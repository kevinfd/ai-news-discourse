# AI Created New Kinds Of Capital. Most Companies Only Govern Oneserge Lucio Is The VP And GM Of Agile Operations Division, Broadcom Inc.

Forbes · Serge Lucio, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-23

Serge Lucio is the VP and GM of Agile Operations Division, Broadcom Inc.
Ask a technology leader today how they decide which AI model to run, and you’ll get an answer that sounds sophisticated but is beside the point. Run Claude Opus directly through Vertex AI Model Garden on GCP, using existing enterprise credits and security controls. Self-host an open-source model like Llama, Gemma or Qwen through Vertex AI Model Garden, Cloud Run or GKE for tighter control at scale. Or stay inside the Anthropic family and choose between Opus and Fable.
These are real decisions IT teams are making right now. And they’re being evaluated the same way: which option is cheapest per token. In fact, Google Cloud CEO Thomas Kurian recently announced that Gemini will offer an auto model selection feature to route prompts and tasks to the most efficient model based on complexity, performance needs and cost. This focus highlights tokenomics: the economic framework and cost-management principles governing how AI tokens are priced, allocated and consumed.
That’s the AI-era equivalent of hiring a new employee and evaluating the decision entirely on their hourly rate.
A New Kind Of Capital, Unmeasured
Here’s what the model-pricing decision obscures: It isn’t which token costs less. It’s what a unit of finished work costs, all in. And there are more ways to produce that unit of work than most organizations account for. A feature can be built by an AI agent working entirely on its own or with a person reviewing and steering the output. It can be built by a person using AI to accelerate their own work or by a person alone, without AI involved. These are four paths, with four different cost profiles, but most organizations can only see the price tag on one of them at a time.
That’s a problem because the right approach changes with the task, and you rarely know which one it is in advance. If a feature is mission-critical and speed matters more than cost, burning through a frontier model and a large token budget to ship it fast can be the correct call. But if the same team is looking at a minor bug fix, the calculus flips: It may not need AI at all, and a person working alone might genuinely be the cheaper, faster option. The trouble is, most organizations don’t have all four paths priced out side by side in a way they can compare before they commit to one.
The Accounting Is Catching Up Faster Than The Governance
The good news is that the finance side of this is more solvable than it looks. New accounting standards and attestation-based verification—the same rigor long applied to labor time—now make it possible to treat AI development spend with real balance-sheet discipline instead of writing it all off as an incurred expense.
The accounting turned out to be the easy part, and the reason is almost embarrassingly simple: We were already tracking work and development hours in ValueOps. The hard part was getting clean AI usage and billing data out of the vendors. Once we had it, the rest followed from a record we already kept. Each engineer’s AI usage was allocated against the work items they’d logged time to, split by default in proportion to that time. Then they confirmed or adjusted the split themselves because the person who did the work knows when the model carried most of the load.
That’s what our finance team could trace: usage tied to a work item, a work item tied to a person and a person who signed off. The portion tied to new development was a candidate for capitalization, while maintenance and experimentation stayed expensed.
The lesson I learned: Pulling the data out of the vendors takes some effort, but attaching it is easy if work and time are already tracked in one place. If they aren’t, start there. Trying to capitalize AI spend before you can show what work it supported means trying to prove a number with no evidence behind it.
No Single Function Owns This
I’d resist the instinct to hand this to one department. For my division, it sits across finance, HR and engineering, and each brings something the others can’t: Finance owns the rules, engineering owns the work records, and HR owns the time and role data that make the split credible. What I’d tell any leader starting out: Name one person accountable across all three, agree on work-type definitions once instead of team by team and review exceptions together monthly so disagreements surface before an audit does.
This isn’t a problem any single function can fix alone. And right now, most organizations have zero people whose job is to own all three at once.
Proving Worth Isn’t The Whole Problem
Knowing the cost per story, or treating token spend as capital instead of overhead, only matters if someone is truly accountable. That’s the part almost nobody has solved, and it’s usually framed backwards as a brake, not an accelerant.
The technology is rarely the bottleneck. Agreeing on who signs off is.
