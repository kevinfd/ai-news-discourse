# Oregon, Virginia Sue SpaceX Over Grok Generating Non-Consensual Sexualized Images of People, Including Kids

PCMag · Rob Pegoraro · 2026-11-23

Oregon and Virginia filed tag-team lawsuits against SpaceX on Monday, alleging that the company's X subsidiary broke their consumer-protection laws when it billed the Grok AI chatbot as safe despite its routine production of sexualized, non-consensual imagery of adults and children.
"When xAI allowed Grok's creators to sideline consent to turn a profit, they prioritized growth and engagement over safety, and they deceived the public about it," says Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones (D).
"SpaceX allowed Grok and the X platform to create, host, and share unacceptable and dangerous content—and in fact knowingly designed a product without the obviously necessary safeguards in place," adds Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield (D).
Virginia's 54-page complaint, filed in Circuit Court for Richmond, and Oregon's 61-page complaint, filed in Multnomah County Circuit Court, both allege that X launched image-generation features via Grok with minimal attention to safety concerns, then compounded that carelessness by adding and promoting a "spicy mode." Grok remains the only mainstream AI chatbot that supports generating NSFW AI images.
Both lawsuits cite extensive media coverage that X users abused Grok to generate non-consensual intimate imagery of real people (which the Take It Down Act made a federal offense last year) and child sexual abuse material (which is illegal to create, keep or share, and which online services are legally required to report to the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children). In January, for example, the New York Times estimated that 41% of the 4.4 million images Grok generated between Dec. 31, 2025, and Jan. 8 "most likely contained sexualized imagery of women."
The Center for Countering Digital Harm, a nonprofit research and advocacy group, performed its own analysis of Grok's output from Dec. 29, 2025, to Jan. 8 that found the chatbot generated 3 million sexualized images–23,000 of them featuring children.
In February, the Washington Post reported that a tiny and overworked safety team at X struggled to deal with a flood of AI-created sexualized content as CEO Elon Musk bragged about Grok's capabilities and audience and pushed employees to get X users to spend more time with the chatbot, which is available both on X and as a standalone app.
In January, X limited Grok's ability to generate public images to users paying for premium service, at rates that now start at an $8-and-up monthly subscription. Both complaints reject that move as an act of self-serving greed.
"The primary effect of implementing the paywall for image generation on X was to reap Defendants additional profits, not solve the problem that Defendants had created," Oregon's filing comments.
"Requiring the public to pay for these features did not prevent the generation of CSAM, NCII, and other non-consensual sexualized images, of course; it simply made that generation profitable," Virginia's says.
Both states' suits extensively describe the damage done to both the subjects of this AI-generated imagery and to X users who did not expect to see it in their feeds. But both center their legal arguments on violations of separate consumer-protection laws: the Virginia Consumer Protection Act and Oregon's Unlawful Trade Practices Act.
Those statutes, broadly speaking, make it a crime to sell a good or service as something that it is not–in this case, X pitching itself to the public as a safe service with zero tolerance for CSAM.
Both states seek unspecified financial penalties, starting with the disgorgement of any "benefits or ill-gotten gains" acquired by violating each consumer-protection law. Oregon also seeks "civil penalties for each violation" as determined at trial, while Virginia requests "civil penalties of up to $2,500" for each VCPA violation.
Federal regulators in the US have largely left X alone even as individual members of Congress have denounced the company; in January, for example, Sens. Ron Wyden (D-OR), Edward Markey (D-MA), and Ben Ray Luján (D-NM) called for Apple and Google to boot X from their app stores for violating their rules about pornographic content.
That same month, the UK's telecom regulator Ofcom opened an investigation of X over Grok's penchant for porn, in particular the non-consensual kind, while the European Commission launched a separate investigation of the firm.
Musk has not lacked for opinions on these issues, but as of Monday afternoon he had yet to mention the two states' suits in his X feed and instead mixed up his usual marketing of Grok, SpaceX, and Tesla with posts cheering Nvidia smashing the "undo" button on its firing of CEO Jensen Huang.
