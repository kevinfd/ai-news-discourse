# Full Speed Ahead: Enterprise AI Agents And The All-Green Illusion

Forbes · Florian Douetteau, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-23

Florian Douetteau is co-founder and CEO of Dataiku, an enterprise agent and analytics platform.
Enterprises are racing forward with thousands of autonomous AI agents. In fact, according to Gartner, the average Fortune 500 enterprise will have more than 150,000 agents in use by 2028, up from fewer than 15 in 2025. But here’s the truth about AI agents in the enterprise: Companies are being lulled into a false sense of security by dashboards that flash green while business logic subtly shatters beneath the surface.
Dataiku’s “Global AI Confessions Report: CIO Edition, 2026,” a survey conducted with The Harris Poll of 685 CIOs worldwide, found that 90% of CIOs report being confident in having complete visibility and tracking into every AI agent in their organization. Yet, 81% admit they lack complete oversight of agents built outside an approved system or formal submission to IT or business leaders.
Same CIOs. Same survey. Both answers given in good faith. CIOs aren’t lying about what they see; they’re looking in the wrong place.
Shadow AI Is The Wrong Answer To The Right Problem
For 30 years, new technology entered the company the same way. Someone bought it, approved it and switched it on, and the CIO knew its purpose, its cost and what it was supposed to return.
Now, AI shows up in two ways the CIO never sees: agents built inside tools the company already pays for, and AI tools employees bring in on their own, known to no one but them.
That’s shadow AI, and according to the aforementioned survey, 84% of CIOs say employees are building agents faster than IT can govern them. But those employees aren’t hiding. They’re doing exactly what their CEO asked: using AI to get more done.
You can’t discipline your way out of something leadership asked for, and CIOs don’t want to. Ninety-one percent of CIOs agree that the most effective AI strategy lets business teams build within a governed environment, yet only 25% are completely confident that those teams can build AI solutions using their organization’s current tools without creating business risk.
So, shadow AI keeps growing, with good intentions and no oversight. And “good intentions” never satisfied a regulator.
A Green Dashboard Is Not Good News
Seventy-nine percent of CIOs told us they’ve had an agent violate business intent, policy or expectations while operating within its technical parameters. No alert fired, and the dashboard was green the whole time. When something does go wrong, about six in 10 CIOs (59%) say they can’t identify and stop or contain a problematic agent the same day.
For traditional software, uptime is measured by whether it worked. For agents, it often measures how long a mistake gets to keep running.
Everyone’s Job, Nobody’s Responsibility
CEOs like me told our companies to build with AI and made AI fluency part of everyone’s job. We forgot the corollary: When AI is everyone’s job, it’s nobody’s responsibility.
When asked who’s primarily responsible when an AI agent makes a wrong or harmful decision, CIOs split five ways: multiple teams (23%); central IT (21%); the data or AI team (20%); security, risk and compliance (18%); or the workflow business owner (13%).
This absence of accountability carries a real price. Seventy-six percent believe their roles are at risk if their companies fail to deliver measurable AI gains by the end of 2027. When something goes wrong, the lack of a clear accountability model means nobody can step in, nobody makes the call and nobody can reconstruct what happened.
Fixing that takes three shifts.
1. Start From Discovery, Not Approval
The question isn’t, “What did we sign off on?” It’s “What’s out there that we didn’t?” Assume it’s already been built, because it has.
2. Give Every Agent An ID, An Owner And A Job Description
An agent without an ID is in the shadows. An agent without an owner is an agent that nobody will ever retire, and nearly half of CIOs (47%) have already decommissioned more than 20 agents this year. An agent without a clearly defined purpose can’t be judged.
3. Judge The Work, Not The Machine
Stop asking whether the agent is up. Ask whether it did the job it was built for, and whether anyone would sign their name to the decisions it made last week.
The CIO’s New Job Description
CIOs should no longer be judged by how much AI they’ve deployed. Deployment is a vanity metric.
Judge them by four answers they should be able to give any day of the week:
• Which agents are running, and who owns each one?
• What does each one cost, by team and use case?
• What does it return against the job it was built for?
• When one goes rogue, who notices, who makes the call and how fast does it stop?
None of those are technology questions. The CIO is no longer the person who buys the software. They’re the person running a workforce nobody hired, nobody onboarded and nobody knows how to fire.
We CEOs created that job. If CIOs are going to answer for that workforce, they need the authority to match. Otherwise, we haven’t given them a new job description. We’ve given them someone to blame.
