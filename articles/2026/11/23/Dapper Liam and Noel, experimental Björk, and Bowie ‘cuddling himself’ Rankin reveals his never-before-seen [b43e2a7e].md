# Dapper Liam and Noel, experimental Björk, and Bowie ‘cuddling himself’: Rankin reveals his never-before-seen shots of the stars

The Guardian · Graeme Green · 2026-11-23

“I hate AI,” says Rankin. “A lot of people think I’m an evangelical twat about it, and I’m not. I think it’s disgusting. I think the people who run it are disgusting. They’ve made a massive gamble with humanity and society and culture.”
His contempt may surprise anyone who’s familiar with Rankin’s substantial AI output. Ever since the artist Damien Hirst introduced him to the possibilities of generative AI in 2021, the British photographer has been going further down the rabbit hole. “I’m not somebody who likes to be critical about something without actually knowing about it,” he explains. “I’ve gone deep into trying to work it out.”
That culminated in nine months of work for his scathing AI conceptual art project and magazine Faik (thefaik.com), made up of artificially generated images, from a skateboarding granny to Rankin-style magazine covers of the likes of Elvis, Frida Kahlo, Nelson Mandela and even God, as well as billboards with bleak warnings: “The people who control AI will control history itself”; “When anyone can generate art in seconds, who will pay for it?”
Rather than falling head-over-heels for the alluring new tech’s “limitless” possibilities, Rankin’s exploration of AI sent him running back into the arms of his first love – photography, as made by real humans in an actual time and place – and inspired his new book, Contact. “Trying to figure out what this new technology means for the craft and the thing I love reaffirmed how important the exchange between people is,” he says.
The hefty 500-plus-word page tome contains an ocean-size collection of images (bands, actors, writers, models) that Rankin took between 1987 and 2006: Oasis, Blur, Tony Blair, Kylie, Kate Moss, André 3000, Hugh Grant … Rather than presenting just the standout shot selected for a magazine cover or lead, Rankin presents contact sheets of thumbnail images, which bring to life the encounter between photographer and subject – the double meaning of the book title’s referencing the importance of human contact. “It’s about how much you kind of fall in love with people each time – platonically, of course, unless it’s my wife,” Rankin says. “I’m always going for connection. You rarely see a picture of mine where the person isn’t looking in through the camera. I tell them: ‘Forget about me – connect through the lens.’”
Born in Glasgow in 1966, Rankin, or John Rankin Waddell, grew up in St Albans, initially training in accountancy before photography consumed him. In 1991, he and Jefferson Hack co-founded the influential arts, culture and fashion magazine Dazed & Confused, Rankin’s exuberant images dominating the covers. He went on to photograph everyone from the Rolling Stones to Queen Elizabeth II. In 2011, he also founded Hunger magazine.
His next major project will be a sequel to his 2006 book Tuulitastic, dedicated to the model Tuuli Shipster, his wife of 17 years. He’s also planning to immerse himself in wet-plate photography, a slow 19th-century process that also feels like an antidote to AI.
Rankin doesn’t believe AI will replace people wanting to take or have “proper photos” taken. But he does expect it to transform the medium and devastate the industry, predicting it will be replacing jobs within less than a year, from image-retouching agencies to hair, makeup and styling. “AI is like Photoshop on crack,” he says.
While he’s not working on any AI-focused projects currently, he hasn’t finished with it entirely. “I’m quite sick of it,” he says. “But I do feel I’ve got more to explore. I’m looking at what you can create with it that you’ve never created before. People really hate it and I can’t stand it. But I’m still inquisitive about what it will become.”
Contact by Rankin is published by Hunger (£30), with a Contact exhibition also at Rankin’s Annroy Gallery, London, 2 December to3 February.
Top Rankin: six photos from his new book
Liam and Noel Gallagher, 2002, Q, issue 190 (main image)
 
“I always loved photographing Liam and Noel. Liam was like a panther – you never knew if he’d scratch you or purr. And Noel had brilliant one-liners. I’d set up a studio. Keith Drummond, the art director, and I were obsessed with David Bailey’s Lennon and McCartney shoot, so that inspired us. They looked so dapper, like real rock stars, and they played up the sibling rivalry for us.”
Björk, 1994, Dazed & Confused, issue 9
  
“Björk is the best person in the world to photograph; she’s so experimental. I was trying to do a version of another photographer’s image. She knew the photo and said: ‘Don’t do that – be yourself.’ I must’ve taken 200 rolls of film with her.”
David Bowie, 1995, Dazed & Confused, issue 14 
  
“I expected to meet the dude from The Man Who Fell to Earth film: a spaceman who was cold, serious and unemotional. Instead, I met Tigger,” says Rankin. “Bowie was so excited. I sometimes say to people: ‘Cuddle yourself’ – it’s a self-love thing. David Bowie was one of the first people I tried it on. You can see he’s cuddling himself, but then there’s a performance there.”
Vivienne Westwood, 2002, POP magazine
  
“Vivienne was so funny – no ego. She was such a laugh to shoot. The editor who commissioned me for this hated editing my shoots, but you can see her mark on this one. Most people would think they look ugly in that picture but Vivienne loved it so much that picture ended up being her poster for her V&A exhibition.”
Goldie, 1999, Dazed & Confused and INCredible Supplement
  
“What’s great about Goldie is that he always performs to the camera. You never fail to get a good picture with Goldie. I like this one, as it’s a bit less like Goldie – it’s more subtle. He’s often in-your-face, which is lovely, but I feel that there’s a bit more of the softer Goldie in this photo.”
