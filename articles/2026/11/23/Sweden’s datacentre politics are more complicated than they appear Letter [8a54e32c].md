# Sweden’s datacentre politics are more complicated than they appear | Letter

The Guardian · Guardian Staff · 2026-11-23

There are distinct tensions over who controls, benefits from and gains access to natural and public resources, write James White and Nora Söderberg Steve Rose’s account of Sweden’s datacentre sector ( ‘Datacentres are deeply unpopular. Has Sweden made one we can all love?’, 12 November ) is a welcome reminder that digital infrastructure can be developed in more environmentally responsible ways. However, his suggestion that datacentres have become politically contentious elsewhere, “but here in Sweden, not so much”, is a misrepresentation.

 Consider the proposed Google datacentre development at Torsboda, near Sundsvall, which requires an enormous allocation of electricity and industrial land. The StoppaGoogle campaign , initiated by the regional political party Norrlandspartiet, has been vocal in challenging what this development will mean for electricity prices, employment and regional development.

 Controversy over datacentres in Sweden’s north reflect wider debate about resource extraction for the green and technological transitions, and the worry that Sweden is becoming an “electricity colony” of big tech , providing cheap, green energy while the economic benefits are realised elsewhere.

 At the same time, Sweden-based researchers, such as Julia Velkova , and Jutta Haider and Malte Rödl , have argued that the sustainability of digital infrastructure cannot be reduced to carbon emissions and technological efficiency. Datacentres place high demands on electricity grids, necessitating government investment and compromising access for other industries, communities and public services. While these direct impacts of datacentres are important, so too are the downstream consequences that they overshadow, including how digital services promote climate disinformation and unsustainable mass consumption.

 Sweden’s renewable energy infrastructure does not make datacentre development environmentally or politically unproblematic, but contributes to distinct tensions over who controls, benefits from and gains access to natural and public resources.

 Rather than presenting Sweden as an exception to the growing politicisation of datacentres, we should recognise how these conflicts emerge differently in different places, shaped by their histories, infrastructures and political economies.
 
 James White and Nora Söderberg
