# The CFO at the center of Amazon’s AI spending blitz

Business Insider · Eugene Kim, Eugene Kim (ekim@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-23

John Felton, Chief Financial Officer of Amazon Web Services. Chris Jung/NurPhoto via Getty Images AWS CFO John Felton steers Amazon's $220 billion AI buildout amid investor worries over returns. He's pushing AWS to build capacity faster while trying to drive down costs. Felton's retail operations background now informs Amazon's AI buildout. At a September staff meeting, Amazon Web Services CFO John Felton offered a blunt assessment of the forecasts behind Amazon's unprecedented AI spending push . The company needed to move beyond a single estimate of future demand and better understand the costs of both stronger and weaker demand for AI, he argued. "Right now our planning process is too cumbersome and too sequential and we need to basically make it much more real time," Felton said, according to a recording of the meeting obtained by Business Insider. "The point forecast is inherently going to be wrong." Felton is grappling with one of the biggest questions in business: How much should you bet on AI when nobody knows how much demand there will ultimately be for this technology? With trillions of dollars at stake, getting the answer wrong could have enormous consequences for Amazon, investors, and the broader economy. As AWS's finance chief, Felton is responsible for getting the answer right. He brings an unusual background to the challenge. Before moving to AWS in 2024, Felton spent roughly two decades helping Amazon build its vast retail and delivery network, where betting too much or too little on future demand could be costly. Now he's applying those lessons to AI, where the stakes are even higher and the future is arguably harder to predict. Amazon is pouring money into chips, power, and data centers long before it knows how much customers will ultimately need. "AI is moving faster than any technology we have seen, and it is still early," Felton told Business Insider. "The signals we see, with customers committing and demand running ahead of what we can install, tell us the investment is justified." Amazon leads all major cloud providers in announced and credible data center capacity, according to an Aterio and Bernstein analysis. Aterio/ Bernstein Building at the last responsible moment Former Amazon executives describe Felton as even-keeled and funny, a steady presence in the company's high-pressure leadership culture. They say he does not just look at the financial case, but also wants to understand what has to happen on the ground for an investment to work. Felton joined Amazon's S-team, its most senior group of executives that include CEO Andy Jassy, in 2020. When he was offered the AWS CFO role about two and a half years ago, he said accepting was "a very easy yes." AWS was becoming one of Amazon's most consequential businesses, and he wanted to help navigate an AI shift he believed would transform the company. Felton's investment theory is simple: Commit early to the assets that take years to secure, and delay everything else until customer demand is clearer. "Land, power and permitting take years, so we commit to those early," Felton told Business Insider. "Everything we can buy later, we buy later, because waiting gives us more information and keeps our choices open." The goal, he said, is to make "the right decision at the last responsible moment." "If the demand is not there, we do not place the order," Felton added. AWS uses customer commitments as its clearest signal of demand, Felton said. Its backlog of cloud contracts stood at $578 billion as of the third quarter and was growing at a triple-digit rate. AWS revenue has accelerated for six straight quarters, most recently growing 39% to $46 billion. The payoff, Felton argues, extends beyond AI services themselves. Customers that already run applications and data on AWS's cloud are likely to bring their AI workloads there too, creating additional demand for computing and storage across the business. "The biggest opportunity is that AI and the rest of AWS grow together," he told Business Insider. "That is the flywheel, and it is why we like the economics." 'The math is hard' Felton is focused on getting more AI capacity online without letting the cost of each data center and server cluster spiral higher. "The math is hard," Felton said at the September meeting. "Are we the lowest-cost kind of provider? How do you measure that?" Felton's strategy is to give AWS more ways to add capacity, rather than make an all-or-nothing commitment years in advance. At the September meeting, Prasad Kalyanaraman, an AWS infrastructure leader, described developing a wider menu of data center designs and server configurations to give the company more options. Kalyanaraman said that approach makes Felton "happy" because AWS would not have to spend the money "three years in advance on an uncertain forecast, which we have no way of predicting." AWS applied that scrutiny even to electrical equipment inside data centers. In a recent procurement process for power-distribution cabinets, the company weighed suppliers' factory capacity and tariff exposure, not simply price. That resulted in AWS internally evaluating a potential deal with manufacturer Jabil that could slash annual costs by $300 million, according to an internal document reviewed by Business Insider. AWS is pursuing similar savings in how it builds data centers. As Business Insider previously reported, Project Houdini uses a modular design that shifts parts of construction into factories, while Project Titus is redesigning facilities for denser AI hardware and lower power and cooling costs. Even with those efforts, equipment can arrive before final sites are ready. Business Insider exclusively reported that AWS began receiving billions of dollars in Nvidia equipment before some data centers had sufficient power, with racks staged in intermediary warehouses. An AWS spokesperson said staging servers ahead of deployment is standard business practice. "We have learned that there is no perfect forecast, so you plan for several possible outcomes, and keep adjusting as the world changes," Felton told Business Insider. The retail lessons behind the role Felton learned the danger of getting capacity planning wrong during the COVID-19 pandemic. Amazon raced to add fulfillment centers, transportation capacity, and workers to meet surging e-commerce demand at the time. When growth later slowed, Amazon was left with too much warehouse capacity. In a 2022 internal email obtained by Business Insider, Felton said Amazon needed to regain the "right balance" of safety, quality, speed, and costs. "We are going to continue to have cost challenges in front of us until we are able to grow into our capacity," he wrote at the time. A former Amazon executive who worked with Felton said he pushed teams during pandemic, tariff, and supply-chain disruptions to show how their plans would hold up if demand weakened or conditions changed. "He's not cavalier at all," the former executive said. "He knows he needs to have data for those numbers." Felton later helped regionalize Amazon's US delivery network, moving inventory and fulfillment closer to customers. In the 2024 announcement of his move to AWS, retail chief Doug Herrington credited Felton and his team with improving delivery speeds and reducing costs. "We built fast during the pandemic because customers needed us," Felton told Business Insider. "That experience taught us to build against demand we can see." Moving quickly with multiple options That discipline can sometimes lead AWS to spend more, rather than less. A former AWS executive told Business Insider that the company spent roughly $100 million to speed up a data center project by six to nine months about two years ago. Felton pressed teams to show why the faster timeline justified the cost, the person said, and AWS ultimately approved the investment because getting capacity online sooner mattered more. The stakes are visible in Amazon's latest quarterly filing. The company disclosed $159.6 billion in long-term commitments to acquire property and equipment that had not yet appeared on its balance sheet, including nearly $64 billion due in 2027. The filing does not say how much is tied to AWS or AI, but it offers a window into the infrastructure pipeline Felton's team must finance — and turn into returns. For Felton, the point is not to wait for perfect information. It is to preserve flexibility where Amazon can, then move before competitors do. "Speed is a choice," he told Business Insider. "There are times to go slow and times to go fast, and with AI I think the answer is to go fast." Have a tip?
