# The Costs Hidden In Your AI Checkout

Forbes · Shawn Yu, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-23

Shawn Yu, ROZO Co-founder, building Visa Layer for Stablecoins.
The initial price is not always the final total. Baymard Institute’s checkout research found that, excluding shoppers who were simply browsing, 40% cited high additional costs, including shipping, taxes and fees, while 12% cited an inability to determine the total cost upfront.
These findings cover e-commerce broadly, rather than AI credits specifically. But they raise a question for AI platforms: Can buyers understand the full cost before committing?
I work on payments between people and AI platforms. For businesses selling AI credits, checkout clarity matters. Buyers should distinguish usable credit from taxes, platform charges and payment fees.
The question is straightforward: How much will leave my account, and how much usable credit will I receive?
Too often, those answers arrive at different moments.
Five Surprises Beyond The Sticker Price
In early October, we reviewed about 100 public posts from people trying to pay for AI credits. This was an informal review, not a representative survey, but several recurring problems stood out.
Tax at the final step. Some buyers reported discovering VAT or GST only near the end of checkout. Their frustration was not always about the obligation itself. It was also about the gap between the price they expected and the total they were asked to approve.
Foreign exchange charges. A checkout denominated in dollars may create an additional cost for someone whose card account uses another currency. The issuer’s charge may appear later on a statement, beyond the platform’s checkout total.
The fallback card. When a payment fails, buyers try another card or payment method. That can change the purchase cost. One person described trying two cards and a mobile wallet before a pay-later option worked.
Minimum top-ups. A minimum purchase is not necessarily a fee: The balance may remain available for future use. But it raises the amount a buyer must commit before knowing whether the product is useful. For someone experimenting with a small AI task, that distinction matters.
Retries and delayed confirmation. When credits do not appear promptly, buyers may try again. One person reported eventually being charged six times. Even when duplicate charges can be reversed, the uncertainty creates work for both the customer and the support team.
These are different problems. Combining them under a single label such as “fees” makes the checkout harder to understand and the underlying issues harder to fix.
What Does $10 Actually Buy?
Consider a hypothetical platform selling $10 of usable credits with a 5.5% card-processing charge, subject to a $0.80 minimum.
The buyer pays $10.80 before tax or any issuer charges. At this purchase size, the minimum makes the effective platform charge 8%.
If a hypothetical 19% tax applies to the entire $10.80 subtotal, the checkout total becomes approximately $12.85. Actual tax treatment depends on the transaction and jurisdiction.
The buyer may still face a currency-conversion charge from their card issuer.
The point is not that every buyer pays this amount. It is that “$10 of credits” describes the balance received, while leaving the total purchase cost unresolved.
A useful checkout should show both numbers together.
Alternative Payment Methods Need The Same Scrutiny
Some commenters suggested cryptocurrency as a cheaper route. But alternative payment methods also need a complete cost explanation.
A stablecoin payment may involve a platform charge, a network fee, a conversion cost and a minimum purchase. Buyers also need to know which token and network the merchant accepts. A balance on one network may not be immediately usable on another.
Explain tax obligations consistently across payment methods. A missing tax line is not sufficient evidence that no tax is due.
The standard should be the same for cards, bank transfers and digital assets: Explain the total, identify the limitations and confirm what the buyer will receive.
Moving a surprise to another payment path does not solve the trust problem.
Why The Surprise Matters
A buyer who sees $12.85 upfront can decide whether the purchase is worthwhile. A buyer who starts with a $10 expectation and discovers the difference at the final step must reassess the purchase after already investing time.
That creates an avoidable question: What else have I missed?
For small purchases, the difference can be particularly noticeable. A fixed minimum charge takes a larger percentage of a small top-up. A minimum balance requirement increases the commitment. Currency conversion can add another layer.
Businesses should distinguish between a price customers consider too high and a price customers struggle to understand. Lowering the price may address the first problem. Clearer checkout design addresses the second.
Show The Landed Price
Importers use landed cost to describe the combined expense of acquiring goods, including transportation and other applicable charges.
AI platforms can apply the same principle from the buyer’s perspective: Show what the buyer pays and what reaches their balance.
Three Changes Would Help
First, show the complete bill before payment authorization. Once the information needed to calculate tax is available, display the credit amount, platform charges, payment charges and applicable tax separately. Where an issuer charge cannot be calculated, explain that limitation plainly.
Second, explain what changes when the buyer switches payment methods. A buyer comparing a card with another route should see the relevant fees, minimums, expected credit amount and confirmation time. Labels should reflect who charges each amount and what it covers.
Third, reduce avoidable payment layers. Where practical, businesses can evaluate more direct payment routes that reduce processing costs or simplify settlement. But assess direct payment by total cost and reliability. Network fees, conversion costs and operational work may still remain.
Clear payment status matters, too. After authorization, buyers should know whether a transaction is pending, completed or failed—and whether another attempt could create a duplicate payment.
Buyers do not need every payment to be free. They need to understand the purchase before committing.
For AI platforms, that means treating checkout clarity as part of the product. The price of accessing intelligence should not require detective work.
