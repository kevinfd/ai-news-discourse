# Many languages, one common neural code

MIT News · Rubina Veerakone | McGovern Institute for Brain Research · 2026-11-23

Languages take on thousands of forms. Some are tonal, like Vietnamese, where shifts in pitch convey distinct meanings. English relies on precise word orders, but this matters less for Russian, where words can be swapped without changing the intended message.
Despite so much variety in the linguistic kingdom, an MIT study has found that the human brain may process all sorts of languages in a universal way.
Artificial intelligence made this finding possible. In the study published today in Nature Neuroscience, the researchers used multilingual models to predict how the human brain responds to various languages. Those models also helped the researchers detect a shared brain process across people who collectively spoke 21 different languages.
“Of course, there are many differences in the ways humans converse with each other. But our team found that there is something fundamentally similar in how the brain communicates,” says Evelina Fedorenko, who is an investigator at MIT’s McGovern Institute for Brain Research, an associate professor of brain and cognitive sciences, and the senior author of the paper.
The study is the first to use multilingual models to predict brain responses across many languages. As researchers develop AI language tools to diagnose brain disorders, this work can extend their benefit to speakers of more languages, and may one day even keep endangered ones from vanishing.
Parsing the linguistic mind
Language allows us to share our thoughts about the outside world and the ones we create in our minds. That power lives within a network of brain regions located in the left hemisphere.
“Every language is managed by these same parts of the brain,” explains Andrea Gregor de Varda, the lead author of the new study, a postdoc in the Fedorenko lab, and fellow in the K. Lisa Yang Integrative Computational Neuroscience (ICoN) Center.
While scientists know that all languages activate the same parts of the brain, de Varda and Fedorenko wanted to know if that meant the brain processes them in the same way.
For example, they wondered, does the brain of someone who speaks Korean operate comparably to the brains of a Hindi or Spanish speaker?
It’s extremely hard to answer that question if you lack the right tools, de Varda says. And they needed one capable of creating a common code for comparing all sorts of languages — a task that is well-suited to artificial intelligence.
Where AI meets the brain
Large language models (LLMs) — such as those powering ChatGPT and Claude — interpret language much like the human brain, although they weren’t designed to do so. That surprising similarity is allowing de Varda and others in the field to wield these tools as a piercing lens into the human mind. Such studies often explore LLMs’ ability to learn or make sense of linguistic elements, such as grammar and meaning, to glean insights into how the brain might handle communication. But early LLMs used to work within a single language. The MIT researchers thought another class of models might help expand that scope.
“Multilingual language models are LLMs that can process hundreds of languages at once,” explains Fedorenko. Like other AIs of their kind, multilingual models ingest words or phrases and output numbers that scientists can extract. “Those numbers show how the model represents that bit of language,” she says. Now the team could study many languages at once using a single numerical code from the models. But the extent to which the models aligned with the human mind still lurked in the unknowns. De Varda believed he could coax an answer into light through a computational approach.
Using machine learning, he merged two data sources: the multilingual models’ code and scans of people’s brains as they performed linguistic tasks. The product was an AI tool known as an encoding model. It takes a multilingual model’s code, say, for a sentence, and predicts how the human brain might light up in response to the same input.
Encoding models at the time only existed for a handful of languages including English, French, and Chinese. But de Varda sought to test whether those models could be extended to more — and importantly, understudied ones. Doing so could reveal new aspects in which AI mirrors the human brain, and even detect a shared neural component across speakers of various languages.
A mental bridge between languages
The Fedorenko lab already had preexisting brain imaging data from speakers of 12 languages: Afrikaans, Dutch, Farsi, French, Lithuanian, Marathi, Norwegian, Romanian, Spanish, Tamil, Turkish, and Vietnamese. They recruited speakers of nine additional ones for their new study — Italian, Portuguese, German, Russian, Polish, Hindi, Arabic, Mandarin, and Korean — and recorded their brain activity while they listened to stories from “Alice in Wonderland” in their native language. By coupling that brain imaging data with outputs from multilingual models, the team created encoding models for those 21 languages.
“We found that the encoding models accurately predicted brain responses in all of the languages we studied — even in Marathi and Lithuanian, that, in contrast to English, we only have a limited amount of data on,” says Fedorenko. “The fact that there’s this emergent similarity between models and humans across so many languages is really quite exciting.”
Another finding was more interesting still. Even though the researchers trained an encoding model in a single language, it could predict brain activity for a language it had never seen before. For example, an encoding model trained to predict brain responses in French could also predict responses in Vietnamese, without ever being trained in Vietnamese. It was a truly fascinating discovery, de Varda says, because this was only possible if all the speakers’ brains were representing language in a shared way: a neural thread binding human communication.
What was that shared component? Dissecting different linguistic features across all the languages suggested that it has something to do with meaning. “That’s the intended power of communication, right? To share meaning; new information or our feelings about the world. So it’s fascinating we see that reflected in this work,” says Fedorenko.
In science, this new study gives brain researchers some explanations for long-held curiosities among them. In society, it could steer emerging technologies into a more inclusive direction. Scientists are starting to explore clinical uses of AI language tools, such as those that can detect cognitive decline based on brain activity patterns during language. But most of those studies, and the methods clinicians use today, are in common and well-studied languages like English. By showing how AI and the human brain overlap across many diverse languages, this new research may be a step toward clinical tools that do not leave most speakers out.
In another vein, reversing the Fedorenko lab’s approach might help preserve languages on the brink of extinction. “Say you had one single speaker of a language that is dying out. You might imagine a case in which we can train a model in the opposite direction: give it images of that speaker’s brain, and it could translate those into language,” notes Fedorenko.
De Varda adds that there are features that clearly differ between languages, too, and that those must be somehow represented in the brain and even LLMs. Defining those differences in future research, he says, might unearth new fragments of knowledge to complete the puzzle of the linguistic mind.
