# The AI bubble hasn't burst, but it has significantly deflated

RNZ · billybuckwheat · 2026-11-23

The AI bubble hasn't burst, but it has significantly deflated
Analysis - Silver linings aren't always easy to spot during a time of crisis.
But the global stock market unravelling of the past six weeks, which has hammered the investment boom driving artificial intelligence, could inadvertently save us from an even greater calamity.
For the past four years, a stock market boom confined to and driven by a mere handful of corporations has inflated the wealth of most Australians and a significant proportion of the developed world.
In the past month and a half, that bubble has been seriously deflated.
Nvidia, which for five years had been basking in the warm embrace of global investors, saw its value decline by 40 percent from its peak early last month before a rebound overnight sparked by the reinstatement of its chief executive Jensen Huang.
Huang's complicated love life, not to mention his potential involvement in an espionage scandal, is only partly to blame.
The chip maker so essential to AI development and the global infrastructure rollout was on the slide for a full month before his impropriety came to light, on growing apprehension that future revenues would not live up to expectations and would arrive later than anticipated.
Nvidia wasn't the only casualty as the great AI investment edifice began to crumble.
New York's Nasdaq exchange, where most of the tech heavyweights are listed, has plunged more than 18 percent from its 6 October peak, eliminating all its gains for this year and then some.
For those who get a kick out of sprouting technical definitions, that sort of decline is well beyond a correction and not far off what's known as a bear market.
Either way, it's bad.
So, what's the good news?
Stock markets aren't the real economy, and central bankers will tell you ad nauseam that supporting them isn't part of their remit.
But the truth is that markets impact ordinary people, who are the real economy, and central bankers are always eager to provide insulation from any shocks.
And so, the events of the past month and a half will force a sudden rethink on interest rates.
Australia has led the charge with five rate hikes this year. But the inflationary surge resulting from the Iran war has seen the US, EU, Japan and the UK all raise rates in recent months.
The US Federal Reserve last week revealed it was talking about a second hike at its most recent meeting. But Wall Street's wobbles are likely to put a stop to those kind of thoughts.
The crisis we could have had
While most people have been focused on global stock exchanges and the faltering AI investment trade, another crisis, one far more serious, has been brewing quietly in the background.
Ballooning government debts and the threat of defaults have been exercising the minds of money market investors for much of this year, especially in the past few months.
That has driven the cost of long-term debt to levels not seen in years, putting enormous strains on government funding costs, particularly in the US.
In some quarters, there were bets being made about which would hit first: an AI-driven stock market correction or a sovereign debt crisis that could rattle the global financial system.
An ongoing AI meltdown won't be pretty. But, unlike a sovereign debt crisis, it won't be catastrophic.
Until a few weeks ago, global investors had been dumping government debt. That has forced down the price of government bonds, forcing the yield or interest rate higher. Much higher.
America, the world's largest economy with the global reserve currency, has been hit hard as the cost of servicing its mammoth debt has soared.
It now pays upwards of $US1.25 trillion a year in interest to service its $US40 trillion debt pile. That's its second-biggest outlay and even more than it spends on defence.
With annual budget deficits of $US2 trillion a year, America's finances are running dangerously close to levels that are unsustainable.
After decades of ultra-low interest rates, many European nations are in similar territory with massive ongoing deficits that will need to be financed by debt.
The good news, for now at least, is that interest rates are unlikely to push higher as the stock market melee plays out, giving governments some much-needed breathing space to sort out their finances.
Bond traders have also taken the pressure off, with long-term yields falling substantially in recent weeks.
But with ageing populations, heightened defence requirements and an ongoing energy transition, a permanent fix will necessarily involve a combination of brutal spending cuts and painful tax increases.
If not addressed, the pressures will continue to build.
How far could stock markets fall?
Economist John Maynard Keynes once quipped: "Stock markets can remain irrational longer than you can remain solvent."
In other words, don't even try to guess.
Since the 1990s, central banks have slashed interest rates any time there has been a dramatic shake-out on stock markets in an effort to minimise the impact on household spending.
The big difference this time around is that inflation is shifting higher as the ongoing conflicts in Iran and Ukraine become more entrenched.
So, while interest rates are unlikely to go higher, there's only limited capacity for cuts.
In the past few years, investors have waited for dramatic corrections and then dived in with their ears pinned back to buy stocks on the cheap, safe in the knowledge that central banks would ride to the rescue.
In the trade, it's known as "buy the dip".
When the pandemic hit in 2020, markets plunged on the spectre of a global economic shutdown. But in just over a month, global stocks began a dramatic recovery.
That was long before any prospect of a vaccine had materialised. And it was all because interest rates were slashed to zero and then into negative territory.
There was similar behaviour when Donald Trump announced his Liberation Day tariffs. A massive plunge, followed by a soaring recovery.
Not any longer. In the past few weeks, there has been real apprehension about "buying the dip" from traders on Wall Street.
This time around, the cavalry just doesn't have the ammo and traders know it.
How will we fare through a meltdown?
Every Australian will feel the pinch from an AI stock shake-out.
Precise numbers are difficult to come by but, of our $4.8 trillion super savings, about 20 per cent is invested on Wall Street. And within that, about 20 percent is directly invested in AI stocks.
Given the vast lift in wealth we've experienced in recent years, you have to expect a pullback at some time.
There is also likely to be fallout on our markets too.
Both our big miners, BHP and Rio Tinto, have transformed themselves into copper giants in recent years to take advantage of the switch from fossil fuels to electrification.
They've also found themselves enormous beneficiaries of the AI rollout as the kilometres of cabling and power generation needed to hook up data centres has boosted demand for the metal.
They've already taken a hit and the ASX, just like Wall Street, has surrendered its gains for the year.
So don't be too shocked to discover your super balance has shrunk the next time you look at your balance.
The Australian dollar has plunged in the past few weeks, partly because the November rate hike, the fifth for the year, is looking premature given the jump in unemployment and could be cut early in the new year.
Views are mixed on where it might head from here. As a commodity exporter, any downturn in the global economy will be reflected in the Aussie dollar.
The one blessing right now is that we are not in the midst of a global financial crisis rerun.
