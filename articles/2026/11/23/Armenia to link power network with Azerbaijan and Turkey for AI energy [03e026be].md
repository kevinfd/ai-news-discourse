# Armenia to link power network with Azerbaijan and Turkey for AI energy

Euronews · Symela Touchtidou · 2026-11-23

Armenia's prime minister announces electrical interconnections with Turkey and Azerbaijan, while Nvidia’s Rev Lebaredian warns of AI’s rising energy needs.
Just three years ago, Armenia and Azerbaijan were in the shadow of yet another bloody conflict. Today they are planning an electricity interconnection, with Turkey also in the picture.
Armenia has arrived on the global artificial intelligence (AI) map. And to keep its place, it needs electricity, partnerships and open borders.
Speaking at DigiTec 2026 in Yerevan, Armenia's prime minister Nikol Pashinyan announced that the country plans to connect its power grid with those of its two neighbours, Azerbaijan and Turkey.
He made the statement during a public discussion with Rev Lebaredian, vice president of Nvidia responsible for physical AI simulation.
"We will connect our grids with the electricity grids of Turkey and Azerbaijan. We are already connected with Iran and Georgia, and we are investing to make energy transmission more efficient," Pashinyan said.
He went on to announce that on Sunday, 22 November, the official launch will take place of TRIPP, the "Trump Route for International Peace and Prosperity" project, stressing that "a very important part of this initiative is energy and the transit of electricity".
"This is truly a very significant and incredible transformation," Pashinyan said. "The 'Crossroads of Peace' project is becoming reality."
From energy surplus to AI demand
The rapid growth of AI factories is sharply increasing Armenia's demand for electricity; Firebird alone plans infrastructure with a capacity of 300 megawatts by the end of 2027.
At DigiTec, Pashinyan revealed that eight months ago the country had surplus electricity capacity, whereas now the expansion of data centres is creating the need to look for import suppliers.
Nvidia vice president Rev Lebaredian also underlined the need for more energy:
"How do we use our advantage now so that we can move from hundreds of megawatts to many gigawatts?" he asked the Armenian prime minister.
He noted that the country's current lead will not last forever; securing energy from more sources is critical if it is to make the most of it before other countries develop similar capabilities, he warned.
Lebaredian also linked energy interconnections to the prospect of lasting peace: by buying energy and providing AI services to its neighbours, Armenia can create mutual interests.
"We become important to each other and that will increase the chances of having peace that will last for a long time," he argued.
More sources, more options
Pashinyan outlined a mix of measures: developing renewable sources, particularly solar, creating storage and improving energy efficiency in buildings and businesses.
He also reiterated the intention to build a new nuclear power plant. The international interconnections form part of this broader effort at diversification.
According to Armenian news agency ARKA, Armenia and Turkey have already discussed restoring and activating the existing but inactive Gyumri-Kars power line.
"If Europe and the US do not build, they will import AI"
Armenia's determination to secure more computing power comes at a time when, in the US and in Europe, the expansion of data centres is provoking resistance over electricity supply, water use and the impact on local communities.
In an interview with Euronews, Lebaredian argued that much of this backlash reflects broader fears about AI and the way it is presented in public debate.
"I believe it is essentially a failure of leadership to describe in a positive way the future that AI will bring," he said.
In his view, the data centre becomes a target because it is "the physical manifestation of AI".
"When people are told that this will take their jobs or will kill them, of course they will not want it," he said.
When the infrastructure is in their area, he added, they can go to the local government and say "I don't want it".
Asked about resource requirements, he acknowledged the need for more energy: "The more energy you have, the more AI you can produce."
He backed further development of sustainable sources, adding that "there is also nuclear energy, which we should be using much more".
On water use, he pointed to the option of closed-loop cooling systems, acknowledging that different technologies involve trade-offs between water consumption, energy use and cost.
"You can use different cooling systems, but the cost may go up because you need more energy for cooling. Evaporative cooling can be more energy-efficient," he explained. "In some places there is a surplus of water. It all depends on local conditions."
On noise, he noted that data centres can be located in sparsely populated areas. "The facility is here, in Hrazdan. No one complains about noise and such issues," he said, referring to Firebird's AI factory.
At the same time, he warned that delaying the development of infrastructure could create new dependencies for Western economies:
"If the US and Europe do not develop their own supply, they will simply have to import AI from the places that are developing it," he stressed.
