# AI Adoption Vs. Value: Four Things Leaders Can Do To Close The Gap​

Forbes · Jana Werner, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-23

Dr. Jana Werner, Executive in Residence at AWS, advising executives of Fortune 500 companies on building adaptive innovative organizations.
Most leadership teams can tell you precisely how many of their people are using AI. Far fewer can tell you whether they are producing better work because of it. A high adoption rate feels reassuring, but it can turn out to be a misleading metric. It tells you people have logged in without telling you whether their work improved. One organization we work with appeared to be 88% AI “adopted,” yet when its leaders looked closer, they found genuinely better work in fewer than 1 in every 5,000 sessions.
As Executives in Residence at AWS, we set out to study what differentiates organizations that get value out of AI at scale from those that don’t. Over nine months, we interviewed 154 executives across 27 countries and 23 industries, including 23 leaders from Amazon’s largest businesses. Our findings are published in our Reimagine report.
Here is what we found separated the two.
Use adoption as a diagnostic, not a destination.
Amazon ran a program called Adoption-to-Value across more than 10,000 mostly nontechnical staff, sorting each one into five stages of AI use from “curious” to “expert.” An important pattern emerged: A “curious” user hands the tool a simple task, takes whatever comes back and rates barely half of those sessions useful. An “expert” rates more than 9 in 10 useful because by that stage whole categories of manual work have disappeared. The tool itself never changed. What changed was how well the person could wield it.
The difference is a matter of hours. A curious user saves about five minutes an interaction; an expert saves more than four and a half hours, 58 times as much, because the expert redesigned entire workflows around the tool.
The headcount using AI tells you almost nothing on its own. Treat it as a diagnostic, a way to see who is stuck and where, and judge the thing by whether the work itself is getting better. One executive we spoke to refuses to track AI as a category at all, focusing instead on outcomes. If your organization is overly focused on metrics that measure activities (adoption rates, hours saved, prompts generated) rather than outcomes (revenue, reduced costs, improved customer experience), you know what to change first.
Decide where freed capacity goes before it is reabsorbed.
Most organizations in our research stall at the efficiency stage, where the gains are real and arrive fast but get reabsorbed into the work that already exists. The person who used to produce one PowerPoint an hour now produces four, which is three more deliverables that someone still must review.
Efficiency has a floor; growth has no ceiling. Leaders who treat efficiency as the end goal get faster versions of the same work, but leaders who treat it as a starting point get something else entirely. Start to assign freed time to a specific growth initiative each quarter, such as redesigning a process end-to-end or tackling a problem previously too expensive to solve, with a specific owner and deadline, not just a vague allocation of “innovation time.” The efficiencies gained and time saved can turn into new products, personalized customer experiences and untapped revenue streams.
Ask who owns the business result.
A consistent difference between organizations getting value from AI and those that did not came down to whether a business leader owned the outcome, or a technology team merely owned the tool. In most cases, AI sits with the technology team: The CTO sponsors the work, the engineers build it and the rest of the business is consulted along the way. What results is technically successful pilots that rarely scale, because no one owns the business outcome firmly enough to fight for the changes to make it real.
Almost nothing that decides whether an initiative succeeds is about the technology, and almost all of it depends on how ready the organization is and who is accountable for the result. If no leader owns an initiative’s impact on revenue, cost or customer experience, that reluctance tells you something worth knowing about the initiative.
Invest in people, not just tools.
An executive at a leading sports entertainment and gaming company put the ratio he trusts at “20 percent tools, 80 percent people.” Most organizations have it inverted. They spend heavily on tools, and their budgets go largely to short-lived technical training, such as this quarter’s prompt techniques and this month’s preferred model, which loses its value as the technology evolves.
As AI absorbs the routine execution that has traditionally filled early careers, such as triaging help desk tickets, reviewing contracts and producing first drafts, it also absorbs the repetitive work through which junior employees traditionally build judgment. Judgment is becoming the scarcest human capability as the usual path to develop it narrows.
The organizations pulling ahead are building five durable capabilities their talent development programs always valued but rarely treated as urgent. Those skills are agility, the ability to unlearn old skills and learn new ones; problem framing, defining the problem rather than just executing a given solution; judgment, recognizing value in information everyone now has; systems thinking, connecting a task to the wider system around it; and curiosity. If most of your training budget teaches a particular tool, move some of it toward the judgment that makes any tool worth having.
This is the question we return to.
Most leadership teams are still asking how many of their people are using AI. The more useful question, and the harder one to answer, is whether those people are producing better work because of it. None of these changes requires a new platform or a larger budget. They require the one thing AI cannot supply on your behalf: the decision to lead differently. Start with the AI investment that disappointed you most last year, and ask which of these four changes could make the difference.
