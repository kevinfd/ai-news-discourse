# The free market isn’t so free. Just look at Jensen Huang

Fast Company · Chris Stokel-Walker · 2026-11-23

There’s plenty to scratch your head about in the speedy and surprising return of Jensen Huang as Nvidia CEO, just 11 days after he resigned following revelations of an affair with a woman who may have helped make him a target of Chinese intelligence. Never mind the fact that two other allegations of improper relationships with Nvidia employees have emerged, alongside a slew of claims about his reportedly toxic management style. Or that he’s still, technically, under investigation, according to Nvidia’s statement announcing his return. Or that six days earlier, the board told investors it “does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO.”
Perhaps most head-scratching of all is the White House’s claim to reporters that “President Trump was totally involved” in Huang’s return to the company, and reports that he dialed into a board meeting to argue for the return of the executive whose resignation the board had accepted less than two weeks earlier. Trump has since claimed in a call to CNBC following the announcement that Huang was “exonerated”—before adding, almost incongruously, that “he’s in the business of selling chips.” (That leap between one thought—the basis for which remains unclear, given the unfinished independent investigation—and another tells its own story.)
Senator Elizabeth Warren has called the decision “reckless.” There’s another word for it that might come to mind. But the most diplomatic way to say it is that it’s the end of a free market in the United States.
Leaders of countries can have their own opinions about who should run a major company operating in their country. But they shouldn’t be influencing boardroom discussions about the running of that company. Nvidia’s own announcement acknowledges that directors received “input from senior levels” of the U.S. government before concluding that Huang’s reinstatement posed no national security risk.
The massive losses in the stock market, with Nvidia a lynchpin of the AI boom, won’t have gone unnoticed at 1600 Pennsylvania Avenue. And for a man who professes to be the master of “the art of the deal,” a deal that boosted Nvidia’s market value by roughly $1 trillion in a matter of hours sounds like a pretty sweet deal.
But rising share prices don’t answer questions about Huang’s conduct, or his paramour’s potential ties to Chinese intelligence. Nor do they explain why a supposedly independent board tasked with holding executives accountable appears to have allowed a world leader to influence its deliberations and ultimately sway its decision. Trump, after all, explicitly said he “convinced them” to reinstate Huang.
And what about the Nvidia employees on the receiving end of Huang’s reported tirades? According to The New York Times, he has threatened to rip off employees’ arms and beat them to death with them, tear the tops of their heads off and “pee on your brain,” and told one employee that his mother should have aborted him. To be sure, many Nvidia employees have expressed support for Huang. But at least when Sam Altman was deposed as CEO by his board at OpenAI nearly three years ago to the day, the groundswell of opinion in favor of reinstating him represented a near-unanimous employee revolt.
The truth is that Jensen Huang may ultimately be cleared of the most serious allegations being made against him—that’s literally what investigations are for. But the independent investigation hasn’t concluded. And some of the most powerful people in America seem to have arrived at the verdict first, and decided the process can catch up later. That’s not a free market. But it is a big, beautiful trade.
