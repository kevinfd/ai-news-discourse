# University Approves Nuclear Weapons Data Center, Township Vows to Fight On

404 Media · Matthew Gault · 2026-11-23

Last week, the University of Michigan’s board of regents approved the construction of a $1.25 billion data center that will power American nuclear weapons research. The regents voted 7 - 1 in a public meeting on November 19 to move forward with the project despite widespread opposition from faculty, staff, and the residents of Ypsilanti Township, Michigan where the data center will operate. A few hours after the vote, the leaders and citizens of the Township gathered to discuss how to stop it.
U of M and Los Alamos National Laboratories (LANL) first announced plans to build what the University then called an “AI research facility” in 2024. U of M now calls the project a “research computing center” and, if all goes to its plan, construction will begin on a 125,000 square foot facility in late 2027. The data center could expand to “no more than 300,000 square feet,” according to a U of M blog post.
During the regent’s meeting, faculty, staff, and members of the community shared their thoughts on the data center’s construction.
“It is widely promoted as the next step in furthering novel research taking place at this University. What is conveniently left out on flyers and pamphlets is that this is only part of the proposition, 10% in fact, the other 90% is being used by Los Alamos — a nuclear weapons developer,” a sophomore studying biochemistry told the board before the vote. “We already have three existing high tech data centers used for research. Why must we push forward with another one that is promoting nuclear warfare?”
An electrician from Ypsilanti Township, a representative of the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers (IBEW) union, supported the construction project. “This will create 300 plus jobs for our members, all local, [and] give back to the community,” he said. “IBEW is in favor of the data center, excuse me, ‘high compute research center.’”
People who attended the regent’s meeting to discuss other topics referenced the vote during their presentations. “You voted today to fund a $785 million data center project in Ypsilanti, much to the opposition of the residents of the area, acting against what many residents in the area are calling environmental racism. I think I can speak for many students when I say I am frankly appalled by this decision,” a student who was there to discuss carbon emissions said.
A library worker present to talk about how the University treats its workers invoked the data center. “I’m not sure I can confidently appeal to your sense of compassion around this. The officials and residents of Ypsi Township are saying ‘stop,’ saying ‘no,’ saying ‘this hurts.’ And you and others responsible for this nuclear weapons data center project have shown you don’t care about the basic issue of consent as you plow ahead,” he said.
Ypsilanti Township is at the tip of the spear in the new nuclear arms race.
Despite the opposition in the room, the regents approved the project. “It’s been very controversial [...] For me, this is all about an investment in the community and it’s an investment, in many different ways,” regent Denise Ilitch said just before the vote. “The community will be enhanced — they may not feel it now, some, but I venture to say that there will be much good that will come from this.”
Many in Ypsilanti Township disagree. After the regents voted, the leadership and residents of the Township gathered for a panel discussion about the data center. One of the speakers that night was Jay Coghlan, the executive director of Nuclear Watch of New Mexico, a watchdog group that tracks Los Alamos. “The data center issue here in Ypsilanti is unique in that it is the clearest example of the pending merger of AI and nuclear weapons, which doesn’t bode well,” Coghlan told 404 Media.
Coghlan told 404 Media that he shared the Township’s concerns about how the data center will use water and electricity but said he thinks its construction will have farther reaching consequences. “Our nation is transforming from the military-industrial complex that Eisenhower warned us about to a Silicon Valley-Pentagon-Wall Street complex that closely links economics, economic inequality, increasing militarism, and I dare say the rise of techno-fascism,” he said.
U of M has repeatedly said the data center will mostly be used to power medical breakthroughs and conduct cancer research, something it’s highlighted in flyers and pamphlets it’s mailed to people in the Township. LANL sent a letter to the people of the community in September that promised it wouldn't build nuclear weapons in their backyard.
“I'm going ‘Duh, yeah, you're not going to bring plutonium to Ypsilanti in Michigan,” Coghlan said. “But I expect this data center to have a lot to do with the design of new nuclear weapons. So, in that way, Ypsilanti Township is at the tip of the spear in the new nuclear arms race.”
Coghlan said he finds hope in the opposition to the project. “I’m very impressed by the intensity of opposition that I have witnessed. There seems to be a pretty widespread public awareness of the data center in Ypsilanti, Township.”
During the Township meeting on November 19, after the regents voted, Ypsilanti Township attorney Douglas Winters said he was mad. “What did we learn on November 3rd? Two weeks ago and two days, voters around this country loudly declared that they were sick and tired of government corruption. They were tired of secrecy. They were tired of abuse of power. They were tired of threats to democracy and the disregard of governmental accountability. And, importantly, they also said they were sick and tired of these data centers,” he said. “Somehow that message did not make it to Ann Arbor. That message did not make it to the board of regents.”
Winters and other leaders vowed to keep fighting the data center’s construction. In May, the community’s water authority voted to restrict new construction on infrastructure to data center projects while it conducts a review of their impact on the Township’s water supply. The University called the move “unlawfully discriminatory.” In August, the Township passed a six month moratorium on new electrical infrastructure, a move aimed at delaying the project.
