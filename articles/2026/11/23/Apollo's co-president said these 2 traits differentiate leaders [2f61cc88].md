# Apollo's co-president said these 2 traits differentiate leaders

Business Insider · Ana Altchek, Ana Altchek (aaltchek@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-23

Scott Kleinman, co-president of Apollo Asset Management, hired 140 of the firm's 200 partners. Benjamin Girette/Bloomberg via Getty Images Scott Kleinman, co-president of Apollo Asset Management, has hired 140 of the firm's 200 partners. He said employees with partner potential have two qualities that set them apart. Kleinman said the top workers demonstrate curiosity and have a "willingness to just grind." Breaking into private equity is known to be grueling. Making partner is the next climb. Scott Kleinman, co-president of Apollo Asset Management , has hired 140 of the firm’s 200 partners, some of whom started in more junior roles before moving up. That's given him a clear view of what separates those who make the cut. Kleinman was the 13th hire at the firm, which now has roughly 6,000 employees. He co-leads Apollo's day-to-day operations, including all of its revenue-generating businesses and enterprise solutions across its integrated investment platform. He told Business Insider that while there are many specific skills for each job, there are two overarching traits that separate those who become partners from those who don't. 
 
 This is part of a series of stories from Business Insider's conversation with Scott Klein, co-president of Apollo Asset Management and chair of the Apollo Opportunity Foundation Board. Apollo is sharing its private-equity playbook with nonprofits Apollo exec says firm plans to lower annual head count growth, mainly because of AI 
 
 The first is intellectual curiosity. Those are the workers, Kleinman said, who not only know "their stuff" but are always going the extra step to ask "why?" "What could we be doing better? How do we disrupt this? How do we disrupt ourselves? Where should the ball be going?" said Kleinman, who also serves as board chair of the Apollo Opportunity Foundation, the company's nonprofit organization . Kleinman said that those workers aren't just asking those questions in relation to their own business. Often these individuals have other interests outside of their core expertise. He said that "cross-stitching" of different knowledge areas across domains is valuable. The second trait is grit, Kleinman said, or "that willingness to just grind." He said these sorts of employees work harder than the next person and don't take no for an answer. When you combine these two qualities, Kleinman said, "that's where you just do better than most people." More than hard work While working harder than the next person may have helped many partners get to where they are, Kleinman said that strategy may no longer be enough to differentiate today's highest performers, as AI levels the playing field. "The thing that got me here is not going to get you there," Kleinman said at the Apollo Opportunity Foundation's biennial summit in October. Kleinman said junior talent needs to consider "the moment that we're in" and what gaps need to be filled. He told Business Insider that entrepreneurship isn't the only route, but it's " a route," and one that more young people are going to have to consider as big companies will be hiring less. The executive said Apollo plans to reduce its head count growth to 3-5% annually, down from its typical 12-15% range. Other executives have offered similar advice to Business Insider on what it takes to climb the corporate ladder in the AI era. PwC's US CEO and the CEO of Nestlé have both named curiosity as a key quality they seek in candidates for promotion. Walmart's chief people officer , meanwhile, pointed to grit — or the ability to persevere through difficult circumstances — as one of the two traits that get workers promoted quickly.
