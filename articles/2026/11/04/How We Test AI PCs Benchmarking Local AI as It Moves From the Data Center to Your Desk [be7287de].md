# How We Test AI PCs: Benchmarking Local AI as It Moves From the Data Center to Your Desk

PCMag · Brian Westover · 2026-11-04

When the first so-called "AI PCs" launched a couple of years ago, they were mostly mainstream laptops whose processors were outfitted with the early instances of neural processing units (NPUs). Branded as "Copilot+ PCs," they offered simple AI enhancements that worked in the background, on standard laptops with mainstream processing and memory. The typical NPU was rated at 40 trillion operations per second (TOPS), and ramped up to 50 TOPS, 60 TOPS, and even 80 TOPS.
Then came the Nvidia DGX Spark. Something of an AI supercomputer in the body of a mini-desktop, it delivered a stunning 1,000 TOPS of AI power—a full petaflop of compute for running local AI models.
Once that level of capability was commercially available, there was no stopping a complete reimagining of the PC to put AI first. Now, we have devices like the new RTX Spark laptops, a family of slim, AI-centric laptops built around new Nvidia silicon. They have enough processing muscle and huge-enough memory pools to bring much the same level of local AI found in the DGX Spark to laptops.
If that's the direction PCs are moving, then as they go, so PCMag follows. We've been working on an AI benchmarking test regimen for a while now, because the writing was on the wall that, before long, AI compute would start to appear on the edge devices users have in their homes, not just the servers and data centers of the major AI providers.
To that end, we've put together the first iteration of our AI hardware test suite, described below. Before we get into it, know that things are sure to evolve as AI PCs, local models, and consumer hardware morph and evolve to stay competitive in this brave new AI-filled world. This is the first step in a new journey.
Beyond Speeds and Feeds: The Moving Target of AI Performance
In my 16 years of reviewing PCs and other consumer tech products, testing has always been at the heart of it. It doesn't replace real-world experience, but it does inform it, giving subjective experience and impressions a clear shape, with objective data and repeatable results. Measuring performance is an inextricable part of evaluating a product: Does it do what it's made to do? And does it do that better or worse than the competition? Those two questions are really at the root of all product reviews.
When evaluating laptops and desktops built around conventional CPUs, GPUs, and memory, we benefit from months and months of the product cycle, during which we test and measure a chip as it's implemented in many PCs. We have clear specs to reference, established metrics to examine, and pre-existing tests that let us zero in on one aspect of performance or another. (See how we test laptops and desktops using our existing productivity and graphics tests.) Sure, there's a lot about the laptop experience that's still subjective, but it all builds on that hardware foundation, and specs and test results let us define that foundation in clear terms.
AI performance isn't so cut-and-dried. Our long-established benchmark routine for laptops and desktops can't effectively evaluate the AI aspect of AI PCs, in part because AI performance is anything but static—we don't get months of testing a single product line to know what the performance should be. The market has moved on by then.
For starters, you've got different types of components, from the NPUs that are part of the very latest processors to powerful GPUs, as well as new, powerful system-on-a-chip (SoC) architectures that combine the two with shared memory for running AI models locally. And that memory is critical to an AI PC: The amount of memory available dictates the size of the AI model you can run.
Then you've got the models themselves. When you're talking about locally run AI, there are more than 2 million open-source AI models available. And with AI technology changing at a breakneck pace, the question of what capabilities and models can be squeezed onto which hardware becomes a very complicated puzzle.
The other complication in measuring AI performance? Individual models rely on different runtime environments, such as ONNX Runtime, DirectML, Apple Core ML, TensorRT-LLM, or OpenVino. All AI models are fundamentally composed of mathematical operations, and the runtime translates pure math into instructions for the silicon a system runs on, optimizing for the specific underlying hardware architectures. Think of the runtime as a middleman layer akin to a car's gearbox, matching the workload and data flow to the different processor hardware. Each chip and OS uses a different runtime (sometimes, more than one), adding another dimension of complexity to AI performance testing.
Doing all of this while the AI ecosystem changes every few weeks has made static benchmarking of AI performance close to impossible. Early benchmarks quickly became unhelpfully shallow as NPU technology developed and AI frameworks changed. And that informed our early efforts: None of the tools, or the environments, stayed the same long enough to be useful. We also looked at adapting enterprise tools for evaluating server-grade hardware, but found most of them to be overkill for our needs. And we've even tried to figure out how to turn genuine hands-on use cases (such as model installation and workflow ease of use) into repeatable, measurable tests. It's all a work in progress, aiming at moving targets, in a world where AI is advancing more rapidly than pretty much any other technology ever has.
Our Test-Suite Rollout: A New Grounding for AI Testing
But now we've got a good footing for the start of our testing. To develop it, we worked with Matthew Murray, a PCMag alum and director of CNET Group Labs, and Scott Wasson, principal analyst at Damage Labs. Wasson has years of experience working with chip makers and with MLCommons, a consortium of AI experts that act as a governing body for a lot of AI benchmarking. He's also a former journalist, which proved valuable in refining broad benchmarking technologies into metrics most useful to you, the end user. That background was instrumental in helping us get a solid footing in this first phase of AI testing.
Because the label "AI PC" has expanded far beyond simple NPU-equipped mainstream laptops to include local agents (the software that manages autonomous AI agents) and powerful desktop developer rigs, one-size-fits-all benchmarks no longer work. We'll talk about the specific tests and what they tell us. But first, we need to talk about the different hardware we'll be deploying these tests on, and how we classify it.
Defining the Hardware Spectrum: The Three Tiers of AI PC We Test
For our testing purposes, we see AI PCs evolving into three distinct categories, ranging from mainstream laptops and desktops to extraordinarily high-end developer desktops capable of running large local AI models. Let's define each.
Class 1 Systems: Everyday AI and Copilot+ PCs
First, we have everyday PCs with NPU hardware. These are the lowest-level AI-enabled laptops and desktops, leveraging an NPU or a less-capable GPU. They will have at least 16GB of system memory, plus 8GB of VRAM if there's a discrete GPU. With mainstream processing power and standard RAM allocations, these basic AI PCs can run small language models and apply machine learning to smaller tasks, such as summarizing text, providing live translation and captions, and enhancing your webcam image.
Class 2 Systems: Workstations and High-End Gaming Rigs
Broadly speaking, these are gaming laptops and mobile workstations with enough extra GPU muscle to run medium-sized LLMs and agents. These are higher-end systems, but the key ingredient isn't price; it's the parallel processing and dedicated VRAM of the graphics card that allow a system to handle locally hosted open-source AI models. These systems feature at least 32GB of system memory and a discrete GPU with at least 16GB of VRAM.
Class 3 Systems: Developer-Grade Local AI
These machines are built for AI, with unique hardware and significantly more memory than the mainstream systems or gaming rigs in Classes 1 and 2. Instead, you'll find that these are desktops and laptops that integrate the CPU and GPU on a single piece of silicon and pair them with a unified memory architecture, allowing the CPU, NPU, and GPU to access the same pool of high-bandwidth memory. Nvidia calls it a "superchip," Apple calls it "Fusion architecture," but the best shorthand I've found so far is to call it a "super SoC" to distinguish it from less-powerful CPUs with integrated graphics. In each case, the basic template is the same: A powerful CPU, graphics cores equivalent to those of a discrete GPU, and shared memory that eliminates basic VRAM constraints.
This category also includes high-end desktops with multi-GPU setups, but configuring such systems with enough VRAM can be very expensive, since commercial graphics cards max out at 16GB to 24GB of VRAM, which requires four or five GPUs in a single system to handle larger models. A 125-billion parameter model compressed to 4-bit precision requires at least 96GB of empty RAM to let the entire model load into memory, so anything less prevents a machine from even running the largest local AI models. And the most capable, cutting-edge models? They all require that larger memory pool.
We're defining Class 3 systems as models with 96GB of unified memory or an aggregate of discrete VRAM, and hardware optimized for AI acceleration. Class 3 systems were initially sold just to AI developers, who need a machine for local AI inference. But as AI agents gain popularity and new uses for this level of hardware and capability emerge, these AI-optimized machines are already on store shelves and will make up a growing portion of the laptop market.
These three Class definitions might seem clear-cut, but we're already seeing new systems that blur the lines between categories in pretty significant ways.
The biggest example is Nvidia's RTX Spark laptops, which have AI-ready "Grace Blackwell" hardware and can be outfitted with up to 128GB of unified memory. They run everything necessary for Class 3 testing. But these laptops are also sold in configurations with 24GB, 32GB, and 64GB memory options. The processing power is broadly similar across the RTX Spark family, but the memory pool in sub-128GB configurations is simply too small to support the high-end testing we apply to Class 3 systems.
That issue isn't limited to the new RTX Spark laptops, either. Nvidia's DGX Spark, the developer desktop that essentially defined Class 3 systems, is now available in a 64GB variant. (The original DGX Spark came with 128GB, which lands it firmly in our Class 3.) And we expect to see similar memory configurations in AMD's new 400-series Ryzen AI boxes, as AI-driven demand for memory keeps DRAM prices unusually high. We also see the same thing on Apple MacBook Pro systems, which can be configured with 128GB, but are also offered with 48GB and 64GB of unified memory.
What we define as Class 3 isn't a ceiling on local AI power, but more of a baseline as this category of AI PC gains momentum. Expect more powerful AI-optimized machines from now on as AI power becomes a staple for professional PC shoppers. As I've said already, things are changing rapidly, and our testing will certainly evolve as the technology advances in baby steps and sometimes in leaps.
Our AI Test Suite: MLPerf Client and LM Studio
With three very different levels of hardware capability, as well as different hardware platforms, different operating systems, and different runtimes, no single test is sufficient for measuring AI performance. So we developed a set of tests for these three categories that scale to match the performance levels in each.
Our AI testing is done with MLPerf Client and LMStudio. MLPerf Client is a standard benchmarking tool that uses specific models to test and measure local AI performance. We use it to test Class 1 and Class 2 systems, in concert with different models and subtests, depending on what the hardware supports. We use LMStudio to run a custom test script with a larger model that works only on Class 3 systems with larger memory allotments.
These three tests are targeted at each of the three system classes, tailored to the hardware and capabilities that define them. Class 1 tests serve as a universal baseline that we can run on any AI-capable system, while Class 2 and 3 tests scale up to the most advanced capabilities you can buy today. Let's examine each in more detail.
Class 1 Testing: AI Baseline Capability
We run Class 1 tests on every AI system we test, including standard laptops with basic NPU hardware. We test them with MLPerf Client version 1.6.1, using Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, an 8-billion-parameter, hardware-optimized, 4-bit integer, quantized model that requires at least 16GB of unified memory or system RAM to run. This version of the test has broad support across hardware platforms (AMD's and Intel's x86, Qualcomm's and Nvidia's Arm, and Apple's M-series) and operating systems (Windows, macOS, and Linux).
The test uses a base prompt set that covers five categories: content generation, creative writing, summarization (at both light and moderate levels), and code analysis. The test runs a warm-up and three timed iterations, reporting an average of the performance across those three runs. The final score consists of the geometric mean of Time to First Token (TTFT, in seconds) and Tokens Per Second (TPS) across the five base prompt categories. I'll discuss these metrics in more detail below.
Class 2 Testing: On-Device Inference
Our Class 2 tests better measure the AI chops of more powerful systems with discrete GPUs and larger VRAM or unified memory. We stick with MLPerf Client, adding Phi 4 Reasoning 14B, a 14-billion-parameter model that requires at least 32GB of system memory, and the raw compute, flexible compiler support, and larger memory bandwidth of a discrete GPU or an equivalent high-bandwidth SoC. An NPU won't cut it, even if the system has more memory.
These tests not only use a larger model that Class 1 systems can't run, but also add an extended workload that includes larger summarization tasks and test KV cache scaling (the math-based scratchpad that allows larger context windows), all of which require more memory and memory bandwidth.
However, some newer systems (RTX Spark systems, in particular) don't work well with MLPerf 1.6.1. In these cases, we use the equivalent tests—the same core tests, using the same models, with the same parameters—in MLPerf 2.0.1, which has newer driver and model support for brand-new devices like RTX Spark PCs running on Arm64. These are the same Llama 3.1 8B Instruct and Phi 4 Reasoning 14B tests described above, so the results are comparable.
Class 3 Testing: Long-Context Workflows
Finally, Class 3 testing is reserved for top-tier systems that pair high inference power with at least 96GB of unified memory or VRAM to run substantially larger AI models. For these systems, we use LMStudio's workflow benchmark, tested with Qwen 3.5 122B A10B using Q4_K_M quantization. While lower-tier Class 1 and 2 systems are tested using an 8- or 14-billion-parameter model (parameters are the weights and biases embedded in the model), this is a massive 122-billion-parameter model that uses a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture, which boosts both broad knowledge and computational throughput for higher-quality responses.
As I've already pointed out, the 96GB minimum requirement for Class 3 testing means that many AI-focused PC lines will have models that fall into either Class 2 or Class 3, depending on the configuration we're testing. But that's fine, because we also run Class 1 and Class 2 tests on Class 3 systems, so we always have some comparable test numbers.
The Metrics That Matter: How We Evaluate AI
Across all of our tests, we look at two primary metrics: TTFT and TPS.
In simple terms, TTFT is the response time, tracking the delay before an AI model begins responding to a prompt. There's usually a longer wait for that first token than for subsequent tokens, because this prefill inference stage is compute-bound, and it directly affects whether an AI's response feels immediate or laggy.
TPS, meanwhile, tracks the token-generation rate for the rest of the response. If TTFT is how long it takes for the model to start "typing" a response, then TPS is the typing speed once it gets going. This measures the "decode" phase of inference, which is mostly limited by a system's memory bandwidth.
The benchmark workloads in these tests aren't short back-and-forth chat responses, but structured, real-world productivity tasks. Class 1 tests handle content generation, creative writing, light summarization (1,000-token inputs condensed to 128-token responses), moderate summarization (1,600-token inputs condensed to 256-token responses), and code analysis. (Here, tokens comprise the prompt and reference information a user provides.) Class 2 tests push a system harder, with more in-depth code parsing and summarization of larger text inputs (4,000- and 8,000-token inputs, condensed into 128-token responses).
The most complex are the Class 3 tests, which repeat long-context prompts at different lengths, roughly 8,000 to 64,000 input tokens tested within an approximately-65,000-token context window, which is the largest number of tokens that a model can keep in working memory. Assuming roughly 100 tokens equals 75 words, that prompt translates to approximately 6,000 to 48,000 words. We still look at response time (TTFT) and TPS for Class 3, but we break them up by prompt length to get a clearer view of how workload size affects the results.
These metrics have limitations, of course. They're easy to measure, but they don't address the quality of the response or the accuracy of the information, just the speed of token output. And there's actually an inverse relationship between speed and quality: You can speed up a model by sacrificing quality and accuracy, since both are computationally expensive. We try to eliminate as much ambiguity as we can by sticking to well-established tests with standardized models, but measuring AI performance with pure numbers tells only part of the story.
What’s Next: Performance-per-Watt, Agentic Workflows, and Image Generation
Eventually, we intend to incorporate other tests, such as measuring system-level power draw with a power analyzer and matching that power data to specific performance measurements to calculate performance per watt. This is an especially important metric for anyone using AI in a business setting, where managing energy use and costs is of strategic importance. We're still getting the equipment and processes for that testing squared away, since it requires more than a simple outlet voltage meter.
Because local AI involves more than just text prompts, we're also investigating the new image-generation and agentic tools testing capabilities in the latest version of MLPerf Client. And, in an effort to share real-world experiences rather than just test numbers, we're also refining how we handle testing new capabilities, such as setting up local agents, using agents to handle routine tasks, and trying out systems that can perform high-quality multi-modal media generation and workflows across different apps. But we also want these tests to give us the same level of repeatable, measurable output across different chips, operating systems, and runtimes, so we haven't committed to any additional tests for image generation, agentic tools, or other use cases just yet.
Which brings us back to something I said at the start: Today's testing is just the beginning, and the AI landscape is changing fast. We're keeping a close eye on other tests and evaluation tools, and you can expect new tests to flesh out this current baseline as the technology continues to advance.
