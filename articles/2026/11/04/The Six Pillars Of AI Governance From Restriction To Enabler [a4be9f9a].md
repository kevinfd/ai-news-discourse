# The Six Pillars Of AI Governance: From Restriction To Enabler

Forbes · Nino Letteriello, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-04

Nino Letteriello is a data and project management leader, DAMA Award winner, WEF author, UN advisor, MIT lecturer & FIT Group founder.
What many of you are sensing is really happening: Employees are moving faster than their organizations when it comes to AI adoption. This is an unexpected scenario, radically different from what we witnessed more than 20 years ago with the advent of big data. Things are changing rapidly, and organizations aren’t ready. AI governance is therefore becoming a necessity rather than an opportunity, making it urgent for every company to implement it as soon as possible. Without further ado, here’s how I believe AI governance should be structured in any organization.
Six AI Governance Pillars
1. Knowledge: Understand the data and the AI already in use.
Effective governance starts with visibility. Organizations need to understand what data they hold, where it comes from, how reliable and relevant it is and how it’s classified from a security perspective. They must also identify the AI tools and services already being used across the business, including informal or unauthorized applications sometimes described as “shadow AI.”
Without this baseline, leaders can’t assess exposure, set meaningful priorities or determine where AI can create value. An inventory of data and AI use is therefore the starting point for informed decisions.
2. Security: Protect information through appropriate access controls.
AI systems can make organizational knowledge easier to retrieve and use, but this also makes access management essential. Not every employee should be able to access every document, data source or business process through an AI assistant.
Governance must translate security requirements into practical controls, including permissions, access rights and safeguards for sensitive information. The objective isn’t to block innovation indiscriminately but to protect the organization’s information assets while enabling appropriate and secure uses of AI.
3. AI Literacy: Build awareness and responsible-use skills.
Policies and technical safeguards are insufficient if people don’t understand how AI works or how to use it appropriately. Employees need to recognize both the opportunities and the limitations of AI, understand the risks of sharing information with external services and know how to evaluate generated outputs.
AI literacy should go beyond compliance training: It should help people make informed choices in everyday work. Clear guidance, practical examples and role-specific training can reduce avoidable risks while increasing confidence and supporting responsible adoption.
4. Accountability: Keep human judgment and responsibility central.
AI can generate recommendations, support analysis and automate tasks, but responsibility for decisions must remain clearly assigned. Organizations should define who can approve an AI use case, who validates its outputs and who is accountable for decisions influenced by the system.
Human oversight is particularly important when outputs may affect people, business-critical processes or significant decisions. The level of review should reflect the context and potential consequences. AI should strengthen human judgment, not become an excuse for leaving responsibility undefined.
5. Audits And Monitoring: Treat governance as an ongoing process.
AI governance can’t be treated as a one-off project. Models, data, business needs and risks change over time, and systems that perform adequately today may produce unreliable or unsuitable results tomorrow.
Organizations need ongoing monitoring, periodic reviews and clear mechanisms for identifying and addressing problems. These activities should assess whether AI systems remain reliable, appropriate and aligned with their intended purposes. Lessons from data governance are directly relevant: Effective control depends on continuous measurement, review and improvement rather than policies that are written once and then forgotten.
6. Technology: Make architecture choices part of governance.
Technology decisions shape the security, cost, compliance, scalability and flexibility of AI adoption. Organizations must evaluate available platforms, commercial solutions, locally deployed systems and alternative architectures in light of their needs and risk profile. The right choice depends on the data involved, the intended use, access requirements and operational constraints.
Technology shouldn’t be selected in isolation or treated as a purely technical matter; it must support the governance model and the organization’s wider objectives. Sound architectural choices create a foundation for innovation that can scale responsibly.
Conclusion
Once these six pillars are understood and put into practice, an organization can develop an AI policy grounded in its actual operating environment. That policy should set out clear principles, responsibilities and good practices, including expectations for ethical use. Starting with a policy alone, without first understanding the data, tools, skills, controls and technical landscape, risks producing rules that are difficult to apply or enforce.
The six pillars are mutually reinforcing. Knowledge makes risks visible, security protects information, literacy helps people act responsibly, accountability keeps decisions answerable to humans, monitoring supports continuous improvement and technology provides the foundations on which AI operates. Together, they turn governance from a restrictive compliance exercise into an enabler of organizational learning, process improvement and innovation.
Data management and data governance professionals can play a central role in this transition because they already understand that technology delivers lasting value only when it’s supported by sound practices, clear responsibilities and a holistic view of the organization.
