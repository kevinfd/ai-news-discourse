# Nvidia shaken by scandal amid wider political new order for big tech

The Guardian · Blake Montgomery · 2026-11-10

Today in tech, we’re talking about Nvidia’s salacious scandal, tech tycoons trying to reconcile with Democrats, and what billionaires should spend their money on if not elections.
Cracks appear in Nvidia’s dominance
On the opening night of Web Summit in Lisbon, the Nvidia CEO, Jensen Huang’s admission that he had an affair with a Chinese datacenter executive suspected to be a spy for Beijing hit the main stage. The news broke less than 24 hours prior, and the reaction was surprising.
Matthew Prince, CEO of Cloudflare, broadened the implications of the scandal from Huang’s personal life to Nvidia’s market dominance.
“Whatever Jensen did in his personal life, that’s very salacious. In the longer term, whether Nvidia is the hero or the villain, we’ll be asking: Were the things they did to cement their market position … just financial engineering?” Prince said in front of tens of thousands of onlookers.
Nvidia’s essential role in the AI boom as the world’s premier chipmaker had given it a Teflon reputation in Silicon Valley. It served as both a bank – providing loans to its own buyers to purchase its chips – and hardware provider. Prince credited Huang for doing “a lot to cement the AI ecosystem”.
Likewise asked to respond to the news, Richard Socher, CEO of the four-month-old AI startup Recursive Superintelligence, simply said he was “grateful to have Jensen in my corner”.
Prince responded immediately: “That’s captured interest!” After all, Nvidia participated in Recursive’s $500m investment round, announced in April. The chipmaker has faced criticism over its circular financing deals, but the superiority of its hardware has for many years allowed it to make strong alliances within Silicon Valley that have warded off lasting damage.
US intelligence agencies are already scrutinizing Huang. Judging by Prince’s remarks, Silicon Valley may soon do the same.
Jensen Huang, family man; Nvidia, family business.
My colleague Johana Bhuiyan analyzes Huang’s confession and what it might mean for one of the world’s most valuable companies, which is bound up with his family to an unusual degree for Silicon Valley:
For more than 30 years, Jensen Huang has been the seemingly unshakable public face of Nvidia. He co-founded the company as a young engineer in 1993 and oversaw its growth into a multitrillion dollar chipmaker and bellwether of the AI industry at large.
He’s also made it a family business. His children, Madison and Spencer, have taken leadership roles at the firm. While not a company executive, his wife, Lori Mills Huang, leads the family foundation which, as of 2024, controls over 60m Nvidia shares worth billions. The two married some 40 years ago. During public appearances, he’s fond of telling the story of how they met in college.
But Huang’s image as a family man has been shattered as of Sunday. He admitted in a letter to employees on Sunday that he had been having an affair. Nvidia’s board of directors have now launched an independent investigation about the national security concerns surrounding Huang’s relationship.
“Throughout my life, my family has given me love and support,” Huang wrote in his letter. “They deserve my honesty and loyalty. Instead, I let them down and damaged the life we built together. I take full responsibility for what I did and the hurt I caused, and I accept the criticism that comes with it.”
On one hand, Huang’s affair is not just a matter of morals or ethics, as there are potential national security concerns. On the other, Huang is core to the DNA of Nvidia, which in turn is the bedrock of the entire AI industry – an industry upon which the US economy relies greatly. Huang himself may be too big to fail.
Investors are doubting what was once seen as an unstoppable juggernaut. Nvidia shares fell 7.5% as soon as the stock market opened, wiping over $330bn off the company’s value.
Tech barons attempt reconciliation after blue wave. Will newly powerful Democrats accept?
In the wake of Tuesday’s blue wave, Silicon Valley is attempting to reconcile with the new power brokers in the US.
Tech’s tycoons spent the last two years cozying up to Donald Trump. On Thursday, Elon Musk sang a different tune. After railing against Texas Democrat James Talarico with jibes like “He’ll trans your kids” and spending tens of millions to elect his opponent, Musk responded kindly when the senator-elect called him a “great innovator” on a podcast. Musk tweeted: “Nice of him to say that”. How cordial! It’s a far cry from the extreme and unrelenting partisanship that Musk has hawked for months leading up to the elections.
Venture capitalist Chamath Palihapitiya, host of the influential All-In podcast, tweeted on Sunday: “When I’m wrong, I’m wrong … This was a complete and total repudiation about the state of the economy. The economy is broken.” His message sounds much closer to affordability-obsessed Democrats.
Democratic lawmakers are putting out statements that any reconciliation by the tech oligarchy remains largely one-sided and transactional. Newly in-control Democrats do seem to differ in their views on the best strategy for dealing with the conservative problem of Silicon Valley. Members of Congress are eager to haul in tech CEOs for grillings and pass AI safety laws. Newly elected governors campaigned on promises of moratoria on datacenter construction.
It is not clear which is the best path forward for Democrats as they assume power. The US stock market relies heavily on the AI industry’s growth. Hamstringing that industry with bans on new datacenters and legislation that cracks down on AI model development risks sending the economy into a backslide. However, the voting public wants to make a public example of AI CEOs, who are despised on both sides of the aisle despite their alliance with Trump and the Republican elite.
