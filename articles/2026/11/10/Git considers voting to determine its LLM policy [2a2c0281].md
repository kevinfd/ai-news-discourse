# Git considers voting to determine its LLM policy

LWN.net · adunk · 2026-11-10

Git considers voting to determine its LLM policy
[LWN subscriber-only content]
One of the dominant themes of 2026 has been the debate within development projects about the terms under which LLM-written contributions should be accepted — if, indeed, they should be accepted at all. The Git project has not been immune to this trend; the topic was discussed at the recent Git Contributor's Summit. In this case, the path to a resolution may be somewhat circuitous; the project, which has never been governed as a democracy, is considering adopting a voting process as a way of bringing the discussion to an acceptable conclusion.
Like most projects, the Git community has been considering its policy toward LLM-written contributions for a while. In October 2025, the project updated its SubmittingPatches document to include this policy:
	The Developer's Certificate of Origin requires contributors to
	certify that they know the origin of their contributions to the
	project and that they have the right to submit it under the
	project's license. It's not yet clear that this can be legally
	satisfied when submitting significant amount of content that has
	been generated by AI tools.
Another issue with AI generated content is that AIs still often hallucinate or just produce bad code, commit messages, documentation or output, even when you point out their mistakes.
To avoid these issues, we will reject anything that looks AI generated, that sounds overly formal or bloated, that looks like AI slop, that looks good on the surface but makes no sense, or that senders don't understand or cannot explain.
This policy was reconsidered at the Contributors Summit, but without any clear conclusion. Some participants seem to be open to relaxing the policy, while others raised the ongoing copyright concerns or worries that accepting LLM-written contributions will erode some people's trust in the project. About the only consensus that, seemingly, was reached was that it would not be possible to come to a consensus on the topic.
The idea of putting the question to a vote was raised in that same session, and Emily Shaffer agreed to take on the task of creating a process within which such a vote could be held. A draft version of that process was posted for consideration on October 21.
The voting proposal seems to be somewhat influenced by Debian's process, but it is not exactly the same. When the time comes to vote on a topic (the determination of that time is left to the Git maintainer), proposals are to be formatted as patches to the project's documentation. Each proposal would be posted and discussed individually, with the intent of polishing it, rather than transforming it into something substantially different. Anybody who is eligible to vote (eligibility is discussed later) can put forward a proposal; any given proposal will only be added to the ballot if it is seconded by another eligible voter.
The discussion period is expected to last "
around 4 weeks
" as the
proposals are reworked into their final form.  At the end of that period,
the proposals will be collected into a ballot, and voting will take place
over a two-week period.  The proposal says nothing about how the votes will
be counted — whether the proposal with the most direct votes wins, or
whether some sort of ranked-choice process will be used.
Eligibility to vote, as described in the proposal, would be:
	Votes are welcome from contributors who have authored a commit
	which has been merged to next, or who have provided 2 or more code
	reviews, and who have not been banned from participation in the
	project.
As was pointed out in the ensuing discussion, that was a rather more
inclusive set of criteria than was discussed at the Summit, where a
requirement of at least 50 commits in the Git history was tossed out.
There was no real agreement on what the correct threshold should be;
Patrick Steinhardt suggested
that a commit count of 25 or 30 might make sense, Jeff King proposed
a more complex rule that gives some weight to longer-term contributors, and
brian m. carlson just wanted a
threshold "
where people are not incentivized to contribute only to
vote
".  Fortunately, nobody suggested setting up a process to vote on
that question.
Kristoffer Haugsbakk claimed that the vote process would lead to a foreordained conclusion for the issue at hand:
	The vote will pass. Those who work on Git at least once a month
	work for tech companies. Those tech companies are now AI
	companies. The votes will happen to drift towards agreement with
	their management.
That led to objections from developers like Steinhardt and carlson, who insisted that they would vote based on their own conclusions rather than those of their employers. They both agreed, though, that the voting process should be secret so that employers could not force their developers to vote a certain way.
The conversation has slowed down without any firm conclusions.  There are
numerous
open questions still, including the eligibility rules and the system
that would be used to count the votes.  For the latter, Debian's devotee
and the Condorcet Internet Voting
Service (which is used for elections to the Linux Foundation Technical
Advisory Board) have been proposed.  The Git project is considering the
addition of a fundamental piece of governance policy, which is not
something to be done lightly, so a conclusion may be some time in coming.
The voting process is meant to help in situations where a consensus seems
unlikely to emerge, but that process itself almost needs a consensus behind
it before it can be adopted.
