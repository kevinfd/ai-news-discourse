# How AI Is Raising The Bar For SaaS Companies

Forbes · Ed Frederici, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-10

Ed Frederici is Chief Technology Officer at Appfire, a leading enterprise collaboration software company.
AI has revived the debate about whether software as a service (SaaS) can maintain its market position. But the better question is whether SaaS companies can meet the higher bar customers are setting for differentiation, outcomes and value.
Pressure on public SaaS valuations and questions about AI-driven disruption are pushing organizations to prove their value faster. According to a McKinsey survey from August 2026, nearly nine in 10 organizations use AI in at least one business function, though many are still experimenting or piloting rather than scaling it across the enterprise. 
Together, these shifts are pressuring SaaS companies from multiple directions. Externally, buyers have more options and higher expectations. Internally, companies must operationalize AI responsibly, rethink how roles evolve and manage the cost of adding AI to products.
These pressures are connected, but buyers feel market changes first. They have more products to evaluate, more AI claims to vet and less patience for software that fails to prove its value. 
Competition is increasing, and differentiation matters more.
That is why competition comes first. With the ability to generate code becoming cheaper, AI lowers barriers to entry for SaaS products. Differentiation therefore becomes harder and more important. In a crowded market, buyers have less patience for products that are difficult to buy, onboard, use or maintain.
The strongest SaaS companies will stand out by understanding customer workflows and removing friction. That means solving specific problems more effectively than competitors, embedding governance, risk and compliance (GRC) and business outcomes directly into workflows and making the product experience simple enough to drive adoption.
Competition can signal healthy demand, but the challenge is proving why your product deserves to win. That requires quality, compliance, flexibility and a product experience that fits naturally into how people work. 
Adoption is still uneven across organizations.
Winning customers is only the first step. As AI becomes part of more SaaS products, companies also have to help customers adopt those capabilities with confidence.
Even as AI tools become more available, adoption is moving at different speeds. According to a 2025 survey by DeskPro, the tech sector is seeing a 92% adoption rate in using AI for support operations, while adoption in regulated sectors sits at 58% as they face different risk considerations, workflows and trust requirements. When it comes to scaling AI across an organization, you’re only as fast as your slowest team. 
That uneven adoption makes scaling difficult. Organizations cannot rely on isolated AI experiments if they want AI to become part of everyday work. They need clear use cases, governance, data standards and a shared understanding of where AI should and should not be used.
Without that foundation, AI remains fragmented instead of becoming an enterprise capability.
While hands-on experience often drives adoption faster than policy, organizations still need guardrails. As AI becomes core business infrastructure, leaders must hold it to the same expectations for accuracy, security, performance and reliability as any critical system. Those expectations also reshape the features organizations need from the people building and managing AI-enabled products.
Roles are evolving as AI takes on execution.
As AI increasingly handles operational work such as coding, testing, documentation and analysis, people remain central, though their roles are shifting.
Software engineers will need strong judgment in review, architecture, security and system-level work. If AI writes more implementation, humans need to evaluate whether the solution is safe, scalable, maintainable and aligned with organizational standards. AI-generated code should still be reviewed with the same rigor as other contributions.
Entry-level roles may change as AI takes on basic tasks, and new roles will emerge to manage AI across the business. The chief AI officer is one example, but it only helps if the mandate is clear. A leadership seat created for optics can overlap with the CTO or CIO and slow progress.
The role needs a defined problem to own, clear authority and measurable ROI. The same expectation applies to AI investments. As organizations expand AI across products and operations, SaaS providers must find ways to help leaders demonstrate that their investments deliver measurable value.
AI investment requires discipline.
AI can create an advantage, but also introduce cost. That creates several financial pressures:
• Balancing Innovation With Cost Control: AI infrastructure, model usage and compute costs can increase the cost of goods sold and put pressure on margins.
• Prioritizing High-Impact Use Cases: Not every AI feature will drive meaningful differentiation. Companies should focus on areas that deliver clear customer and business value
• Aligning Pricing With Delivered Value: Customers may pay for AI-enabled capabilities, but only when the value is clear.
• Maintaining Visibility Into ROI: Leaders need to track which investments deliver results, and then adjust when they do not.
• Avoiding Overextension: If an AI feature increases operating costs without improving the product experience or business outcome, it becomes a margin problem.
The investment-return gap was already clear in 2025 when 85% of organizations had increased AI investment over the prior year, according to Deloitte, and 91% planned to increase it again. However, only 6% reported payback in under a year. 
For SaaS developers looking to help organizations navigate these headwinds, the best starting point is to choose the highest-value use cases, estimate costs up front and connect the investment to customer or business value.
Some AI features will drive differentiation. Others will add cost without changing the customer outcome. SaaS products should give leaders visibility into which is which.
SaaS is not dead. The bar is higher.
AI is making software easier to build, but harder to differentiate. Success will depend less on who ships the most AI features to who solves customer problems most effectively, earning trust across the enterprise and delivering measurable business value.
SaaS is not disappearing. The bar for winning has simply risen.
