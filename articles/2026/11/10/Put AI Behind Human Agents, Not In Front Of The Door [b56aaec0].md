# Put AI Behind Human Agents, Not In Front Of The Door

Forbes · Damini Sood · 2026-11-10

Damini Sood, VP - Sales and Marketing, KBS International, enhances small business competitiveness through supply chain and tech innovations.
In my last article, I argued that tech support teams need a 911 mindset: You should respond fast, prioritize by business impact and connect frontline teams with engineering in real time. The question now is: Where does AI fit?
Companies are turning to chatbots to cut support costs. But I believe AI is being deployed in the wrong place. A bot can close a ticket; it can’t build the trust that keeps customers through the next outage and the next renewal.
Where Chatbots Break Down
Imagine a store owner whose credit card terminal freezes at 5 p.m. on a Friday. With a line at the register, they need a solution, not a chatbot repeating fixes they’ve already tried.
Yet many companies measure AI success by containment rate—how many customers never reach a human. But abandoned conversations aren’t resolved problems; they’re lost trust and potential business.
I’m not against automation. I’m against using AI as a gatekeeper. AI belongs behind human agents, not in front of the door.
What A Bot Can’t Build
Trust in a vendor is built when someone answers during a crisis, understands a customer’s setup and delivers on their promises. A bot can be polite and accurate, but it can’t identify the bigger concern, make judgment calls or take ownership.
Customers may forgive an outage when a person owns the problem, but many likely won’t forget being trapped in a chatbot loop. Every interaction can build trust or erode it.
The question isn’t whether to use AI, but whether it empowers your support team or puts distance between them and your customers.
Taking The 911 Model A Step Further
In my last piece, I looked at how 911 dispatchers respond. Here I want to look at the tools behind them. When a 911 call comes in, the dispatch system has often already put the caller’s location, number and call history on the screen. The system does the lookup. The person focuses on what to do.
Tech support can work the same way. I call it the “rule of twos”: It should take a customer two clicks to ask for help from inside the product. Then, you should have a two-step identity check that runs in the background before the call connects.
When a specialist picks up, the screen already shows who the customer is as well as their plan, their device and software version, their network status and recent error logs.
Checking identity manually often takes two to three minutes per call. Doing it in the background saves that time. It also makes it harder for scammers to talk their way in. And the agent starts the call with facts, not a blank screen.
The 2-2-2-15 Standard
I measure each call against what I call the “2-2-2-15 standard”: It should take customers two clicks to ask for help. Then, you have your two-step background check. It should take two minutes to reach a trained person. Altogether, it should take 15 minutes from first click to a fix.
Fifteen minutes sounds unrealistic for hard problems. But look at where the time goes. Take a typical 18-minute call: In my experience, only four or five minutes usually go to the actual fix. The rest goes to checking identity, digging through different systems, waiting for a manager to approve something and writing notes.
AI working behind the human agent can cut most of that through:
• Diagnosis: Today an agent might check signal strength in one tool, system status in another and payment logs in a third. AI can pull them together in seconds and point to the two or three most likely causes.
• The Rules: AI can bring up the policies that apply, like payment dispute or privacy rules, and the right fix steps for that setup.
• The Notes: When the call ends, AI can summarize it, log the steps, tag the root cause and update the case. The human agent can checks the notes instead of writing them. Those tagged causes also feed the incident reviews I recommended in my previous article. Patterns show up without anyone digging through old tickets.
If you remove the waiting and the paperwork, your diagnosis time can drop by half. The person on the call still makes the decisions. They just get there faster.
The time saved matters as much as the speed. A human agent who isn’t jumping between screens can pay attention to the customer. They can explain what went wrong in plain words. They can mention what to watch for next week. AI clears the clutter. The person gets room for the part of the call that builds trust.
From Connected To Empowered
In my last article, I wrote that frontline agents need diagnostic tools and a direct line to engineering. The next step is letting them act on what they find.
In most support setups, even a well-connected agent must wait for someone else to approve the fix. In a model built on emergency response, the person who answers can fix it. If a payment batch was flagged by mistake, they release it. If a device has failed, they ship a new one right away.
That power has limits. Emergency dispatchers follow strict rules, and support agents should too. AI can watch transactions as they happen. It can flag anything unusual or over a set dollar amount. Those cases need a second sign-off from the lead on duty. Decisions happen at the front line. The checks run in the background.
This matters for the relationship too. “I’ve released payment” builds more trust than “I’ve opened a ticket.”
Where To Start
Almost every company has a chatbot now. That makes good human support stand out more than ever.
The 911 mindset tells your team how to respond. Putting AI behind human agents gives them the tools to do it at scale. The relationship stays with people.
If you’re deciding where to start, look at your own calls. Find out how much time your team spends on identity checks, lookups and notes. Point the AI at those. Leave the conversation to people.
Customers don’t stay loyal to software. They stay loyal to the people who showed up when it counted.
