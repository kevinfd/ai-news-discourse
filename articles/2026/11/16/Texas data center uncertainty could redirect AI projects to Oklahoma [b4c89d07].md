# Texas data center uncertainty could redirect AI projects to Oklahoma

Finance & Commerce · Reuters · 2026-11-16

Texas data center uncertainty could redirect AI projects to Oklahoma
Oklahoma could attract AI data center developers as Texas weighs restrictions on new projects, potentially redirecting billions in infrastructure investment. The shift could create development opportunities for Oklahoma while raising questions about power capacity, water resources, zoning and local infrastructure.
The Blueprint
- Texas Gov.-elect Gina Hinojosa has proposed a moratorium on new data center projects pending legislation.
- Oklahoma Gov.-elect Mike Mazzei supports data centers that meet requirements for industrial zoning, private power and closed-loop cooling.
- Texas has 821 data centers, with 126 more planned; Oklahoma has 85, with 10 planned.
- An estimated $7 trillion in global data center infrastructure investment is projected by 2030, according to McKinsey.
A simmering policy debate in Texas regarding the rapid expansion of AI data centers could soon influence development decisions north of the Red River, as Oklahoma leaders weigh the economic opportunities and infrastructure demands of the energy-intensive industry.
The uncertainty stems from proposals in Texas to slow the pace of construction of facilities that support the booming AI industry. Texas Gov.-elect Gina Hinojosa campaigned on enacting a statewide moratorium on new data center projects until the Texas Legislature convenes next year and passes legislation granting local communities greater control over where and how the facilities are built.
That proposal follows a step already taken by outgoing Texas Gov. Greg Abbott, who paused state permits for new data centers to allow state agencies time to audit pending projects and study their strain on the state’s electric grid operated by the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT). The Public Utility Commission of Texas set a Dec. 10 deadline for regulators to verify the viability of large-load power projects seeking grid connections.
There’s been speculation about Texas permitting resuming after the state review wraps up in December, but Hinojosa’s upcoming term has raised fresh questions about the timeline for new development. Some experts say the uncertainty could result in tech companies and infrastructure developers looking at neighboring states like Oklahoma, where the incoming governor has expressed support for data center development provided projects meet certain criteria.
Michael Webber, energy policy researcher at the University of Texas, told NBC’s Dallas-Fort Worth affiliate NBC 5 that even the prospect of delays can shift corporate strategies.
“I think that a moratorium or the threat of a moratorium is enough to get the developers to consider other states,” Webber told the broadcast news station. “They are in a race and they want speed to power, and one of the reasons why they like Texas is because they can move quickly here.”
Oklahoma as an alternative to Texas?
Oklahoma has multiple advantages that are attractive to hyperscalers like Amazon and Google, including cheap and abundant energy, as well as a light regulatory environment. Oklahoma Gov.-elect Mike Mazzei has stated he has no issue with data centers locating in the state as long as they don’t use taxpayer funding and develop on land zoned industrial.
Additionally, he said they should use closed-loop water cooling systems to protect local water supplies and generate their own electricity. Oklahoma passed a bill permitting behind-the-meter generation in 2025. The measure had an emergency clause and went into effect two months after its passage in July 2025.
Cleanview’s tracker shows 821 data centers on 263 campuses in Texas, with 126 more planned. The tool shows Oklahoma has 85 data centers on 17 campuses, with 10 more planned.
If plans are put on hold due to a moratorium in the Lone Star State, Hart Brown, president of AI and Transformation at Saxum in Oklahoma City, said some of those plans could cross the Red River where there’s more favorable business conditions.
“Oklahoma has very similar advantages to Texas, both geographically as well as from an infrastructure perspective,” Brown said, adding that a transition to Oklahoma is a “very natural move for a lot of these companies.”
The scale of capital involved in national data center construction, particularly for hyperscalers, is substantial. A McKinsey analysis on U.S. hyperscale data centers projects $7 trillion in global data center infrastructure capital expenditure by 2030, with more than 40% of that concentrated here in the U.S.
Brown estimates the U.S. is currently only about 13% of the way through its long-term AI infrastructure buildout. He said about $3.5 trillion has already been allocated nationwide, with another $3.5 trillion or more likely required for future construction and equipment. He also said roughly one-third of that total planned capacity had been slated for Texas.
Local resistance, grid demand and Oklahoma’s strategy
An influx of projects into Oklahoma is no guarantee, however, and developers face a different logistical situation with respect to the grid. Unlike Texas, which operates its own largely isolated power grid, Oklahoma is part of the multi-state Southwest Power Pool. Securing connections through regional transmission queues can involve extensive engineering reviews and multi-year delays.
To bypass grid backlogs and satisfy state power expectations, Brown said developers of data centers are increasingly considering onsite, behind-the-meter power generation, which is one of the requirements Mazzei mentioned during his campaign.
“Most of the larger enterprises have fully recognized the grid is going to take time to adapt to these new types of facilities, so they will generate their own electricity,” Brown said. “That’s something that all of these data center companies have absolutely agreed to and understand that dynamic.”
Meanwhile, local sentiment presents another significant consideration. Throughout the U.S., data center proposals have drawn bipartisan scrutiny from residents concerned about high water consumption, heavy electrical loads, noise and the conversion of rural land.
Debates regarding these issues are unfolding across Oklahoma, too, where active moratoriums on data center construction are in place in Oklahoma City, Tulsa, Edmond and Norman, as well as in multiple tribal nations, as local governments assess zoning and utility impacts. It’s even led to Brian Pillmore, the former mayor of Yukon, being ousted from the office after he supported a proposed data center in the town.
Brown estimates about 25% of Oklahoma communities are geographically receptive to hosting data center developments, while 15%-20% are firmly opposed. He also said roughly 50%-60% of communities remain mixed or undecided as residents seek more information regarding quality of life and local utility impacts.
But rather than these issues being settled entirely at the Capitol, Brown said local governance will play the decisive role.
“It really becomes a matter of community by community,” Brown said, adding that some municipalities view the facilities as a means to expand their tax base and modernize infrastructure without raising resident taxes, while others remain cautious or skeptical.
Taking a look at the political fallout in Texas, Brown said the Lone Star State over-indexed on data centers in the permitting process.
“When you look overall at what the nation needs potentially, we’re looking at maybe 120 gigawatts overall of AI capacity. Texas was most recently sitting on more than 540 gigawatts of permits and so the scale of that caused Abbott to create a moratorium. … Now we have Governor-elect Hinojosa that really campaigned on that issue,” Brown said.
He said that’s a lesson on data center development for other states, and certainly for Oklahoma. He posed some considerations regarding the state’s overall approach.
“How many of these facilities, and what do we think is viable for the state of Oklahoma overall, and then within the communities themselves that want those facilities and potentially benefit, if it makes sense to them overall. But having that strategy is incredibly important,” Brown said.
