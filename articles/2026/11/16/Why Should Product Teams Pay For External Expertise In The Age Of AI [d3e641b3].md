# Why Should Product Teams Pay For External Expertise In The Age Of AI?

Forbes · Polina Chebanova, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-16

Polina is co-founder of Phenomenon Studio and product strategist. Building and scaling digital products with $500М+ raised collectively.
”Why is it so expensive? I put together a prototype in Claude Code in 20 minutes.” Teams developing digital products are hearing this phrase more and more often. And we’re no exception. If a founder or product manager can build a prototype themselves, and an AI developer can write more code in the same amount of time, why hire an external team? And what exactly are they being paid for?
Yet the outsourced development market is growing: Mordor Intelligence estimates it will reach $618 billion in 2026, up from $564 billion the year before. The key change is this: Companies that previously saw external teams as a way to save money are now hiring them to gain access to expertise they lack in-house.
In my view, product studios and agencies whose value continues to lie solely in writing code to a brief will become a slower and more expensive version of AI. Others will be hired as partners who can help make better product decisions.
What Changes When Everyone Can Do More
Recently, our head of design used Claude Code to create a dashboard for tracking invoices on the studio’s internal SaaS platform. It took him 80 hours, 16 of which were spent auditing the code. He did this using Claude as well, as he did when fixing the errors he found. He then handed the code to the front-end developers for review. They received a working draft that simply needed to be brought up to production standards—a draft written by a designer who had never done any programming before.
A couple of years ago, we wouldn’t even have considered such a scenario. Today, our designers create their own apps during design sprints to practice working with AI. One of them built an app for tracking car expenses and published it on the App Store.
According to Figma’s 2026 AI report, “The number of designers participating in development doubled to 41%, and the number of developers doing design work rose from 44% to 60%.”
AI has expanded the scope of almost every role—both in in-house teams and in agencies. We can all do more, faster. Until recently, during the sales pitch, we would show the client a portfolio and explain how we could solve their problem. Today, we arrive with a clickable prototype of the solution.
Production scales with the speed of the model, but the ability to identify exactly what needs to be created doesn’t. This is the partner’s role.
What It Means To Work With A Partner
A contractor receives a brief and is responsible for delivering it to a high standard. A partner can look at it and say: “There are a few issues here; let’s discuss them.”
This doesn’t mean they’ll argue endlessly with the client. People within the company understand well why the business needs a product or a new feature but may not know the day-to-day workflow of the people who’ll be using them.
An external team may not know the industry as deeply as the client, but they’ve seen dozens of products lose users in the same places, such as during screen transitions or in the absence of a use case that seemed rare. And their first task is to check whether they’re solving the right problem.
The “superpowers” we’ve gained from AI have made this verification even more essential. It creates a temptation to accept the first convincing result as the right solution, but the ability to quickly create or discover something doesn’t equate to a better understanding of the problem.
Katie Dill, head of design at Stripe, advises asking yourself: Would I be satisfied with this result if I’d spent a week on it?
This is a good way to distinguish a strong solution from a result that’s simply impressive for how fast it was achieved. That’s why soft skills such as asking the right questions and disagreeing in a reasoned manner are enjoying a renaissance. Previously, a significant portion of a team’s time was spent on technical implementation, but as this has accelerated, there’s now more room for productive dialogue.
When A Business Needs A Partner
Not every project requires a partnership. If a company knows what it’s building and for whom, and the decision has already been made, all that’s required of an external team is high-quality delivery and adherence to deadlines. The situation is different when there’s a business objective, but the path to achieving it hasn’t yet been defined.
We usually know which scenario applies by the first meeting. The first thing we ask is why the company needs this project. Those ready for a partnership start talking about their business: what isn’t working and where they want to go.
If the answer boils down to, “We need an app; just work out the costs,” the decisions have already been made, and the company simply needs a contractor. Next, we gauge their reaction when we say, “Perhaps a different solution is needed here.”
Sometimes, even at the sales stage, we mention what we would change in the technical brief. If we’re dealing with a partner, they’ll take an interest. In such projects, the dialogue must involve someone who can make decisions. Otherwise, the team is formally invited for their expertise but in reality is left with nothing more than the right to carry out the brief.
If an external team is involved in choosing the solution, they can no longer say, “We simply followed the brief.” In a partnership-based model of collaboration, the team also takes responsibility for the consequences: A failed project damages both parties’ reputations.
