# I went to a math class at Caltech. The professor no longer grades homework because everyone's using AI.

Business Insider · Truman Dickerson, Truman Dickerson (tdickerson@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-16

I went to a math class at Caltech, where the specter of AI loomed large. Truman Dickerson I attended an intro math class at Caltech, an elite STEM university in Pasadena. I didn't understand the material, but I did understand that AI was looming large. The professor, Yi Ni, said he no longer grades homework because students use AI to do it. Professor Yi Ni had no laptop or projector. He instead rapidly filled eight blackboards with chalk scrawl during an introductory math class at Caltech on Friday. Though the instruction was entirely analog, the specter of AI loomed large. Ni, a professor at Caltech for 17 years, told me that he no longer grades homework on accuracy because students are using AI to complete it. Now, he grades it only on completion. "There is a problem in the education process," Ni said. "Virtually all the homework in college math, you can use AI." Chatbots, once derided for their inability to perform such basic tasks as counting the r's in "strawberry," can now do math as well as a graduate student. And if you're an undergraduate student, that's kind of a big deal. Some, Business Insider has reported, are reconsidering whether to study math at all . From my perch near the back of the lecture hall on Friday, however, I didn't see any students prompting ChatGPT. In many ways, the class resembled a typical early morning university lecture (for undergrads, 10 a.m. is basically the crack of dawn). Sweatshirted students arrived late and filtered in the upper rows, while the overachievers sat in the front and asked questions. Many bent their heads over large iPads and took furious notes while Yi filled up blackboard after blackboard with formulas and practice problems. Some had no note-taking materials to speak of. A couple closed their eyes for extended periods. The class is a "core requirement" in Caltech's curriculum, meaning everyone at the school has to take it. Students I spoke to afterward gave a variety of majors, but none said they were comfortable speaking on the record. So later on I spoke with Sathvik Redrouthu, a sophomore who'd taken the class last year. Redrouthu, a math major , said he didn't use AI because he wanted to understand the concepts. "I was interested in the class," he said, adding that mainlining the homework with AI is like "shooting yourself in the foot" for when an in-person exam comes around. "I personally think that if you leave AI out, then it pushes the average student to learn properly," he said. If you're not interested in math, though, Redrouthu said you'd probably turn to AI — and you'd get away with it, until the exam at least. Ni told me that, as a professor, he can't tell whether a problem set he assigns is completed using AI. It's unlike AI-generated writing, which is usually a pretty obvious amalgamation of short sentences, em dashes, and hypophora. But math is all numbers: the specialty of AI programs . Caltech has no unified guidance for how math professors should plan around AI now. Redrouthu said that this year, some of his classes come with an auspicious caveat: "AI allowed."
