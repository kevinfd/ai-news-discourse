# The symmetry of the universe may have broken in the first seconds of time

Scientific American · Anne-Katherine Burns · 2026-11-16

The subatomic particles that make up our world are roughly 100,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 times lighter than the average human. At this unfathomably small size, the universe behaves remarkably diﬀerently than it does on the human scale. The mathematical theory that describes the behavior of these particles is called quantum field theory, or QFT for short. QFT was created by combining quantum mechanics with Einstein’s special theory of relativity and was built on the idea that the particles that make up the universe have a tendency toward order and symmetry.
In a recent study, my collaborator and I asked: What would happen if the universe, in its infancy, did not obey the symmetries embedded in quantum field theory? What if particles in the early universe operated under diﬀerent laws of physics than they do today?
One of the most fundamental of these symmetries is that between matter and antimatter. According to QFT, particles and their corresponding antiparticles, such as electrons and positrons, must have exactly equal masses. Scientists have long assumed that this symmetry holds for all particles and has been respected over the entire course of the universe’s 13.8-billion-year history.

Although it may sound trivial on the surface, symmetry between particles and antiparticles comes from three deep underlying assumptions about the laws of physics. One of these assumptions is that no matter which direction you’re facing in space or how fast you’re moving, the laws of physics will be the same. Whether you’re standing on Earth or in a rocket ship heading toward Alpha Centauri, the laws that govern the universe should be equal.
The second of these assumptions is that in order to communicate from one location—say, Topeka, Kan.—to another location—say, Barcelona—something (a phone signal, a mail carrier, a messenger pigeon) must physically travel from Topeka to Barcelona. For phone signals, this happens so fast that it can feel instantaneous, but in reality there is a physical signal traveling back and forth. Information cannot be transmitted without some delay.
The third assumption is that you cannot have less-than-zero energy. Once a state has reached its lowest possible energy, you cannot extract any more out of it, no matter how hard you try.
These assumptions may seem obvious, but without them, our universe would look very, very diﬀerent.
In our study, my collaborator and I called these assumptions into question. What if these symmetries were broken at the very beginning of the universe? For instance, what might the consequences be if the masses of the electron and positron were not exactly equal in the first few minutes after the big bang?
To tackle these big questions, we investigated the first event in the history of the universe for which physicists have direct evidence: big bang nucleosynthesis. This is the name for the process that formed the light elements such as hydrogen, helium and lithium in the first few minutes after the cosmos was born. During the time that these elements were forming, the entire universe was composed of a hot and dense plasma of particles like the inside of the sun. If the masses of the electrons and positrons that helped make up this plasma were diﬀerent, then different amounts of each element might have formed from this plasma.
Astronomers have amazingly precise observations of the amounts of each of these elements in the universe today. For instance, we’ve measured that 24.6 percent of the mass produced during big bang nucleosynthesis was helium 4.
Using a simulation of big bang nucleosynthesis, we determined the amount of each element that we would expect to be produced if the masses of electrons and positrons were unequal and compared that to the actual observation.
We found something striking: the masses of the electron and positron do not have to be exactly equal for our theoretical predictions to match the observed amounts of light elements in the universe. This means that it’s possible that the symmetry between matter and antimatter was violated in the early universe and that the laws of particle physics in the early universe may have been diﬀerent than they are today.
Our specific model of symmetry violation has other potentially important implications as well. While it was not the focus of our study, we found that this type of symmetry violation could also explain the fact that there is so much more matter in the universe than antimatter.
In addition, it could also be a key to solving one of cosmology’s greatest modern mysteries: the fact that diﬀerent measurement methods give us very diﬀerent values for the expansion rate of the universe—a problem known as the Hubble tension.
All of this tells us something important: the laws of physics may have been profoundly diﬀerent in the early universe and could change again in the far future. If that were true, it would mean that the rules that we think of as eternal and unchanging are actually alive and evolving over time. Although we don’t have definitive proof that this is correct, the fact that it is possible is remarkable in itself. The universe of the deep past and distant future may not be younger and older versions of our own but something entirely foreign to us, governed by laws we are only just beginning to understand.
