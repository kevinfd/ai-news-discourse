# Why Your AI Tooling Isn't AI Governance

Forbes · Jim Tunnessen, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-16

Jim Tunnessen, Founder & CEO of GOVERNBOX.ai. 2x Former Federal CIO, D.ENG AI/ML candidate, Principle Researcher at Gradient Descent LLC.
Across major industry gatherings, from privacy and risk forums to enterprise technology summits, a persistent misconception has emerged: enterprise leaders are conflating basic AI monitoring with comprehensive AI governance.
There is a difference between strict regulatory mandates governing artificial intelligence (AI) and the telemetry tools software vendors market under the banner of “AI Governance.” For the C-suite, understanding this distinction is crucial for enterprise risk management and regulatory compliance.
The Illusion Of “Little g” Governance
The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF), which underpins much of global AI regulatory discourse, is structured around four core functions: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage.
Many enterprise software platforms market “AI Governance” capabilities that merely discover or monitor AI assets across a network. While discovery and visibility across an enterprise IT environment are necessary foundational steps, this represents only “little g” governance. Passive observation falls well short of active policy enforcement and risk mitigation.
Monitoring tools confirm that an AI system exists and is operating within basic technical thresholds, but they do not provide the defensible documentation or operational accountability required by regulators or boards. Most important, vendor software does not assume legal or regulatory liability. When an autonomous system hallucinates, leaks proprietary data, violates intellectual property rights or produces biased decisions in hiring or credit, accountability rests entirely with the deploying enterprise.
Counting on network monitoring to fulfill regulatory duties is like inspecting a single server rack and declaring that you operate a fully compliant, secure data center. Real oversight takes a structured program with clear ownership.
The Cost Of The Compliance Gap
The consequences of this gap are accelerating alongside global enforcement. The European Union’s AI Act, which began phased enforcement in August 2026, imposes stringent requirements on high-risk AI deployments, mandating risk management systems, high-quality training data governance, continuous logging and human-in-the-loop validation. Simultaneously, U.S. regulatory bodies like the FTC and SEC are scrutinizing automated decision-making and deceptive AI claims, while individual states are enforcing their own ADMT laws auditable by the states’ Attorneys General. California’s and Colorado’s laws take effect January 1, 2027.
Meeting these mandates is critical. Telemetry tools can flag an anomaly, but they cannot assess whether a model’s training corpus violated user consent agreements, nor can they determine whether an autonomous agent’s actions align with corporate fiduciary duties.
Moving Toward “Big G” AI Governance
To build a governance framework that holds up to an audit, leaders need to go beyond monitoring and put a real program in place. I recommend focusing on four areas:
- Executive Ownership And Charter: Make one C-level executive accountable for AI governance, such as a chief risk officer or chief AI officer. That person should enforce companywide policies on how AI may be used and lead a governance council that brings together legal, technical and business leaders.
- Immutable AI Inventory: Keep a single, up-to-date register of every AI system in use across the organization, from in-house models to third-party tools and autonomous agents.
- Algorithmic Impact Assessments And Risk Registers: Conduct structured impact evaluations detailing data lineage, bias testing, cybersecurity resilience and mandatory human-in-the-loop intervention protocols, tied directly to an enterprise risk register.
- Board-Level Reporting & Metrics: Track and report core operational metrics to the board, including total AI exposure, vendor compliance attestations, workforce training completion rates and quantified deployment risk profiles.
True “Big G” AI governance is a companywide program with clear accountability and evidence that will stand up to an audit. It draws on the data that “little g” monitoring tools collect and turns it into policies and decisions leaders can act on. That’s how an organization shows regulators and its board that people, not machines, remain in control of its growing AI portfolio.
