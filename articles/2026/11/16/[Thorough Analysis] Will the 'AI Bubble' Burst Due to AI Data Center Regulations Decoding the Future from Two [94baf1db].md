# [Thorough Analysis] Will the 'AI Bubble' Burst Due to AI Data Center Regulations? Decoding the Future from Two Market Shocks and CoreWeave's Earnings

[Thorough Analysis] Will the 'AI Bubble' Burst Due to AI Data Center Regulations? Decoding the Future from Two Market Shocks and CoreWeave's Earnings
In the autumn of 2026, a series of events poured cold water on AI-related stocks, which had been the driving force of the stock market. These were the 'CoreWeave Shock' that occurred in October and the 'Midterm Election Shock' that took place immediately after the U.S. midterm elections in November.
Behind the two major plunges in just about a month lies a common question held by market participants: 'Can the massive investment in AI data centers really be sustained going forward?'
In this article, we will thoroughly dissect the three headwinds facing data center construction and the latest earnings of the high-profile AI cloud giant CoreWeave, providing an easy-to-understand explanation of the reality of an AI bubble burst and the strategies investors should take.
The 'Three Headwinds' Hindering AI Data Center Investment and the Impact of the U.S. Midterm Elections
The October 'CoreWeave Shock' and the November 'Midterm Election Shock'
Recent market turmoil was triggered by two symbolic downturns.
The first is the 'CoreWeave Shock' that occurred in October 2026. Credit concerns emerged regarding U.S.-based CoreWeave, which provides AI-specialized cloud infrastructure (so-called neo-cloud), questioning whether it could repay its massive debt. As the company's corporate bond yields soared, its stock price plummeted by 15% in a single day, and the repercussions spread to the semiconductor index (down about 4%) and Nvidia (down about 3%). In the Japanese market the following day, the Nikkei Stock Average plunged by 3,318 yen, with Advantest down 15% and Lasertec down 13%, recording declines comparable to the epicenter, CoreWeave.
The second is the 'Midterm Election Shock' that occurred after the U.S. midterm elections on November 3rd. Immediately after the Democratic Party regained a majority in the House of Representatives, the market initially reacted positively, thinking that 'excessive fiscal spending would be curbed by a divided government.' However, immediately after that, it was disliked that 'Democratic candidates who take a stance of opposition or caution toward data center construction' were elected one after another in gubernatorial and local elections across various regions, causing the market to reverse and plunge. The U.S. tech stock index fell by over 6% in a single day, and the semiconductor index fell by as much as 12%, marking the largest decline since the COVID shock.
Both shocks can be said to be the result of the market becoming aware of the risk of a slowdown in data center investment, which is the foundation of AI infrastructure. Currently, there are broadly 'three deceleration factors' at work in data center construction.
Headwind 1: Regulatory Risk of 'Construction Permit Suspension' Emerging in Gubernatorial Elections
The first headwind is construction regulation by politics and administration. The Democratic Party, which will take the lead in the House of Representatives starting next January, is expected to introduce a regulatory bill to curb the soaring electricity costs caused by data centers. However, since the President has veto power at the federal level, the hurdle for passing the law is considered high.
A more serious impact comes from permits and approvals at the state and local government level. This is because approval from local governments, such as permission to connect to the total power grid and environmental impact assessments, is essential for data center construction.
Of particular impact is Texas, the largest hub, which accounts for about 20-30% of the data center capacity planned within the U.S. In that state, there was already a move to tighten grid connection reviews since the summer even under a Republican governor, but in this election, a Democratic governor who pledged to 'suspend all new permits until the state legislature develops laws' was elected. Furthermore, in Georgia, a new governor has also called for suspensions or conditional regulations, and the Public Service Commission, which determines electricity rates, has also seen the Democratic Party take the lead for the first time in 30 years.
Combining the five states where the Democratic Party won the governor's post this time, it reaches about 40% of future data center construction planned capacity. Although there is no impact on facilities already in operation, a strong headwind is beginning to blow against new construction plans.
Headwind 2: Soaring Electricity Costs Nationwide and Bipartisan 'Cost Burden' Debates
The second headwind is the tightening of power infrastructure and rising electricity costs. While the movement to stop construction itself is centered on Democratic governors, the issue of 'who will bear the costs of rapidly increasing electricity bills and power grid maintenance' has become a common issue across party lines.
In September 2026, even in the Republican-majority House of Representatives, a bill was passed by an overwhelming majority to encourage state authorities to consider having large-scale power consumers bear the costs of transmission equipment (the Senate Democrats stopped it in the direction of making it stricter, saying 'it should be mandatory, not just a consideration'). There is deep-seated opposition across the U.S. to the fact that as a result of attracting data centers, electricity bills for unrelated local residents are also jumping.
Looking at commercial electricity rates in August, while the national average only rose 4.3% year-on-year, it rose by more than 20% in Georgia, where data centers are concentrated, and by more than 10% for industrial use in Texas. Furthermore, in the power grid in the eastern United States, the capacity market price for securing power generation capacity has jumped more than 10 times in four years, and the limits of infrastructure are being realized.
Headwind 3: The Sharp Rise in 'Procurement Interest Rates' Demanded by the Market
The third headwind is the rise in 'procurement interest rates' determined by the financial market. In addition to the high level of government bond yields, the credit spread (risk premium) added when data center-related companies issue corporate bonds is expanding rapidly.
The aforementioned CoreWeave corporate bonds were sold off to a level requiring a yield exceeding 15% in the market. This is equivalent to approximately twice the average for corporate bonds with the same rating. While the interest rates on already issued bonds will not change, the company will be unable to raise funds without paying extremely high interest rates when borrowing or issuing new bonds in the future.
Furthermore, if regulations intensify, stating that "data center operators should bear the costs of electricity and power grid infrastructure themselves," it is pointed out that there is a risk of falling into a negative spiral where the profitability of operators deteriorates, leading to further declines in creditworthiness and increases in borrowing costs.
The Truth Behind CoreWeave's Financial Results and the Seeds of a 'Second Shock'
Revenue Up 2.7x but Still a Net Loss? The Burden of Interest and Electricity Costs Revealed in Financial Results
Amidst this growing market caution, CoreWeave's latest financial results, announced on the morning of November 11, highlighted both the light and shadow of AI infrastructure companies.
CoreWeave adopts a business model where it leases the data center buildings themselves while owning core equipment such as GPUs and power generation facilities to rent out computing power. The top-line figures in the announced results were very strong, with revenue surging approximately 2.7 times (nearly triple) year-on-year, and operating profit, which indicates the profitability of the core business, secured a surplus of approximately $200 million. Following these results, the company's stock price has also recovered slightly for the time being.
However, the bottom line fell into a net loss of approximately $700 million. The biggest factor is the ballooning interest expense. The company's borrowings have surged from $35.1 billion to $46.9 billion in the last three months, and the company's own outlook suggests that future interest expenses will further expand to the $1.05 billion to $1.15 billion range. While the company explains that "the growth in operating profit will exceed the increase in interest," the weight of the interest burden is clear.
Also, in terms of costs, the increase in rent and electricity costs cannot be overlooked. Although it is difficult to see the exact amount from the outside because electricity charges are sometimes added to rent as common service fees, the financial documents clearly state that "electricity costs are expected to continue to fluctuate and be subject to inflationary pressure." If electricity costs expand more than expected in the future, there is a risk that the growth of operating profit will slow down.
Refinancing Within One Year and Customer Concentration—Scenarios for Resurging Credit Anxiety
So, can a sudden drop like the "second CoreWeave shock" happen again in the future? There are two major risks to be wary of.
The first is the "risk of refinancing borrowings." Of the $46.9 billion in borrowings, $10.6 billion will reach its repayment deadline within one year. If the company is forced to refinance in the current high-interest-rate environment of over 15%, the interest burden will become even heavier, and credit anxiety could resurface.
The second is the "risk of demand slowdown due to customer concentration." Neo-cloud's main sales destinations are concentrated on hyperscalers such as Microsoft. It has already been reported that Microsoft plans to narrow down its external cloud suppliers, and if sales and operating profit do not grow as expected, there is a concern that the scenario for repaying the massive debt will collapse.
In addition, it is necessary to be careful about the possibility that similar credit anxiety will spread to the market, triggered by the trends of other mid-sized neo-cloud companies that rely on borrowing, as well as companies like Oracle, not just CoreWeave.
Conclusion: Will the AI Bubble Collapse? Indicators Investors Should Watch and Defensive Measures
Regulations Alone Won't Stop It, but the 'Boiling Frog' Risk Is Steadily Rising
In conclusion, it is considered unlikely that "AI data center regulations will trigger a complete halt in construction and immediately cause the AI bubble to collapse."
The reasons for this are that data center construction has economic effects that bring significant revenue to local businesses, and environmental and power issues are not fatal risks like a nuclear power plant accident, but are ultimately "problems that can be solved with money." It is highly likely that the moves by governors who have called for a suspension of new permits will ultimately settle on a landing point with conditions such as "operators must bear appropriate power grid infrastructure costs and electricity costs."
However, optimism is forbidden. The costs of responding to construction regulations, rising electricity bills, high procurement interest rates, and expensive GPUs and memory—all costs to operate a data center are piling up like a snowball.
While this shock does not mean an immediate end to the AI market, as long as profitability is deteriorating due to rising costs, the reality is that "the probability of the AI bubble collapsing is steadily increasing." To avoid falling into a "boiling frog" state where it is too late by the time you notice, a stance of calmly assessing changes in the cost structure is required.
Hyperscaler Investment Plans and Portfolio Diversification Strategies
To forecast the future of the AI market, investors should focus on the following four indicators.
- Specific outcomes of construction regulations and permitting at the state level
- Rules regarding the burden of electricity costs and transmission infrastructure expenses for data center operators
- Trends in corporate bond yields and credit spreads (cost of capital)
- Capital expenditure plans of major hyperscalers (Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta)
In particular, the fourth indicator—the capital expenditure plans of hyperscalers—is the most critical metric that dictates data center demand itself. If these plans are revised downward, the AI market could cool down rapidly.
It is also important to note that even if you do not hold individual AI-related stocks, you may still be significantly affected indirectly through index investing. Approximately 30% of the S&P 500, nearly half of the Nasdaq 100, and even about 20% of the All Country World Index (ACWI) are influenced by AI-related companies and hyperscalers, primarily among their top holdings.
If you feel your portfolio is too heavily weighted toward the technology sector, it may be an effective defensive strategy to incorporate sector ETFs such as healthcare or utilities to diversify your risk, as these are less susceptible to interest rate fluctuations and economic cycles.
