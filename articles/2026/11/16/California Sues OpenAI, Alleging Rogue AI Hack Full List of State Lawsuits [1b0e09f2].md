# California Sues OpenAI, Alleging Rogue AI Hack: Full List of State Lawsuits

Newsweek · Steve Mollman · 2026-11-16

California has sued OpenAI, alleging a rogue AI cyberattack while joining a growing number of states taking the ChatGPT maker to court over a range of safety concerns.
The lawsuit, filed Monday by California Attorney General Rob Bonta, differs from earlier cases brought by other states that largely accuse OpenAI of misleading consumers about ChatGPT's safety and exposing children and other vulnerable users to harm.
California instead is targeting the company's development of autonomous AI agents, alleging that inadequate safeguards allowed its technology to escape a controlled testing environment and hack the AI platform Hugging Face.

OpenAI defended its response to the incident in a statement to Newsweek, saying it had strengthened safeguards, disclosed its findings and cooperated with California investigators.
"This lawsuit appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that's been identified by every major AI lab," OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri said.
OpenAI faces legal challenges on two fronts: the potential harm its chatbots cause to users and the risks posed by autonomous AI systems capable of taking actions without direct human supervision.
The lawsuits also reflect growing efforts by state attorneys general from both major political parties to use existing consumer protection laws to regulate artificial intelligence, even as lawmakers debate new federal safeguards.
Why Is California Suing OpenAI?
Bonta filed the lawsuit in San Francisco Superior Court on Monday, alleging that OpenAI endangered public safety by developing and testing advanced AI models without adequate safeguards.
The complaint follows a July incident in which autonomous AI agents developed by OpenAI escaped their testing environment and gained unauthorized access to Hugging Face, a platform widely used by AI developers.
According to the complaint, approximately 700 autonomous AI agents attacked Hugging Face after escaping their testing environments, carrying out roughly 17,600 attacker actions during a cyberattack lasting about 2 1/2 days.
California alleges that the incident was foreseeable and preventable. Its lawsuit accuses OpenAI of violating the state's Unfair Competition Law and creating a public nuisance.
Although Hugging Face was the target of the alleged cyberattack, California's lawsuit focuses on the broader risks that OpenAI's testing practices allegedly posed to the public.
"The Hugging Face attack was the predictable result of an internal culture at OpenAI that prioritizes growth and rapid model development over safety and accountability — and this was only one of 80 known incidents," Bonta said in a statement shared with Newsweek by his office on Monday.
The complaint describes the 80 incidents as instances in which OpenAI lost control of autonomous agents during testing, rather than 80 attacks comparable to the Hugging Face breach.
The complaint also alleges that OpenAI agents compromised the company's own infrastructure, obtaining administrator-level access to critical systems, including services used to deploy models and manage sensitive credentials.
California accused OpenAI of failing to cooperate fully with its investigation.
According to the complaint, OpenAI initially responded to a September 30 investigative subpoena with a 14-page letter containing information it had already disclosed publicly, without producing supporting documents.
The company later provided additional materials, including redacted communications about previously undisclosed incidents, but failed to supply detailed information about other incidents and breaches of its own systems, the state alleges.
OpenAI disputed that characterization, saying it had provided multiple technical briefings and responses to investigators' questions.
"We take misalignment seriously, and we have engaged—and will continue to engage—constructively with the Attorney General's office to share information and advance strong, practical safety standards for the entire industry," OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri told Newsweek on Monday.
Pusateri said the company had published technical and independent reports, notified affected organizations, strengthened safeguards and developed a framework for identifying and disclosing further incidents.
OpenAI said it had notified more than 100 organizations as of September 26 about potentially concerning activity by its AI models, although it stressed that notification did not necessarily mean a security breach or exposure of private information.
The company said it was reviewing approximately 50 petabytes of data to identify incidents and had strengthened security controls, restricted internet access and expanded monitoring.
In a September 30 update, OpenAI said it had not identified another incident comparable in scale or severity to the Hugging Face attack, although its investigation was continuing.
California is seeking court-ordered changes to OpenAI's practices, along with restitution, disgorgement of gains and civil penalties of $2,500 for each proven violation of the state's Unfair Competition Law.
The complaint does not specify a total monetary amount or prescribe a detailed set of technical safeguards.
Which Other States Are Suing OpenAI?
California's lawsuit follows a series of legal challenges brought by other states in 2026.
Florida — June 1
Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier sued OpenAI and CEO Sam Altman on June 1, alleging that the company marketed ChatGPT as safe despite knowing about risks to users, including children.
The complaint accuses OpenAI of misleading consumers, disregarding safety warnings and failing to provide adequate safeguards against harmful chatbot responses.
In September, Florida asked a judge to prevent OpenAI from developing new AI models without outside oversight. That request remained pending as of November 12, according to Reuters.
Arizona, Arkansas, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska and West Virginia — October 14
Eight states filed separate but coordinated lawsuits accusing OpenAI of misleading consumers about ChatGPT's safety.
The bipartisan group of attorneys general alleged that ChatGPT could reinforce delusions, encourage self-harm and provide dangerous advice while presenting itself as a trustworthy companion.
The lawsuits also accuse OpenAI of prioritizing user engagement and commercial growth over safety.
Iowa Attorney General Brenna Bird announced the coordinated action alongside officials from Kansas, Nebraska and West Virginia. Arizona, Arkansas, Idaho and Montana also filed cases in their respective state courts.
The states are seeking financial penalties and court-ordered changes to OpenAI's practices, including restrictions on developing advanced self-improving AI models without sufficient safeguards.
Texas — October 23
Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton sued OpenAI, affiliated entities and Altman, alleging that the company misrepresented ChatGPT's safety and failed to protect consumers from harmful outputs.
The lawsuit claims that OpenAI exposed users, including minors, to risks involving self-harm, violence, misleading information and excessive emotional reliance on AI chatbots.
Texas alleges violations of its Deceptive Trade Practices–Consumer Protection Act.
What Is Hugging Face, the AI Platform at the Center of the Hack?
Hugging Face is an AI technology company best known for operating a platform where developers and researchers share machine learning models, data sets and tools.
Its platform has become a widely used resource for building and testing artificial intelligence systems, including open-source models.
The company was targeted in July when OpenAI's autonomous AI agents gained unauthorized access to parts of its infrastructure after escaping their testing environments.
According to the California complaint, the agents coordinated their activities despite safeguards intended to keep them isolated.
The incident raised concerns about whether AI systems capable of independently using software tools and accessing external networks could cause harm beyond the environments in which they are tested.
Sam Altman Is Named in Only 2 of the OpenAI Lawsuits
Altman is personally named as a defendant in only the lawsuits brought by Florida and Texas.
Those cases allege that OpenAI misrepresented the safety of ChatGPT and failed to adequately protect users from harmful interactions.
The eight coordinated lawsuits filed in October target OpenAI corporate entities rather than Altman personally.
California's new lawsuit names OpenAI Foundation and OpenAI Group PBC as defendants, rather than Altman.
How Is California's OpenAI Lawsuit Different?
Unlike the earlier state lawsuits, California's case focuses on the actions of autonomous AI agents rather than primarily on ChatGPT's interactions with consumers.
Autonomous agents can perform sequences of tasks, use digital tools and interact with external systems with limited human intervention.
California alleges that OpenAI failed to adequately control those capabilities, allowing its agents to access computer systems without authorization.
The case follows an investigation launched by Bonta's office in September and an investigative subpoena issued later that month.
Alabama's attorney general also subpoenaed OpenAI over the Hugging Face incident, while Delaware has reportedly sought information about the company's safety practices and governance.
Those investigations are separate from the lawsuits filed by state attorneys general.
OpenAI also faces private litigation in California. In September, the nonprofit Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology sued the company, seeking restrictions on AI agents accessing third-party systems without authorization.
A separate coalition of nonprofit groups sued OpenAI and several other AI developers this month, seeking to halt the development of advanced AI models over alleged public safety risks.
Neither case was brought by a state attorney general.
What Happens Next in California's OpenAI Lawsuit?
California is seeking a court order requiring OpenAI to address what the state describes as unsafe AI development practices, along with financial penalties and other relief.
The case could help establish how existing consumer protection laws apply to autonomous AI systems that act beyond their intended controls.
The lawsuit also comes as California considers additional AI safety requirements. Under a September executive order, Governor Gavin Newsom directed state officials to submit recommendations by Monday on measures including independent audits, emergency shutdown mechanisms and expanded reporting of serious AI incidents.
