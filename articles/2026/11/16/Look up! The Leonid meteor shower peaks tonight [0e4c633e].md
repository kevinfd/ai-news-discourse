# Look up! The Leonid meteor shower peaks tonight

Scientific American · Mary Randolph · 2026-11-16

The Leonid meteor shower will hit its peak on Monday night and into early Tuesday, when it will brighten the sky with fireballs and colorful shooting stars.
The Leonids come from Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle. When the Earth crosses through Tempel-Tuttle’s trail of debris each year, the leftover space rocks can enter our atmosphere and fall as bright and sometimes blue and green meteors.
The height of this year’s show is expected to start around midnight tonight and to last until dawn, with around three meteors per hour in the darkest places. Stargazers on the East Coast are in luck this year because the shower will peak on a clear night—but with a nearly half-full moon, some of the fainter meteors may be difficult to spot.

Though the shower will see relatively few meteors per hour, the Leonids can lead to especially great stargazing because of their tendency to produce fireballs. These larger pieces of comet debris are brilliant, and they are more vivid and last longer than a typical shooting star. The shower will also include Earth-grazing meteors, which shoot past the horizon and are often particularly vibrant. The Leonids are known for being some of the fastest meteors, at 44 miles per second. Blink, and you might miss them.
Every so often, the Leonids’ parent comet, Tempel-Tuttle, produces so much debris that the shower becomes a meteor storm, marked by more than 1,000 meteors per hour. The last Leonid meteor storm was in 2002; the phenomenon tends to happen around every 33 years.
NASA advises sky-gazers to wear lots of layers, bring a sleeping bag and lie down on the ground with their feet pointed to the east for the best viewing experience. After around 30 minutes, when your eyes adjust, you can catch the bright streaks across the night sky from midnight until dawn.
