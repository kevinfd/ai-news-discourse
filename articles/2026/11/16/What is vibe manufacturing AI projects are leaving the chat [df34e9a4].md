# What is vibe manufacturing? AI projects are leaving the chat

Mashable · Olivia Tauber · 2026-11-16

What is vibe manufacturing? AI projects are leaving the chat
The next wave of AI projects might just arrive in the mail.
As AI tools become more capable, people are asking chatbots to help design things they can hold, wear, or install in their homes, from custom jewelry and household gadgets to metal parts built around precise measurements.
Welcome to "vibe manufacturing." The phrase borrows from "vibe coding," where people describe the software they want and ask AI to help create it. Here, the goal is a physical object, with AI helping develop a design that can be printed at home or sent to a manufacturer.

The appeal is easy to understand. Most people have encountered a product that would be perfect if it were a little smaller, worked differently, or fit that one awkward space. These tools offer a starting point for making their own version. Getting it right, though, still takes more than a good prompt.
What is vibe manufacturing?
Vibe manufacturing loosely describes using conversational AI to help design and build physical things. Someone explains what they want, supplies measurements or reference images, and works with an AI tool to develop it. Depending on the project, that could involve generating a digital model, choosing components, writing software for a device, or figuring out assembly.
The bridge between the conversation and the object is often CAD, short for computer-aided design. CAD software creates precise digital designs for physical objects, and AI tools can help translate everyday requests into the code or geometry behind them.
Entrepreneur Greg Isenberg outlines the process in an Oct. 4 explainer on X. "You describe what you want. An AI turns it into a precise design file. A printer on your desk tests it overnight," he writes.

Tools such as the open-source text-to-cad project support that process, providing workflows for creating models with AI agents, producing engineering drawings, and checking designs for fabrication. The resulting files can go to a printer or an outside shop with the equipment to make the parts.
What are people actually making?
Some of the projects getting attention solve problems that are deeply specific to their creators.
In October, entrepreneur and former X head of product Nikita Bier posted that he'd used AI to design a dog door with a Wi-Fi-controlled lock, customized to his house. Despite having no background in metal fabrication or electrical engineering, he said it was being manufactured for almost the same price as a mass-produced alternative. "We are now entering into an era where any product can be created exactly to a consumer's preferences & needs," he wrote.

Other makers are tackling repairs. In a post on r/ApolloScooters, one Reddit user described using Claude to modify an existing design file after their scooter’s rear fender broke. They ordered a replacement through a 3D-printing service and shared a photo of the result, while noting that they would make the next version shorter.
Around the house, customization can be decorative, too. An employee for AI modeling tool Meshy, Jessica Bergau, used the company's AI tool to design cabinet knobs, then asked Claude to generate a script that added screw threads to the models. A blog post documents her plastic prints and the work needed to get an actual screw to fit.

Some people are taking their AI-generated ideas to professional craftspeople. In an Instagram post, Dubai-based jeweler AV Creations said a client brought in an AI-generated design that its team recreated as a bespoke gold dog tag pendant, complete with geometric engravings, diamond detailing, and a sculptural carved cube.
Others are experimenting with more complex systems. In a post on X, Ruben Kostandyan shared that he'd asked GPT-5.5 to design an industrial mini HVAC system using ForgeCAD.

Greg Isenberg sees a business opportunity in what he calls "the gadgets nobody would build" — products for audiences too small to interest a large manufacturer. He points to accessories for particular bikes or coffee machines, replacement parts for discontinued products, and equipment tailored to an individual business.
"When the design is a file and the factory makes one at a time, the product can fit you instead," he writes.
That vision extends beyond personal projects to businesses serving small markets. Whether those businesses can consistently deliver reliable products at a workable price is a separate question.
What does it take to get an AI design made?

Turning an AI-assisted design into a physical object often relies on existing manufacturing services: SendCutSend lets customers order custom metal parts, Xometry offers processes including machining and 3D printing, and JLCPCB can manufacture and assemble circuit boards. Each requires files specifying what to build.
But access to production equipment doesn’t guarantee a workable design. "A design can look perfect on screen and be impossible to manufacture," Isenberg writes. He recommends checking a manufacturer’s guidelines, testing a physical prototype, and paying a mechanical engineer to review files before ordering.
Dominic Böttger's account of making a case for an air-quality sensor shows why. He used Claude Code to develop the enclosure, but early versions had misplaced supports and an opening wide enough for the circuit board to slide out. Getting it right took seven design revisions and three prints, with photographs helping guide corrections. "The feedback loop needs the physical part," he writes.
Industrial manufacturers face the added challenge of making those results repeatable. Technology and manufacturing organization Automation Alley explores that ambition in its March 2026 Integr8 playbook, Vibe Manufacturing: Where AI Meets Additive. Engineers set requirements such as strength, durability, and cost, then use AI to explore designs for additive manufacturing, the category that includes 3D printing.
"AI explores the options. Additive manufacturing turns those options into physical reality," CEO Tom Kelly writes in the foreword. The report also discusses "Smart Product Recipes," digital instructions intended to standardize production across locations.
As AI tools improve, more people may get the chance to make the products they've been wishing someone would sell. The real test will be how well those ideas hold up once they leave the chat window.
