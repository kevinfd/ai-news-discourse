# No matter how you ask, more Americans are negative than positive about AI

Our World in Data · atlasunshrugged · 2026-11-16

No matter how you ask, more Americans are negative than positive about AI
Five high-quality surveys ask about AI in different ways. Their wording matters, but the overall picture is consistent.
Online, the benefits and risks of artificial intelligence are intensely debated. Yet we all know that the online world is far from representative, and so I wanted to know what Americans more broadly think about it.
But when it comes to surveys, I tend to be cautious. What people report in a survey can shift quite substantially when a question is worded slightly differently or when respondents can choose their answer from different options.
So instead of just relying on one survey, I searched for a range of recent high-quality surveys and put them together in this chart.1
As you can see, the survey’s wording does matter. When a middle answer is offered, many people take it — 37% did in Pew’s recent survey, shown at the top — and a smaller share reports a positive view: in both Pew’s and Gallup’s surveys, only 9% chose it.
What surprised me is how consistent the overall result is. Every survey finds that more Americans have a negative view of AI than a positive one. In Pew’s survey, 52% say they are “more concerned than excited”, and 9% are “more excited than concerned” — a ratio of more than five to one. When Pew first asked this in 2021, the ratio was about two to one.
Few Americans are enthusiastic about AI. When people have strong feelings, they are mostly negative. In the Ipsos survey, 17% said AI is an “extremely bad” thing for society, and just 2% said “extremely good”.2
People’s worries extend to the most extreme risks. In a Quinnipiac poll in September 2026, Americans were asked: “How concerned are you that future AI systems could potentially threaten human survival?” 35% said they are somewhat concerned and 38% said they are very concerned. Most Americans (73%) believe there is a risk that AI could threaten the survival of humanity.3
And concern about AI is not confined to one party. In Pew’s survey, 56% of Democrats and 49% of Republicans are more concerned than excited about AI. And in both parties, only around one in ten are more excited than concerned.
There is no question that many find the prospects of AI exciting. But what this data shows is that for more Americans, the concerns are larger. If the industry wants to earn the public’s trust, it will have to take these widespread concerns much more seriously than it has so far.

Endnotes
- I only included surveys based on probability samples. Quinnipiac called randomly generated landline and cell phone numbers. The other four surveyed members of online panels that were themselves recruited at random, mostly by mail to randomly selected addresses: Pew’s American Trends Panel, the Gallup Panel, the panel used by the Annenberg Public Policy Center, and Ipsos’s KnowledgePanel. All five surveys weight their results to match the US adult population, and publish the full wording of their questions and their results. I did not include any of the many polls based on opt-in online panels, which anyone can sign up to. This method can obviously lead to very biased results (an appalling example is an opt-in poll that found that 20% of Americans under 30 agreed that “the Holocaust is a myth” — when Pew asked the same question on its probability-based panel it found 3%). In 2021, Pew introduced the question with a short definition and referred to “artificial intelligence computer programs” rather than “artificial intelligence (AI)”: “Artificial intelligence computer programs are designed to learn tasks that humans typically do, for instance recognizing speech or pictures. Overall, would you say the increased use of artificial intelligence computer programs in daily life makes you feel…” The answer options were the same, and Pew reports the results as one trend.
