# If a Data Center Moratorium Is Coming, Bill Ackman Just Explained Who Stands to Benefit Most

The Motley Fool · Adam Levy · 2026-11-16

The midterm elections brought the political reality facing the AI industry to the forefront. Many saw the election results as a referendum on AI data center build-outs specifically, as citizens voted for officials who vowed to take a stance and increase regulations on further data center construction. That's on top of specific ballot measures in certain regions of the country to block or restrict further development.
Indeed, AI companies are likely to face increased political pressure going forward, as Democrats control Congress and executive offices in key states. Moratoriums on permitting and construction could be a huge setback for some companies. Other companies, however, are far better positioned to navigate the political environment. Bill Ackman called out exactly who stands to benefit.
Image source: Getty Images.
Who thrives when new data centers get harder to build?
There are many factors that can slow down the data center build-out. The companies best positioned to overcome most of them are the well-capitalized hyperscalers: Amazon (AMZN -0.29%), Microsoft (MSFT +0.17%), Alphabet (GOOG -0.96%) (GOOGL -0.98%), and Meta Platforms (META +2.90%). Not only do these companies have huge cash-generative businesses that can help fund their data center projects, but their good credit and high equity valuations allow them to raise outside capital at relatively low cost.
That said, capital isn't the only constraint in building new data centers, as Ackman points out in his third-quarter letter to Pershing Square shareholders. "In addition to capital, standing up new data center capacity requires permitting, power, chip supply, and the navigation of political considerations," Ackman wrote. Here, Ackman suggests, the hyperscalers also hold an advantage over other companies. "The neoclouds and other secondary participants face meaningful challenges with each of these requirements."
Many neocloud companies are currently benefiting from the compute capacity shortage. In fact, the hyperscalers are among their biggest customers, as they offload some tasks to third-party data centers to dedicate their capacity to tasks they're uniquely capable of fulfilling. However, neocloud companies' continued growth will stem from their ability to build more capacity, not from charging higher prices for existing capacity.
For example, Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX -0.59%), better known as SpaceX, expects significant revenue growth from rapid expansion of its data center capacity in 2027. CEO Elon Musk expects to add 10 gigawatts of capacity next year compared to the 1.7 gigawatts it offered as of the end of the third quarter.

But Ackman finds SpaceX and the various neocloud companies in a much more precarious position, especially in light of the political attitudes this fall. "To the extent those challenges slow the pace at which others can bring capacity online, the current shortage of compute will persist for longer, and the benefit will accrue to those who already have capacity in place and the means to add more," he wrote in his article released Nov. 2, before the election.
In other words, the hyperscalers (which Pershing Square also happens to own stock in) are in a much better position to navigate potential political challenges, and if data center moratoriums slow growth in compute supply, they have more capacity than anyone else to serve their customers. That will likely show up in strong earnings results.
The strength is underappreciated
Despite the strong position the hyperscalers occupy, their stock prices remain relatively low compared with those of neocloud companies and secondary participants. All four trade for forward P/E ratios between 21 and 28. The stocks have been weighed down by concerns that the stepped up capital spending required for the AI build-out won't result in a good return on invested capital and translate into strong revenue growth. That's despite the mounting evidence that the investments are already producing good returns.
Meanwhile, neocloud companies, which rely on easy access to capital and friendly regulators to allow them to build quickly, trade at enterprise value-to-estimated-sales multiples just slightly below the hyperscalers. That's despite the significant risks they face and the heightened uncertainty in their sales outlook. There should be a wider gap between the valuations of neoclouds and hyperscalers.

Not only do neoclouds face the risk of slower growth due to heightened regulations, but their growth is also unsustainable in the long run. As capacity constraints ease, the amount they can charge for commodity-like compute infrastructure will drop considerably. Meanwhile, the hyperscalers, with full platforms for AI development, can maintain premium pricing. That's a far less cyclical business, and one that deserves a premium valuation relative to the commodity providers.
Hyperscalers are better positioned for whatever the environment brings, and their stocks look like great long-term opportunities relative to other AI stocks.
