# Generative AI Usage in the Games Industry

IWGB Game Workers · kingbob000 · 2026-11-16

The following manifesto was written by members of the IWGB Game Workers' Branch in collaboration with members of Game Workers Unite Ireland of the Republic of Ireland, CSVI of Spain, and the FIOM-CGIL Milan Work Council
Introduction
Our Aims
We, a collective of video game unions and work councils from Europe, write this manifesto to raise awareness of the harms of generative AI (genAI) use within the video game development sector.
GenAI is currently harming the games industry, its workers and the games they produce. Left unchecked, this harm will continue to grow and impact more workers, more games, and more players, even more acutely.
Our goal is to bring to light the risks and dangers genAI poses to the workforce, as well as to set guidelines and standards for games industry workers, stakeholders, and regulators, suggesting actions which can be taken to reduce – and ideally eliminate – these dangers.
Scope
This manifesto discusses genAI in the context of the video game development sector. These uses include, but are not limited to:
- The creation of assets included in a released game which are experienced by players – for example: art, sound, code, and dialogue assets.
- The creation of materials which are used as part of the process of development but which players won’t experience when playing the released game – for example: early concept art, reference materials, pre-translations, etc.
By “genAI” we mean a specific type of AI used to generate text, pictures, videos, or music in response to limited inputs (usually just text “prompts”) using LLMs and other energy-intensive models. The wider harms of genAI won't be covered here, but they constitute additional extremely compelling reasons to resist the use of genAI. These include:
- Severe harm to the environment (Zewe, 2025)
- The destruction of communities (Nguyen, 2025)
- The exploitation of the Global South (F.Regilme, 2025)
This document won’t go into detail about how different genAI models work, nor discuss the comparative quality of their output.
The focus of this document is on games industry workers (us!), how we are and will be impacted, and how we can resist.
The Impact of GenAI
GenAI Creates Worse Working Conditions
- GenAI is both directly and indirectly replacing game industry workers.
- Many game workers are being forced to use genAI, either explicitly or implicitly.
- Working with genAI is less desirable, fulfilling, and educational than the same work without genAI.
- Over time, working with genAI leads to worker fatigue, cognitive decline and burnout.
- Using genAI removes a sense of ownership from workers.
- GenAI is causing a marked decline in junior positions within the industry, leading to a lack of new talent.
Proponents of generative AI claim that it is a tool of empowerment for workers. For that to be true, every worker would be free to choose if, when, and how to use it. We would also need to trust that every employer would use the promised productivity improvements to benefit their workers.
In practice, this is not what we are seeing.
In many workplaces today, workers are being forced to use genAI ‘tools’, sometimes in very specific ways (Plunkett, 2025). Some companies do not explicitly impose the usage of genAI, but set expectations which cannot be reached with or without its use. Allowing the choice of usage, while also creating expectations that the worker should be able to keep pace with automated procedures, is no choice at all (Shadmi & Merchant, 2025).
When workers do use genAI, they find work more onerous than before. Many of the tasks related to genAI work are more tedious and less fulfilling, since the work becomes less about exercising the skills workers have built and invested in, and instead pivots to a plethora of small, parallel tasks which amount to supervising genAI and bug-fixing its output.
Workers using genAI have reported ‘AI brain fry’ and reduced focus (Pearl et al., 2025). Although gains to productivity are possible, these tend to be short term. Employees often initially work faster, take on larger tasks, work overtime, and ignore breaks, due to working with genAI feeling 'more like chatting' (Rangantathan & Ye, 2026). However, once this novelty declines, so does productivity, with workers reporting cognitive fatigue, burnout, and inhibited decision making.
A recent study (Shaw & Nave, 2026) showed that participants who used genAI frequently adopted results that were systematically incorrect, especially under time pressure but also in the face of incentives and feedback. The researchers were able to prove that users of genAI habitually delegate their agency and offload decision making to an external party with relative ease. They call this process ‘cognitive surrender’ (Shaw & Nave, 2026).
The use of genAI also removes a sense of ownership from workers, who become alienated from the results of their labour: the things they produce no longer feel like they have the workers’ stamp on them, and therefore they can no longer take pride in them.
GenAI is disproportionately used for work which would traditionally be given to junior workers, and is hence directly reducing the amount of junior positions available within the industry. This is worsening an already existing problem for newcomers to the industry, who are finding it harder than ever to find work, but it is also a loss of potential fresh perspectives, and is staggeringly short-sighted when it comes to the industry’s sustainable future.
GenAI has a profound negative impact on workers by reducing the availability and appeal of work. Games are best made by people, and people do their best when they are healthy and connected to their work, refining their skills and accumulating real experience.
GenAI Harms Relations Between Workers and Employers
- GenAI is sold to employers as an alternative to workers.
- GenAI often increases pressure on workers when used by employers to set policy, timescales, etc.
- GenAI can be used by employers to abdicate responsibility.
GenAI’s promise to employers is to more fully automate processes, to enable them to be completed with fewer workers. This directly reduces the amount of jobs available for game workers. In some cases, workers have even been forced to train the models which then replaced them, leaving them unemployed.
GenAI interfaces are engineered to be human-like and eager-to-please, to prioritise keeping the user engaged, even if engagement comes at the cost of providing them with incorrect information. GenAI’s predisposition towards this sycophancy and lack of real-world context can lead to an increase in unreasonable, damaging requests when used by employers to set policy, timescales, workloads, etc., as well as to task expansion and a general degradation of conditions (Cheng et al., 2026).
In addition, when employers engage with human-like genAI models which never tell them ‘no’, they become less receptive to criticism and pushback from other workers.
GenAI can also obfuscate and confuse employers’ accountability, in two main ways. Firstly, employers may shift their own accountability onto these platforms, blaming an error on the “tool”, rather than accepting responsibility themselves. Secondly, they may blame workers for issues which arise through the use of genAI – which the employers have mandated.
Any time genAI is used without exacting human verification, the possibility of error increases. Exacting human verification takes time, which negates the promise of genAI automation, and therefore many employers are choosing to skip this step, either directly or indirectly by imposing time constraints on workers which make detailed verification impossible. Workers are then blamed for failures in the genAI’s output.
Employers see genAI as a way of cutting labour costs, employing fewer workers than they would need to without it. GenAI companies ingratiate themselves with employers, by promising the impossible. Moreover, mistakes made by genAI are often blamed on the workers, rather than the employers mandating its use.
GenAI Appropriates the Work of Workers
- GenAI models are often trained on stolen third-party data, with employers failing to credit or compensate the original creators.
- Where genAI models are trained on internal data, employees/creators are likewise rarely credited or compensated.
GenAI models are mostly trained on stolen datasets, and, as stated by the owner of a genAI company, ‘impossible to create’ without stealing data (Milmo, 2024). Employers often feel entitled to automatically include their employees’ contribution into their internal dataset without consent and/or extra compensation.
This point does not require much explanation, but please do not let the relative smallness of this section undermine its importance: stealing the creations of others is wrong, creating very similar versions of those creations is wrong, and using those similar versions to generate wealth which the original creator does not share in is wrong.
GenAI does not create, it appropriates. Content output by genAI has its origins in the work of humans, who are rarely compensated for that output.
GenAI Perpetuates Discrimination
- Most genAI models are trained on datasets that carry an intrinsic and exclusionary Western bias that limits diversity and marginalises non-dominant languages, cultures, and ethnicities.
- GenAI content averages out data used to train the AI, leading to a homogenisation of culture.
GenAI models are trained on vast swathes of historical data which is often unvetted and without context, as they aggregate large amounts of data and look for commonalities to generate a response without any direct connection to reality. The use of old data can reflect outdated opinions and values which we would be horrified at today, as well as generate middle of the road solutions.
The predominance and absence of certain types of people in historical data can also reinforce their predominance or absence in the output of genAI models today: for example, the predominance of white male doctors in historical medical literature may lead genAI models to assume that a referenced doctor is white, or to create a white male character when asked to create a doctor.
Within the games industry, this has two main types of effect: it can increase discrimination within workplaces, for example, when used in hiring practices, and it can reduce the range of representation within created media, leading to more homogenous characters and viewpoints in the games we create and play.
GenAI reproduces both historical and contemporary biases and can lead to both implicit and explicit discrimination, including but not limited to racism, sexism, homophobia, transphobia, etc.
GenAI Has Hidden Costs
- GenAI’s output is unreliable and often incorrect.
- GenAI providers obfuscate the cost of genAI, its inner workings, and its limitations.
- GenAI providers aim to silo and monopolise knowledge to sell it back to corporations.
GenAI providers obscure a significant portion of their associated costs (to the environment, mental health, etc.), which impacts the ability of workers to make an informed choice on whether and how to use them (Nguyen, 2025).
GenAI platforms also disincentivise education on their limitations. Average usage of genAI goes down with increased understanding of how genAI actually works behind the scenes (Tully et al., 2025, 20).
GenAI providers sell employers a seductive image of the future, where production can be fully automated. However, in actuality, genAI platforms are gatekeeping transferable skills (Shadmi & Merchant, 2025). Once a model is trained, the ‘owner’ (usually a large conglomerate) of the trained AI has a uniquely powerful position and complete ownership over skills that were previously owned and shared by workers.
This is bad for workers, but it is also bad for employers who outsource their knowledge and skills to third-party genAI providers, as once these companies have a monopoly on this previously institutional knowledge, their prices are unlikely to remain competitive.
GenAI providers aim to replace employers’ dependence on workers with dependence on their own models. Companies which buy into this promise while genAI usage is cheap may come to regret this when it is the only option and prices increase. Workers will regret this much sooner.
Recommendations
Given the present and future impact of genAI on the games industry, we make the following recommendations:
Recommendations for Workers
We understand that the industry is very unstable at present, and that can make it more difficult to speak out. Please do as much as you are comfortable with, and remember that we are stronger together.
- Avoid usage of genAI services whenever possible.
- Make your voices heard.
  - Distance yourself from genAI usage.
  - When genAI content is presented to you, question its use and express dissatisfaction.
  - Talk to your colleagues about the risks and harms of genAI.
  - Make it clear to your employer why you do not want to use genAI.
  - Support other colleagues when they speak out about genAI.
- Do not burn yourself out because of artificially inflated expectations. Do not be a hero by trying to work at an unrealistic pace.
- Educate yourself on how to spot genAI content so that you can avoid accidentally using it in your own work.
- Seek out information on your company’s genAI services from impartial sources, and share it with your colleagues.
- Ask your local worker representatives to negotiate an agreement on genAI usage for your company. Discuss its contents with your colleagues.
  - This agreement could reinforce protections on refusing to use genAI.
  - You could ask to form a committee to evaluate/investigate violations on genAI usage.
- Organise. Unionise. Do it now, not when it is too late and you are already under threat.
Recommendations for Employers:
- Listen to your workers.
  - Involve the workforce in development of policy regarding genAI.
  - Workers should be the ones who decide when, where, and how to use it.
- Listen to your customers.
- Avoid the use of generative AI entirely.
  - Ensure that no worker is required to use genAI.
  - Disallow the use of genAI in the company code of conduct, even for early stages of development and prototyping.
  - If it is absolutely impossible to avoid usage, label/declare usage precisely.
- The chain of accountability at work should be respected and managers should be held accountable for the output of their team.
- Inform workers about the negative outcomes of using genAI. Information should come from impartial sources, not companies trying to sell their genAI services.
- Schedule training for workers on how genAI actually works behind the scenes, its real limits and capabilities.
- Do not use your employees’ work as training data. If you do:
  - Only use data after explicit consent.
  - Ensure fair compensation for reuse of their labor.
  - Allow them to withdraw their work from such datasets.
Recommendations for Players:
- Support games made without genAI.
- Be vocal about disliking genAI in games – leave comments, make videos, ask for change.
- Educate yourself on how to spot genAI content so that you can avoid it.
Recommendations for Regulators:
The UTAW Tech Workers’ Inquiry have put together an incredible resource with suggestions for regulators which we wholeheartedly agree with:
https://techworkersinquiry.org/ai/
In addition to their points, we would also like to suggest that:
- It should be forbidden to submit a person’s work to any genAI dataset without explicit permission and consent, and most importantly without fair compensation for reuse of labour.
- Any acceptable submission must be for datasets that are transparent in how data is used, stored, and retained. Workers must be able to withdraw their content from such datasets at any time
Conclusion – GenAI Against Humanity
GenAI is worsening our working conditions. Our belief is that workers should be the ones who decide when, where and how to use it, as they are the ones who know how to do their jobs. No one likes being forced to do something that harms them, and a lot of workers are being forced to use genAI against their will.
We do not have to accept the use of genAI to make video games;
it is not inevitable.
If genAI was just a useful tool, as advertised, it would make our lives easier and we would have more free time, but the opposite is happening. The owners of these tools are aware of this and that is why they push it: to normalise it, to spread its use, and to ensure it becomes indispensable before raising prices. They are the ones in power, not workers, not even CEOs in video game studios.
We firmly believe that video games are nothing without the humans who make them – they are a way for us to express ourselves, to share knowledge or a point of view with the world, to entertain all kinds of gamers. There is no intent in anything made by a genAI probabilistic engine. If no one put their skill, effort, soul, and time into making a game, why would anyone waste their time playing it?
In the current context, everything has to be data-driven: percentages, averages, time spent, productivity… But quality is subjective and cannot be measured, so companies disregard it. People want high-quality games, they want to spend their time playing a game whose developers poured everything into making it, because they are more enjoyable, as it happens with any other art form.
Years after the public release of genAI chatbots, their use has not been as widespread as their owners would like, but they have been deployed as an excuse for mass layoffs with the goal of pleasing shareholders. We should stop thinking about what is good for business, but instead think about what is good for society in general, for workers, for gamers. What good is it if a CEO earns millions every month if the working conditions at their studio are worsening, along with the video games they create?
No genAI will be able to replace the humanity in any work of art.
We are sick of shareholder value being the be-all and end-all of this industry.
We are sick of watching five giant conglomerates just shuffling money around in a giant scam to be the winner of this never-ending hype contest.
We are sick of seeing people fired or forced to quit just to maximise profits.
We are sick of being told to just accept that this is what is good for business.
GenAI is bad for workers and games. Workers, protect your interests. No games get made without our collective strength. Organise. Fight for your rights.
FAQ
- What happens if most of my colleagues want to use genAI in some capacity? In other words, if a developer really wants to use genAI as part of their process, can they use it?
- The short answer is yes. Obviously the other problems are still there, therefore you should decide as a collective if you want to use it, on the whole, given concerns around the environment, cost, ethics, law, honesty, the sanctity of art, etc. Our aim in this document is to educate people about the harms of genAI, so that both employees and employers are less inclined to use it.
- What if the quality of the output is good, or good enough?
- This doesn’t change the main focus of our manifesto. We want workers’ conditions to improve, and to organise against abuses and for better ownership.
- Why are you talking about AI and not layoffs, RTO mandates, workplace toxicity, etc.?
- While the issues caused by genAI are coming from the same place as these other adjacent crises, the genAI situation is developing rapidly and has practical knock-ons to every aspect of our industry. We’re still working on the other issues plaguing our industry, with old and new methods, and we encourage everyone to join us in that fight.
- How do I know if I should get compensation for an AI-generated output? Is there even a way to find out if a specific data entry was important for a generated output? Or to remove a data entry from a set?
- There are examples of research going in this direction like “WAGLE: Strategic Weight Attribution for Effective and Modular Unlearning in Large Language Models” (J Jia, J Liu, Y Zhang, P Ram, N Baracaldo, S Liu, 2024) and “Losing is for Cherishing: Data Valuation Based on Machine Unlearning and Shapley Value” (Ma, Le ; Yang, Shirao ; Wang, Zihao ; Wang, Yinggui ; Wang, Lei ; Wei, Tao ; Zhang, Kejun, 2025), but the specific implementation is not important.
- Why are you against the future?
- We are not. We simply believe that workers have the right to shape their future. Being exploited and overworked is not a future we are willing to accept. Time will prove genAI ‘tools’ and their usefulness, let us not be trampled in the meantime. The future is not yet written.
