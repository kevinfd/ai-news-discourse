# The Value Of Connected Intelligence In CRE Asset Management

Forbes · Eglae Recchia, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-16

Eglae Recchia, CEO of Keyway, an agentic operating system built for CRE.
Commercial real estate (CRE) asset managers make decisions that directly influence property income, operating performance and long-term asset value. Yet the information behind those decisions is often scattered across leases, amendments, financial statements, budgets and property management systems.
Even if they have the necessary data to make these decisions, the challenge is connecting contractual obligations, financial performance and operating conditions in time to inform decisions.
AI designed for commercial real estate can help address this challenge by interpreting industry-specific information and embedding it within the workflows asset managers rely on. Its value extends beyond document processing to improving how firms understand and manage their properties.
As CEO of Keyway, a company building AI for commercial real estate, I see these challenges firsthand. That experience informs my perspective and gives me a professional interest in industry-specific AI. Still, the broader question is not which technology firms adopt, but how reliably they can turn existing information into better decisions.
Why Lease Abstraction Matters Beyond The Lease
Lease abstraction is a particularly relevant example because lease terms influence nearly every aspect of asset performance, from rental income and expense recoveries to occupancy planning and future cash flow.
Consider a lease amendment that extends a tenant’s term, provides three months of base rent abatement and preserves operating expense reimbursement obligations.
Lease administration must update the abstract, accounting needs to apply the correct charges, and the asset manager must evaluate the effect on projected income and the property’s business plan. These activities are connected, but the information often moves between teams through separate reviews and manual updates.
An AI-enabled workflow can interpret the original lease alongside its amendments, identify changes, link extracted terms to their source language and structure the results according to the firm’s requirements. Following human validation, that information can support the financial and operational processes that depend on it.
The broader opportunity is to make lease information useful throughout asset management, rather than treating abstraction as an isolated administrative task.
Why Industry Specialization Matters
General-purpose AI models have made it easier to summarize documents, extract information and answer questions about complex materials. These capabilities are valuable, but asset management requires information to remain consistent and actionable across multiple business processes.
A lease amendment, for example, must be interpreted in the context of existing contractual obligations, reflected in financial assumptions and made available to the teams responsible for implementing those changes.
General-purpose models can be adapted for these processes, but they often don’t provide the required CRE-specific business logic, integrations and validation controls out of the box.
The reliability challenge extends beyond real estate. For example, research published in Nature documents how large language models can generate plausible but incorrect answers. In CRE, an incorrectly extracted rent escalation or reimbursement obligation could similarly affect financial projections if accepted without verification.
Industry-specialized AI platforms can incorporate these requirements into recurring workflows, accommodating the firm’s accounting conventions, lease structures and operating practices while maintaining traceability.
This distinction becomes especially important when organizations seek to use AI consistently across properties, departments and portfolios. Human expertise and judgment remain essential, complementing AI’s ability to process and connect information with the experience and strategic perspective needed to make informed decisions.
Specialization also involves trade-offs. Implementation may require data preparation, integration work, employee training and ongoing oversight. General-purpose AI may be the easier choice for drafting communications, brainstorming or summarizing non-sensitive materials when specialized workflows and system integrations are unnecessary.
Connecting Financial Performance Across Assets
Asset managers also need to understand how developments across different functions affect property performance.
Suppose a property’s net operating income falls below projections. The underlying causes may include higher insurance expenses, delayed tenant reimbursements or a rent concession negotiated during a recent renewal. Investigating those factors requires information from accounting, leasing and property management, often maintained in different systems.
When relevant information is connected, asset managers can examine the drivers of financial performance more efficiently and identify discrepancies that deserve attention.
At the portfolio level, the implications are greater. Several major tenant leases expiring within the same quarter could create a concentration of income at risk. Recurring operating expense increases across properties may indicate broader cost pressures.
This is where enterprise intelligence becomes valuable by connecting an organization’s contractual, financial and operational knowledge to provide a more comprehensive understanding of its assets.
But connecting systems may also introduce risks. Inconsistent property identifiers, outdated records and incompatible data formats can undermine results. Sensitive financial and tenant information also requires appropriate access permissions and security controls. Firms should always assess data quality, integration requirements and vendor safeguards before deployment.
Making Intelligence Part Of Everyday Asset Management
The lasting value of AI depends on how effectively it supports the work asset managers already perform.
Embedded workflows allow reviewed information to move more consistently between lease administration, accounting, property management and financial oversight. They also help preserve institutional knowledge that might otherwise remain fragmented across teams.
Reliability is essential. An incorrectly interpreted rent concession can distort income projections, while inconsistent expense classifications can undermine portfolio comparisons.
For executives, the measure of success should extend beyond time saved on abstraction or reporting. The more meaningful question is whether asset managers can recognize developing risks sooner, understand performance drivers more clearly and coordinate action across their portfolios.
Firms already hold much of the information they need to manage their assets effectively, but connecting that information through industry-specific intelligence can make it more useful at every stage of asset oversight.
The advantage lies in putting the knowledge firms already possess to work across their portfolios. With a clearer understanding of asset performance, emerging risks and investment opportunities, firms can make more confident decisions, maximize the value of their existing assets and pursue growth with greater precision.
