# Over 400 American AI Data Centers Use As Much Electricity As Wisconsin, Michigan, or Indiana

TIME · Simmone Shah · 2026-11-16

America’s hyperscale data centers together use as much electricity as some entire U.S. states and produce millions of metric tons of carbon dioxide over the course of a year, according to a new study published Monday in Nature Sustainability.
The number of hyperscale data centers, which are larger and more powerful than traditional data centers, and are often used to train and run large-scale AI models, has exploded in recent years. Between 2019 and 2024, the number of data centers around the world doubled from a little over 500 to over 1,100, with more than half located in the United States. That number is only expected to grow: the research firm Synergy estimates that the total capacity of U.S. data centers will double in the next three years, largely driven by the growth of hyperscale data centers.
American voters rallied against data center development in the midterm elections this November, with many citing economic and environmental concerns. In 40 out of 41 local ballot initiatives across the country, citizens voted to limit or prevent data center development. Yet few studies have quantified just how much electricity each hyperscale data center uses, and what fuels generate the electricity, the researchers say.
“Even though there were a lot of [public and scientific] statements about the environmental impact of data centers, when we started this research, we realized that there was basically very little, if none, peer-reviewed data,” says Francesca Dominici, director of the Harvard Data Science Initiative and senior author of the paper.
The study provides one of the first facility-level estimates of the electricity use and carbon dioxide emissions of hyperscale data centers since the rise of AI. The researchers used satellite imagery to build a list of 403 hyperscale data centers across the U.S., and then estimated the annual electricity consumption for each facility between May 2024 and April 2025 under a range of operating scenarios. They then used data from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency to calculate their emissions.
They found that, together, the data centers used between 68 and 99 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity—with the upper estimate equivalent to what some states, like Indiana, Michigan, and Tennessee, use in a year. In the lower scenario, the facilities still consumed approximately the annual electricity sold to customers in Wisconsin, roughly 70 TWh, or Minnesota, around 65 TWh.
More than half of the electricity powering the data centers comes from fossil fuels, primarily natural gas and coal. The facilities studied produced a combined estimated 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide—roughly the annual emissions of Denmark.
Half of the electricity use of all the data centers studied was concentrated in just four states: Virginia, Oregon, Ohio, and Iowa, the report found. Virginia has long been known as “data center alley” due to its high number of data centers, but size and power capacity can also impact electricity consumption. The researchers identified 26 hyperscale facilities in Iowa, combined electricity consumption for which was approximately 7.2 TWh, with the average facility having substantially higher estimated electricity demand than the average facility in Virginia, where 142 hyperscale facilities account for approximately 21.4 TWh of annual electricity consumption.
However, the emissions impacts were not always the same. Virginia, Iowa, and Ohio have the most emissions attributable to hyperscale data centers, while Oregon, which is home to more hyperscale data centers than Iowa and Ohio, has significantly fewer emissions due to a cleaner energy grid.
“For the same amount of electricity consumed, the regional electricity mix associated with data centers in Wyoming produces approximately seven times more carbon dioxide than in Oregon,” says Gianluca Guidi, Harvard data scientist and the study’s lead author. “So these environmental impacts are not necessarily inevitable. Geography matters.”
Without clean energy alternatives, U.S. data centers are projected to consume more natural gas than most countries by 2035, according to a report from BloombergNEF.
The electricity demand and carbon emissions are likely even higher than the research shows. The team did not have enough data to assess every single hyperscale data center in the U.S. at the time, and more data centers have emerged since their study concluded in April 2025.
The researchers hope to continue to quantify the environmental impacts of hyperscale data centers to better understand their impact on air pollution, water, and health, adding that, as AI continues to scale, its infrastructure growth must be done sustainably.
“We tend to imagine AI as something that is almost immaterial, but in the end, the infrastructure power is profoundly physical,” says Guidi. “Behind every algorithm, there are enormous buildings, power plants, water resources, and ultimately also communities that live with the consequences.”
