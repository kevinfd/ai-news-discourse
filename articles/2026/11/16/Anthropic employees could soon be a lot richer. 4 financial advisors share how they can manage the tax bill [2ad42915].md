# Anthropic employees could soon be a lot richer. 4 financial advisors share how they can manage the tax bill.

Business Insider · Alex Nicoll, Alex Nicoll (anicoll@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-16

Some Anthropic workers are about to become super wealthy. Here's how they can manage their new tax bills. Samuel Boivin/NurPhoto via Getty Images This year is minting many single-stock millionaires through IPOs from SpaceX and Anthropic. Now, workers face the challenge of diversifying portfolios and paying high taxes. Financial advisors walked through strategies to help: from psychological tricks to buying carwashes. Some Silicon Valley employees are about to see their financial circumstances switch from well-off to extremely wealthy . The year already saw the largest IPO ever, SpaceX, a record that could be broken later this month by Anthropic. For some employees, the windfalls could be life-changing but tied to a single company. Eric Franklin, a former Amazon employee who co-founded Prospero Wealth, a wealth advisory business focused on tech workers, said his firm has talked with several Anthropic employees who are "looking at $100 to $200 million outcomes." With wealth will come taxes, especially as employees try to diversify away from their biggest holdings: their employer. "If you think about the average Anthropic person or SpaceX person , their balance is their 401(k), maybe they've been doing their backdoor Roth IRA, and then they own Anthropic or SpaceX," said Thomas Kopelman, cofounder of AllStreet Wealth, who has managed the wealth of roughly 10 SpaceX employees. Selling those shares to diversify their investments could trigger enormous tax bills, especially for early employees who received shares at a fraction of their current value. In California, where profits from stock sales are taxed like regular income, those capital gains can face up to a 37.1% tax. Business Insider spoke with four financial advisors about the strategies tech workers can use to diversify their fortunes while minimizing their tax bills. Their advice ranged from simple psychological tricks to sophisticated investment strategies — and even buying car washes or shipping-container businesses. But the advisors agreed that before cashing out, employees should figure out how much money they actually need and build a plan around it. "You've won the game; you have all the wealth that you possibly could need," Koppelman said. "What do you want your lifestyle to look like and how much should we lock in day one?" From there, advisors can help a tech worker create a plan to minimize their tax costs and diversify their wealth. Timing is important to consider — employees and other insiders are usually restricted from selling their stock right when a company goes public. "It's almost never: 'Oh, well, let's use this one single product and boom, you're done," Franklin said, instead choosing different strategies for different timelines. Here are some of the tools tech workers are using to save on taxes as they look to stop being all in on their employers, from the simplest to the most complex. The case for keeping it simple The simplest option is to change your mindset. If the only thing certain in life is death and taxes, you might as well come to terms with both. "The real challenge when you have these positions is you look at the value that's on your statement, and you think that's all your money," said Alex Shahidi, co-CIO of Evoke Advisors, a $28 billion AUM wealth advisory firm. "But in reality, there's a tax liability that's not written on your statement." Sophisticated strategies to try to defer and minimize those taxes introduce complexity which, "oftentimes creates unpleasant surprises in the future," he said. One approach for someone prioritizing simplicity, he said, is to sell some one year, some the next year, spreading it out over tax years. They could also focus on building a diversified portfolio, including assets such as gold, commodities, or other equities, to reduce their dependence on their employer's stock. One "completely psychological" technique is to wait until the stock appreciates enough to cover the expected tax bill and then sell it. "Then the market paid the tax for you," Shahidi said. "It actually works. Even very smart people are like, 'I just feel better about that.'" Tax strategy du jour: Tax-loss harvesting The first obvious tool for any tax-aware investor is direct indexing . Instead of holding an index fund that tracks, for example, the S&P 500, investors buy individual stocks that roughly mirror that index's performance. This allows them to roughly achieve the same returns as the index while using losses to offset gains in a process known as tax-loss harvesting. With smaller positions, this is usually where Prospero Wealth's Franklin will start. "That's a place where they can feel like they're getting something beyond what they would generally do themselves," he said. "It's something that's easy to explain." For wealthier clients, advisors may recommend direct indexing "on steroids," Franklin said, or long-short indexing, which introduces leverage and short selling to increase tax losses or to try to outperform an index intentionally. Long-short strategies have higher fees and can often be riskier than direct indexing. "The bigger the portfolio, the more long-short makes sense," Kopelman said. Borrowing against your holdings A popular way to access your stock value without immediately selling and paying taxes is to borrow against your shares. Investors could take out a loan against their shares, but rates aren't fixed and therefore create a lot of risk. Instead, advisors said there are other ways to borrow, with their help. Franklin gave the example of a client at a recently IPO'd tech company who decided to pledge a third of their position, $8 million, to a bank in exchange for instant liquidity of up to 90% of the shares' value. "You get the cash immediately, and then you can immediately diversify that and start doing tax-loss harvesting," Franklin said. This will allow you to generate much higher tax losses than you would if you were just investing with whatever cash you have on hand. Franklin's client was using what's called a variable pre-paid forward, a contract that allows you to delay selling your stock for 3 to 5 years while getting the money now. Of course, there's a risk that the stock price will have gone down in that time, and you could lose money when you repay it. Single-stock exchanges: classic but complicated A classic way to diversify a single stock is to use an exchange fund. An exchange fund allows investors to pool their appreciated shares with other investors' holdings, giving them exposure to a more diversified portfolio without immediately triggering capital gains taxes. The main caveat is that it comes with a seven-year lockup, fees, and sometimes a waitlist, depending on which stock you have. "You almost never can get into them by the position size that you want," Kopelman said. "It's an option, but it's one that I've never had a client do." Some of the advisors said they avoid exchange funds for this reason, though Eric Franklin said they can be a good tool for money you know you won't need until seven years in the future. The more unusual strategies: car washes and solar panels From there, tax strategies can get even more esoteric. One option, said Gabriel Shahin, founder of Falcon Wealth Advisors, is investing in opportunity zone funds, which pool investment capital into low-income areas while allowing you to defer tax. "The problem is you're not going to get the same juice for investing in an op zone as you are in a tech company, let alone a private tech company," Shahin, whose firm manages $1.8 billion, said, meaning you really need to be focused on lowering tax for that to make sense. You can also buy a business, Shahin said, and use the paper losses from buying or operating the business to offset up to $512,000 in income, the current IRS limit for excess business losses that a married couple can claim. The tax code's bonus depreciation rules , which allow you to deduct up to 100% of the value of certain types of property, allow you to supercharge that with even more paper losses. There are a few catches: you need to pay with your time to pass participation rules, such as spending 500 hours a year on the activity. This also requires you to set up a separate LLC and do taxes on it, Shahin said. Shahin said investors adopting this strategy often buy shipping container businesses, car washes, or convenience stores. The most popular, he said, are solar businesses that allow you also to get solar tax credits. There are some "cool and gnarly" strategies out there, but they really require help from a professional, especially one with a tax focus like his own, Shahin said.
