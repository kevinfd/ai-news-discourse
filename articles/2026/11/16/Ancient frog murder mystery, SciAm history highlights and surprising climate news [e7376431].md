# Ancient frog murder mystery, SciAm history highlights and surprising climate news

Scientific American · Rachel Feltman, Andrea Thompson, Sushmita Pathak, Alex Sugiura · 2026-11-16

Happy Monday, listeners! For Scientific American’s Science Quickly, I’m Rachel Feltman. You’re listening to our weekly science news roundup.
The United Nations’ latest Climate Change Conference, COP31, is underway in Türkiye, so it’s only fitting that we start with some climate news. You may have seen headlines saying that global CO2 emissions tied to fossil fuels are dropping. As shocking as that sounds, it is true. A study published last week in Nature Climate Change projected a .4% emissions drop for the year—which stands in sharp and appealing contrast to the average increase of .8% they found each year from 2019 to 2025. But don’t go popping the champagne just yet. This almost certainly isn’t a sign that fossil fuels are on their way out, at least not at the rate that we need them to be. Instead of a global shift toward cleaner fuels, the study authors say, this dip is likely a reflection of high pump prices tied to the war in Iran.
The study authors note that in the U.S., China and the European Union, people paid an average of 10 to 50 percent more to gas up their vehicles in 2026 than they did in 2025. In a survey conducted by the Washington Post, ABC News and the market research group Ipsos back in April, 44% of respondents said they had cut back on driving due to cost. That could very well explain why oil emissions are projected to drop by 2% compared to last year. Meanwhile, the study authors calculated a .3% drop in gas and a .6% rise in coal.
On supporting science journalism
This isn’t the only behavior-driven dip we’ve seen in recent memory. In 2020, amid lockdowns due to the COVID pandemic, global CO2 emissions from fossil fuels dropped by 5.4%. But by 2021, emissions had pretty much already bounced back to 2019 levels.
However, this study does have a bit of genuinely encouraging news. The researchers found that solar and wind power, along with electric vehicles, are growing quickly enough to offset around two thirds of the growth in overall demand for energy. Because that’s the tricky thing about lowering emissions, or at least one of the trickiest things: We’re trying to cut down on fossil fuel use while the truth is that we’re using more and more energy every year. Some of that is due to a sort of energy use lifestyle creep that we should arguably really try to curb, like our sudden need to generate video memes on demand. But on the other end of the spectrum, hundreds of millions of people worldwide still lack access to electricity for their homes. So whenever we talk about cutting emissions, as necessary and urgent as that endeavor is, we also have to recognize that simply using less energy, or even capping our global consumption at its current rate, isn’t a feasible goal.
So back to that good news: The authors of this recent study project that, from now through 2028, EVs, solar and wind energy will offset around 64 to 79 percent of that inevitable growth. They also noted that 59 countries with growing economies managed to emit less in 2025 than they did in 2019. If that shift to cleaner energy continues, we could reach a true peak in fossil fuel CO2 emissions in the years to come.

Speaking of history, our final story for the week features some very old, very mysterious bones.
Between 2016 and 2018, archaeologists in England excavated a shallow ditch containing some 9,000 tiny bones. Radiocarbon dating set them in the Roman period, around the third century C.E., and they seem to have all been ditched there in the span of a single year. It gets stranger: Most of the bones came from frogs. Now it would take quite a mass casualty event to explain so many amphibians croaking in one spot, which made researchers wonder whether human activity could be to blame, as it so often is for weird stuff. Was this some kind of frog-based ritual? Or a popular roadside joint for ordering fried frog legs? A study published last month in Environmental Archaeology claims to have finally solved the mystery of the frog cemetery, and humans weren’t to blame after all.
The true culprits? Owls.
Microscopic evaluation of the tiny remains revealed that most had tell-tale signs of being digested while also carrying breakage patterns consistent with the eating habits of short-eared owls. Other probable predators such as otters, snakes and herons would have dumped fewer leftovers compared to the owls, which are known to have the charming affectation of swallowing dinner whole before regurgitating pellets of largely intact bones.
But what’s with the sudden rush of owl-on-frog crime in this specific location? Because, remember all these bones seem to go into the ditch the same year. What happened? Apparently conditions were just perfect for providing the birds with an all-you-can-eat buffet of amphibians that year. Sea levels were falling in the area at the time, which left a bunch of temporary ponds around for frogs to breed in. Meanwhile, humans were clearing out the woods to let their animals graze, which meant more grassland for short-eared owls to roost in. As for the huge concentration of bones in this one area, this was probably a ditch of upchucked frogs that filled in quickly due to random circumstances, which protected the bones within from the elements while most were washed away.
