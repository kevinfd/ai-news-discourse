# If the AI bubble is popping, history shows these stocks could boom

CNBC · Deena Zaidi · 2026-11-16

If the AI boom is unraveling, history suggests investors may want to look at some of the market's least exciting stocks: sellers and purveyors of consumer staples, from cookies to tissues to toothpaste. These companies could have more room to rally as technology shares weaken, according to BTIG's chief market technician, Jonathan Krinsky, who sees a historical corollary in the rotation that followed the dot-com peak in early 2000. In the current market, staples gained 10% over the past month despite concerns that they are expensive and offer little growth, Krinsky wrote. "Sometimes the market is wrong, but other times it's telling a story for those willing to listen," Krinsky wrote in a report on Sunday. A dot-com lesson The S & P 500 consumer staples index outperformed the S & P 500 information technology index by 33% over the last 30 days, the largest such move in 25 years, according to BTIG. The only period with similar moves was from April 2000 through October 2001, Krinsky said. Back then, even after the initial surge, staples continued to outperform tech by nearly 10 percentage points over the next two years. "When the dot-com bubble peaked in March 2000, it wasn't a broad bear market right away." Krinsky wrote. "In fact, in the one-year following that peak, seven S & P sectors gained 12% or more, and consumer staples were up by over 50%!" Between March 10, 2000 — when the market topped — and March 10, 2001, consumer staples climbed 50.3%, while information technology plunged 56.9%, according to BTIG. Utilities were up 44%, financials rose 36.4% and healthcare gained 25.9%. Boring is beautiful More recently, BTIG said consumer staples are again advancing while tech retreats. Between June 22 and Nov. 13, consumer staples gained 12.6%, while information technology fell 7.3%. Healthcare did even better, climbing 25%, followed by a 19.7% gain in energy. Financials rose 8.7% and consumer discretionary stocks gained 5.9%. Like technology, industrial and communication services companies also fell, 7.7% and 7.2%, respectively. Krinsky noted that one of the differences between then and now is that many sectors had fallen 20% to 30% heading into the March 2000 technology peak, creating a more extreme starting point than exists today. But the current leadership provided by defensive stocks is hard to ignore, he said. "While we continue to think correlations rise and thus staples won't be completely immune to a broad-based decline, they are showing too much relative strength to ignore," he wrote. Among individual staples stocks with constructive chart patterns, Krinsky highlighted Costco , Mondelez International , Colgate-Palmolive and Brown-Forman . "Consumer staples may be boring, but boring can be beautiful," he wrote.
