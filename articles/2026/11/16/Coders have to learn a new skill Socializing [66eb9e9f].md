# Coders have to learn a new skill: Socializing

Business Insider · Amanda Hoover, Amanda Hoover (ahoover@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-16

Getty Images; Tyler Le/BI Michael Irwin's latest project relied more on his woodworking and people skills than his deep knowledge of coding. The principal software engineer for Docker, a platform that helps developers adopt models, tools and agents securely, handmade a wooden base to serve as a gaming controller, attached red, blue, and green buttons with wires, and took it to a conference last week. It corresponded to a game coded by AI, and Irwin used the gimmick to draw people to his booth where he engaged them in talks about AI agents. The game is a frill that Irwin wouldn't have taken the time to make before AI accelerated coding. "It's been a dream of mine for a long time, and it's just now more accessible than it's ever been," he says. Gone are the days when software engineers spent most of their time in solitude, coding remotely and rarely attending meetings. Their remit has expanded beyond the keyboard, and the boundaries of technical work have blurred. Companies, including Amazon , increasingly expect engineers to connect personally with customers. People outside engineering teams at many companies have taken up generating code and using AI at work; a fifth of OpenAI's Codex users were non-developers as of June. Bosses expect developers to manage teams of agents and communicate complex technical work in simple terms, all while acting as designers, architects, and big picture conceptual thinkers rather than cogs in a coding machine. "The micro level is mostly able to be passed off," Irwin says. "It takes a different mindset." "This new way of working, it might not suit the typical engineer personality," says Sam Dhar, an engineer at Galileo, an AI startup Cisco acquired earlier this year. But it relies on the expertise and judgement that engineers have always had to build over their careers. Still, there's pressure for developers to make that mindset shift in a condensed timeline or risk being laid off and replaced by someone who can charm their way through an interview rather than ace a take-home coding test . Hit swiftly and broadly by automation, developers are a bellwether for white-collar work. They sit at the frontier of AI's transformation of work, which will either swallow jobs or create new ones, depending on who you ask. "It's only happening to engineering first," Dhar says. "It's potentially going to manifest in a way where all work is done in some respects with AI, where the accountability still resides with the humans who are reviewing and fixing and asking the right questions." Reports of the coding apocalypse are greatly exaggerated, so far. More people work in the field than ever: About 1.9 million people worked in software development, testing, and quality assurance in the US as of last year, up from about 1.5 million in 2020, according to the US Bureau of Labor Statistics. The bureau projects the industry will grow by 10% over the next decade. A developer in 2020 would hardly recognize the demands of the job today. Vibe coding forever changed software engineering last year. Big companies like Microsoft said Google AI had written large swaths of their latest code, and engineers began spending more time reviewing lines of AI-generated code than they did typing it. Experienced developers finished projects faster, and companies scaled back on hiring junior engineers . 2026, the father of vibe coding said, would be the year of " agentic engineering ." Amazon Web Service recently used agents to generate 100% of the code for a new emulator tool. Software engineers have moved away from writing code and on to concepts. They still need deep technical expertise to evaluate AI's work, but they also need business sense. "You have to really understand a lot more about why we're doing whatever it is we're doing, and ask a lot of the right questions," says Paul Elliott, vice president of product and engineering at software testing company Chromatic. That requires more conversations across the company. For all the left-brain, right-brain debate and stereotypes of developers working remotely with headphones on, engineers are making the leap to become more forward-facing. "It's definitely a trainable skill," Elliott says. Marc Brooker, vice president and distinguished engineer at Amazon Web Services, says engineers on his team now spend more time with customers and prospects, talking to decision-makers and developers at other companies. Training for these scenarios is less formal and more often involves shadowing calls with engineers who have more public-facing experience. "The outcome of that is so that the engineers can go back to their teams and their organizations and turn what they learned into really great product decisions, really great technical decisions," Brooker says. Dylan Wight, founding software engineer at Flagler Health, an AI-native operating system for musculoskeletal care, says that his days now consist of reviewing code issues raised by agents and determining if they're practical problems or oversensitive flags. Wight also has multiple meetings weekly with the system's users in the healthcare industry, which he welcomes. He has a side "that's more entrepreneurial and likes talking to users and understanding their needs," he says. "If people wanted to just think about code, I think it would get harder, because that part is mostly done." The wall between engineer and product manager is breaking down. Product managers previously owned many of those client conversations, volleying between business needs and the technical expertise of developer teams. Now, AI companies increasingly use the term " member of technical staff " to describe their workers. Anthropic released a report about its workforce last month, showing that technical workers spent 16% more time in meetings now compared to a year ago. At the same time, more workers are shipping code, and engineers are gaining a stronger presence: The ratio of engineers to product managers has jumped to 17 to one. "Engineers are definitely taking on more product level autonomy, but product managers are also taking on more technical work," says Frank Fusco, CEO of software company Silicon Society. "I think the definitions of those jobs are changing." Forward-deployed engineers , who work inside another company's systems, have become increasingly popular in the AI era, as they work with clients on solutions, combining technical knowledge with that problem-solving ethos. Companies increasingly demand technical candidates who can work in the office, a shift after years of allowing them to work independently and remotely. Michelle Volberg, CEO of software recruiting company Twill, says that last year about half of the roles her company worked to fill were remote. Now, some 95% of jobs require hybrid or full-time in-office work . That mirrors larger trends in white-collar work as employers have gained power in a tight labor market, but perhaps also a desire to have engineers communicating across the company more frequently. In early 2024, 63% of tech jobs postings had in-office requirements, according to data from recruiting and staffing company Robert Half. That figure now hovers at 84% this fall. The proportion of job posts for remote tech roles declined from 10% in early 2023 to just 3%. "A lot of these managers are bringing engineering on site now to have this collaborative approach," says Kareem Osman, tech industry expert at Robert Half. Some hiring managers now require candidates to have those public speaking skills, Volberg says. She searches for developers active on social media like YouTube and LinkedIn who already talk or post publicly about what they're building. "It also comes down to the soft skills of, 'can you talk to the CEO and the founder about what the business needs directly as an engineer and then translate that into something that you're going to go to your desk and build?'" she says. Data from CompTIA, a nonprofit trade association for the US IT industry, shows that 47% of open software engineer roles in early 2020 listed communication skills in job descriptions, but now nearly 60% of jobs seek candidates with such skills. For some, the soft skills are there and just need some refining. Chuck Hardy, director of product and engineering at Akeneo, says the company pulled engineering teams into customer conversations before the AI boom. He looks for people with critical thinking skills and judgment, and when hiring, gives them sample questions and spends time role-playing with candidates before they go to a panel interview. "We're not there to test you or to trick you," Hardy says. "We're there to create an environment where you can figure out how to demonstrate your best self in a short period of time." For experienced engineers, the AI boom offers an opportunity to step up to more autonomous work, while relying on their expertise to evaluate AI. But for younger engineers, the opportunity to develop expertise in the intricacies of code is vanishing. A potential career ladder collapse looms. At Anthropic, the proportion of junior technical hires declined from 27% in March of 2025 to 9% this May. About 7 to 8% of computer engineering and computer science majors who graduated in the past few years are unemployed, according to the latest data from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York — a proportion on par with performing arts majors. With demand for engineers projected to grow, the vanishing training ground could lead not just to eventual worker shortages but to a shrinking number of people with the practice and savvy to spot issues in AI-generated code. AI can turn anyone into an amateur, letting novice cooks spin up recipes, rusty writers refine their grammar, and those with limited drawing skills design templates. It has democratized coding for people who have never done more than customize an early-aughts blog. A debate brews about whether generalists or specialized experts will emerge as winners. Right now, the transformation of software engineers hints that it's both: a keen, technical eye to search for flaws and vulnerabilities in code, paired with communication skills and creative thinking, makes a candidate highly valuable. That balance could change as AI advances. But it indicates a future when the bounds of white-collar job titles and their tasks could continue to bend and blend.
