# Should you take a buyout? 9 Big Tech workers share how they made the call.

Business Insider · Madison Hoff,Alice Tecotzky, Madison Hoff,Alice Tecotzky (mhoff@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-16

Jay Ongg, Donnetta Kay Hagist, Jonathan Monheit, Fernanda Monteiro, Scott Thurlow, Justin Long, Shawn Mrzena, Mike RothKugel, Bobbie Ziemer, Tyler Le/BI Late last month, some 60,000 Microsoft employees were presented with a choice that could transform the trajectory of their careers: accept a buyout offer or remain at their jobs. Microsoft has said it expects fewer than 5% of eligible employees to accept the voluntary exit offer. The tech giant's move to downsize comes as many companies restructure or shrink their workforces via buyouts or voluntary retirement packages, sometimes citing AI as a rationale. Business Insider spoke to nine people who have previously accepted voluntary exit offers about their decision-making process and their top tips for people grappling with the same choice. They range in age from their 30s to their 60s and mentioned considerations ranging from finances and healthcare coverage to time, family, and identity. We reached out to their former employers for comment. Google and Microsoft declined to comment. Verizon confirmed former employment. Here's what nine people had to say about life after buyouts. Jay Ongg Company: Microsoft Title and years of experience at the time: Principal group engineering manager, spent almost 25 years at Microsoft in various roles. Offer details: Employees who took the Voluntary Retirement Program were offered up to five years of health insurance coverage, with Microsoft paying for the first year; stock vesting; and base pay determined by seniority and tenure, capped at 39 weeks. Jay Ongg suggests that workers debating whether to accept a buyout take stock of their lives and consider the trade-offs. Courtesy of Jay Ongg Because I was there at Microsoft for a long time, it did become a part of my identity. Jay Ongg wasn't thinking about retiring, but when Microsoft offered a voluntary retirement program in the spring, with employees' last day being in July, he began to think more about his future. It was a tough decision to make after more than two decades at Microsoft, where he said he worked on Xbox, Windows Mobile, Teams, and other products. It would mean saying goodbye to a large part of his identity. He decided to take the chance. "It was kind of scary, but I'm actually a pretty optimistic guy," Ongg said. Since then, he's been exercising more, experimenting with large language models to keep flexing his technical skills, and working on a personal finance AI assistant. His advice for workers debating whether to take a buyout is to take stock of your life, goals, and values. Think about yourself and your family. Whatever you decide, you need to feel good about it at the end of the day, he said. "Everything in life is full of tradeoffs. What are you trading off if you take it versus not?" Ongg said. "We all make decisions based on incomplete information, and there's no crime in doing that. We never have complete information." Donnetta Kay Hagist Company: Google Title and years of experience: Core engineering senior program manager, spent more than 5 years at the company and left when she was 36. Offer details: Google buyout for US employees in June 2025 Donnetta Kay Hagist left Google in October 2025. Courtesy of Donnetta Kay Hagist It's a leap of faith, but I have never been happier. My family is thriving now because I left Google. Donnetta Kay Hagist didn't consider Google's voluntary exit offer until she got coffee with a colleague who revealed that he, a respected team leader, was accepting it. Shocked, she called her husband to tell him she, too, wanted to leave. "I believe that things happen for a reason, and this was the push I needed to bet on myself," Hagist said, adding that she wasn't scared to break out of the so-called "golden handcuffs." Given her husband's stable job, finances and healthcare were sorted — they calculated that she wouldn't need to land a new role until the spring. After her last day at Google in October 2025, Hagist spent a lot of time with her daughters, but quickly got nervous about the job market and started applying for new roles. A friend advised her to relax and take a well-deserved solo trip to Switzerland. She went on the vacation; she signed up for a Hyrox and a half-marathon; she spent more time on her relationships. In November, a mechanical services company, The Arcticom Group, reached out about a senior director role. She landed the job. Hagist said those considering a buyout should make sure their basic needs are met, and then ask themselves whether they're OK with their next three years mirroring their previous three years. "If you feel called to do something different, I encourage you to go explore it. The world is so big," she said. Jonathan Monheit Company: Microsoft Title and years of experience: Lead e-discovery program manager, spent more than 12 years at the company and left when he was 58. Offer details: Employees who took the Voluntary Retirement Program were offered up to five years of health insurance coverage, with Microsoft paying for the first year; stock vesting; and base pay determined by seniority and tenure, capped at 39 weeks. Jonathan Monheit had already planned to leave Microsoft. Courtesy of Jonathan Monheit It was hard to imagine a world in which I would say no. For Jonathan Monheit, Microsoft's voluntary retirement offer was a "winner winner, chicken dinner" moment. He and his wife already had plans to move from Seattle to Dallas, where they were building a new home. Monheit had gotten permission to work remotely through the end of 2026 and then planned to leave the company. He received Microsoft's buyout offer in the spring and realized he'd essentially be paid the same amount as if he'd worked through December. After his final day on July 2, Monheit and his wife drove to their new home, stopping at national parks throughout the 10-day journey. They spent the next few weeks settling in before jetting off to Singapore, Thailand, and Toronto. Now, around four months after leaving Microsoft, Monheit is starting to get a bit restless. "You're used to having things drive you every day. There are moments when I'm like, 'OK, what do I do now?' But in general, it's been healthy. I sleep better. I eat better," he said. He's started exploring new jobs. Ideally, Monheit said his next move is to become a consultant, helping companies approach e-discovery more efficiently. Fernanda Monteiro Company: Google Title and years of experience: Head of YouTube partner operations in EMEA, spent 17 years at the company and left when she was 41. Offer details: UK voluntary exit package in late 2025 Fernanda Monteiro started her own business after accepting her offer. Courtesy of Fernanda Monteiro There is the grief, because you're letting a big part of yourself go. That hurts; that is painful. But at the same time, there was also a pressure being released. Fernanda Monteiro was preparing to return to Google from maternity leave in February when an email arrived about a voluntary exit offer. She had initially dismissed it when it hit her inbox in December 2025, but then thought back to research she'd done about the identity transition that accompanies leaving Big Tech. She started thinking of the package as a springboard for a career pivot. Money was the most obvious question — abandoning her salary would mean rethinking finances, but at the same time, childcare in London is expensive. Monteiro could also feel burnout triggers creeping back in, so she started talking to former bosses, close friends, and family about the decision. "Closing my eyes and picturing myself going back to work and being a mother of two — I couldn't see myself in that position anymore. That gave me the answer," Monteiro said. She signed the buyout offer in late January and said she remained an employee until the end of April, though she never returned from maternity leave. She spent the first few weeks ticking off a checklist of corporate errands: scheduling medical appointments, sorting out login permissions, accessing personal documents. It was a bumpy emotional road, pocked with doubt and the exhaustion of a new baby. Now, Monteiro said she's confident in her choice. She has time to spend with her daughters as she builds a business as a career transition advisor, helping individuals and companies navigate transitions out of Big Tech . Scott Thurlow Company: Microsoft Title and years of experience at the time: Principal product manager, spent roughly 29 non-continuous years at Microsoft and left when he was 60. Offer details: Employees who took the Voluntary Retirement Program were offered up to five years of health insurance coverage, with Microsoft paying for the first year; stock vesting; and base pay determined by seniority and tenure, capped at 39 weeks. Scott Thurlow said the VRP accelerated his departure from Microsoft by six months to a year. Courtesy of Scott Thurlow I had something that I was working towards and wanting to do, and the buyout allowed me to accelerate that. Scott Thurlow started at Microsoft in the '90s, then worked for Expedia for a few years before boomeranging to Microsoft in 2007. He lived in Canada from 2014 to 2017 as Microsoft expanded into Vancouver. In 2023, he decided to pursue a doctorate alongside his job. By the time of Microsoft's Voluntary Retirement Program, or VRP, offer, Thurlow was mostly done with coursework and getting ready to begin the research portion on how organizations scale human oversight of AI. "There were two problems with staying around at Microsoft," he said. "Problem No. 1 is time, and problem No. 2 is imagine the conversation when I approach Google saying I want to understand how they're solving these problems if I'm also a Microsoft employee . I'm expecting they won't be entirely receptive to discussing things with me." He said the VRP, which he described as a "self-layoff," accelerated his departure from Microsoft by six months to a year. He left the company in July. Thurlow is hoping to wrap up his doctorate by summer of 2028. After that, he might consult, as he had already set up a practice in anticipation of consulting work, or he might work for someone else. His advice: If you're already working toward something else, a buyout could be a good opportunity. If you're unhappy with your workplace, consider taking it, too. He recommends having something to do after the buyout. "It can be a professional, it can be technical, it can be volunteering at social or charitable institutions," he said. "Just make sure you have something to invest yourself into so that you stay engaged with your life and your community." Justin Long Company: Microsoft Title and years of experience: Principal site reliability engineering manager, spent more than 26 years as a full-time employee and left when he was 50. Offer details: Employees who took the Voluntary Retirement Program were offered up to five years of health insurance coverage, with Microsoft paying for the first year; stock vesting; and base pay determined by seniority and tenure, capped at 39 weeks. Justin Long could feel AI burnout building up when the offer came in. Courtesy of Justin Long If you're already a little unhappy now, maybe this is the kick that you need to go find something else. I would rather be happy in a new thing than struggle to hold onto what is no longer what it was. Justin Long started preparing for life after Microsoft after witnessing rounds of layoffs in 2023 and 2025. He'd paid off his house, was saving more, and planned to become an individual contributor again instead of a manager. When the company offered him a voluntary retirement package earlier this year, he brought it up to his wife. "She was shocked because she knows how much I loved working there. But at the same time, it was getting less enjoyable," Long said. Long had aimed to retire at 55 but could stop working early with the package, especially because it offered years of healthcare. His wife got more comfortable with the idea after they met with the financial advisor who had been helping them plan for retirement. "All the right things fit into place. Not any one thing made me make the choice," Long said, adding that he could feel AI burnout building. Long rested for a week after his contract ended and then went on a trip to Hawaii with family, where he worked on his photography and scuba certification. For Long, this is true retirement — he'd only return to work if he started doing something new. He's started physical therapy for long-ignored shoulder pain; he's been cooking more, including banana oatmeal cookies from TikTok and split pea soup with Japanese sweet potatoes; he began volunteering for the King County Democrats. Long encouraged those grappling with a similar choice, especially at Microsoft, to consider their relationship to their jobs in a period of rapid transition. Shawn Mrzena Company: Microsoft Title and years of experience at the time: Business strategy and sales program leader, spent nearly 26 years at Microsoft and left when he was 56. Offer details: Employees who took the Voluntary Retirement Program were offered up to five years of health insurance coverage, with Microsoft paying for the first year; stock vesting; and base pay determined by seniority and tenure, capped at 39 weeks. Since taking the VRP, Shawn Mrzena has been staying busy with activities he enjoys. Courtesy of Shawn Mrzena I don't get the Sunday night jitters anymore. Shawn Mrzena said he only planned to be in the tech industry for a decade. He ended up working at Microsoft for about 26 years. "I always say that Microsoft was my mini MBA. That's where I learned about business," he said. When Microsoft offered the VRP earlier this year, he had already planned to retire early or pivot to something new. He experienced several technological shifts during his career, and the rapid pace and demand of AI wasn't one he was excited about. "I just decided, 'You know what? I'm going to leave it to the next generation,'" he said. "This is not something I want to invest my energy and time into. I'm not waking up excited in the morning." Mrzena said he's busier now than when he had a job, but busy doing things he wants to do. "I'll purchase something that I'm interested in, like an instructional guide on building something, and I will spend my day fabricating, learning, building, arguing with AI or the book," he said. He is already exploring semi-retirement involving work in the trades. "I've always wanted to learn how to fabricate," he said. "So I still am looking at enrolling in our community college locally here and getting a welder certificate." For those debating a buyout, he said, you already know if it's time, especially if you are unhappy or hanging on by a thread: "This should be a quick decision. You know if you want to stay." Mike RothKugel Company: Microsoft Title and years of experience at the time: Principal group product manager for Microsoft Teams, spent more than 23 non-continuous years at the company and left when he was 56. Offer details: Employees who took the Voluntary Retirement Program were offered up to five years of health insurance coverage, with Microsoft paying for the first year; stock vesting; and base pay determined by seniority and tenure, capped at 39 weeks. Mike RothKugel enjoys fishing. Courtesy of Mike RothKugel The tech industry is obviously very different now than it was three years ago. And there's a lot of uncertainty. Mike RothKugel worked at Microsoft for over 20 years, with a few years at Google in the 2010s. For his last nine years at Microsoft, he worked on the Teams product group. He said he was planning on retiring in about two years when the VRP was offered earlier this year. After discussing it with his financial advisor, his thoughts turned to time. "I missed a lot of time with my kids growing up, and I was like, 'Hey, maybe I can get some of that time back right now, at least spend a little bit more time with them while they're still around, they're still in college, they're still on my payroll,'" he said. He said the VRP's healthcare offering — first year of healthcare paid for by Microsoft, followed by four years of COBRA at the person's expense — was a big factor. "That really helped put my mind at ease a bit," he said. "It'll still end before I'm eligible for Medicare or anything, but we'll cross that bridge when we get to it." Before the VRP, RothKugel's family bought a vacation house in Roche Harbor on San Juan Island, Washington. His kids are at college, so he spends his days with his wife and bulldog. He's been remodeling the San Juan home, taking calls for consulting and advisory work, and helping promote his sister's distillery, the Striped Pig in Charleston, South Carolina. "I've started to get a little bit of that itch of I really want to get back into work, but I'm avoiding that," he said. "I'm going to try to avoid that for as long as possible." Bobbie Ziemer Company: Verizon Title and years of experience: Senior client partner in business development, spent six years at the company and left when she was 54. Offer details: 3 weeks of pay per year of service, and continued healthcare for the number of weeks of separation pay, rounded up to the nearest whole number. Bobbie Ziemer wasn't prepared for such a grueling job search. Courtesy of Bobbie Ziemer In my mind, the money that I would accept from taking the separation package would put me way ahead. I never anticipated that I would ultimately spend that over nine months trying to get a job. When Bobbie Ziemer saw Verizon's voluntary exit offer in summer 2024, she knew what the severance money would go toward: the remaining college tuition for two of her kids and her nest egg. A self-identified "grinder," Ziemer said she'd never spent more than a few weeks without a job and assumed this time would be no different. After weighing whether she was OK with never working at Verizon again, as the package stipulated, Ziemer decided to stop working at the company in early September. Ziemer immediately started updating her résumé and reaching out to her network. Yet her optimism and funds dwindled as the job search dragged on, leading her to sign up for Medicaid and forgo luxuries like the hair salon. "It made me second-guess my talent, second-guess my experience, second-guess my value, second-guess my performance and achievements," Ziemer said. She landed a job in June but was recently laid off. Despite the challenges, Ziemer said she doesn't regret taking the buyout offer . Anyone considering a similar choice should take an honest look at their finances, she said, and prepare for more unemployment than they'd want.
