# Anna Brockman, Wife to One of the “Kings of AI,” Is a Republican Mega-Donor. What Is It Like to Be Queen?

Vanity Fair · vanityfairmagazine · 2026-11-03

It was weeks before the election and spirits were low in MAGA’s top ranks. The president’s approval ratings had plunged to historic depths. Polling suggested his endorsement had become an albatross for many of his preferred candidates. And so Donald Trump had retreated to the gilded comfort of Mar-a-Lago to attend to “matters of national importance,” including, on October 17, a candlelit dinner with some of his most loyal supporters.
At the head of the table were just four seats: Google cofounder Sergey Brin, President Trump himself, OpenAI cofounder Greg Brockman, and his wife, Anna Brockman.
The Brockmans, worth nearly $30 billion, have used their wealth to become the 16th-biggest political mega-donors this midterms cycle: Last year, the couple donated a combined $25 million to MAGA Inc. and $25 million to the pro-AI super PAC Leading the Future. Greg has said that he and his wife are “single-issue donors,” their lone cause being AI, the technology he’s helped build. “It’s not popular,” he recently acknowledged, so “we really want to support politicians who really lean into [it].” And it has been “we” at each of these turns—the Brockmans working in tandem toward a shared vision of the future.
Yet despite Anna Brockman’s frequent presence amid the backdrop of this pivotal scene in history, next to nothing has previously been reported about her. When The Wall Street Journal profiled Greg Brockman in May, they wrote of his elusive wife, “She is intensely private: almost no information, including her maiden name, is available online.”
Anna Chang has an unlikely past—one that raises some new questions. How did a onetime registered Democrat and ambitious tech worker who “loves everything that positively changes the world” become the retired wife of one of the wealthiest men on earth, Donald Trump’s frequent guest, and one of the single-largest donors seeking to hasten a future where artificial intelligence is developed with minimal regulatory oversight?
The partners of billionaires have long been appointed to roles distributing their spouses’ wealth through philanthropic and political giving. These women, often brilliant in their own right, become trusted confidantes and strategic advisers. But they also find themselves in paradoxical positions, often wishing for the personal privacy of a private citizen while wielding immense power through their wealth and stature.
Sources close to the Brockmans and to OpenAI suggested that Anna is no bystander, and is worthy of scrutiny as a significant player herself. “She’s not a silent sidekick to Greg,” says one AI industry insider. “She is a force.”
When Greg and Anna met at a Silicon Valley dinner party in 2018, Greg said they “instantly connected.” She was the daughter of Korean immigrants, and he had a long-standing reverence for Korean culture: He has a black belt in tae kwon do, and even attended Korean summer camp as a teenager, picking up some of the language. They had also both worked at the payments-software start-up Stripe in the 2010s, though their tenures never overlapped. (Both Greg and Anna Brockman declined Vanity Fair’s request for comment for this story.)
In 2015, Greg had left his position as Stripe’s chief technology officer to cofound OpenAI with 10 fellow visionaries out of his San Francisco living room. A gifted and relentless coder, he muscled his way through seemingly impossible projects in the hopes of eventually achieving artificial general intelligence, or AGI (the point when the technology can match or exceed human-level capabilities at every possible task). He sometimes clocked 90-hour workweeks. Greg’s colleagues from over the years describe him as fiercely competitive, not just with work projects but also with hobbies like running and weight lifting.
Anna was a quirky and exuberant animal lover from Texas—one source estimates she and her husband live with over 20 animals—who had worked for AmeriCorps mentoring working-class students after graduating from Wesleyan University, well known as an ultraliberal school. (In a total coincidence, I discovered midway through reporting that Anna and I had overlapped there during our college years.) When she became one of the first 100 or so employees at Dropbox, her idealism persisted. In an introduction to her new colleagues, she told them she spent her free time “taking on adventures to help save the world.”
While a source close to Anna said she doesn’t drink alcohol, sources who’ve spent time with her said she has sometimes drawn attention for her juvenile humor and unconventional manner. She “curses like a drunken sailor,” said one. Another described her as “fratty.”
Early in the Brockmans’ relationship, Anna experienced a series of mysterious health mishaps. The couple spent years visiting doctor after doctor, and eventually discovered she had Hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome (hEDS). Greg has also said that she has Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (POTS) and Mast Cell Activation Syndrome (MCAS). She believed that with the right AI tools, her arduous investigation might have been made much simpler. “Her whole passion for AI is driven by this medical journey,” said her friend and former colleague Sujay Jaswa.
In her late 30s, Anna was also diagnosed with autism. “Her autism can come at a cost, one of which is requiring a lot of alone time (the fur kids and myself excluded) and downtime to rest and recover,” her husband wrote, referring to their many pets. “A beautiful trait of her autism is her deep connection to animals and attention to understanding their needs,” Greg posted on X.
He, too, required patience from his partner while he focused on his interior life. During their first holiday season together, he decided to teach himself machine learning, scratching out equations in a notebook. “She was there with me no matter how many planned activities it meant skipping,” he wrote on his blog. He described her as “someone who made me feel it was ok if I failed.” Greg said on the Tetragrammaton With Rick Rubin podcast that they even put off having children so that he could focus all his energy on AI.
Anna’s early adoption of AI tools helped her practically float up the corporate ladder, but it also opened the door to loftier visions. In a 2018 blog post, she wrote, “I believe in the power of AI to allow us to become more human, today and in the future, by alleviating monotony and opening the space for creativity and intuition.”
Meanwhile, Greg sounded like he was experiencing vertigo from the progress they were making at OpenAI. His journals from this period, portions of which were later made public as part of discovery for the Musk v. Altman trial, ricochet between self-doubt and megalomania. “No person should have control over what we’re creating,” he wrote in August 2017. Then three months later, he appeared to copy and paste a chat with an unknown correspondent into the journal document. “It is better for us to become increasingly kings of this industry,” one of them wrote. “Being the kings of AI is not so bad.”
In Anna, he found the ultimate support system. According to the WSJ, he took the other side of a bet with his then OpenAI colleague (and later Anthropic cofounder) Daniela Amodei, who wagered $100 that Anna was not the one. He won. As Greg put it, they “have been inseparable ever since.”
At the Musk v. Altman trial, Anna was by her husband’s side almost every day. Some observers noted that she held his hand right up to the last second as she walked him to the witness stand. Sources who know the couple described Anna, 38, as “very devoted” and Greg, 38, as “the ultimate wife guy.”
When Anna and Greg got married in 2019, they held a civil ceremony in OpenAI’s office officiated by fellow OpenAI cofounder Ilya Sutskever. The ring bearer was a robotic hand.
As wife to the “wife guy” at the helm of one of the most powerful companies on earth, Anna has enjoyed a front row seat to scenes of such intrigue that they have inspired multiple books, and the highly anticipated upcoming film Artificial. (Cooper Hoffman, the actor who plays Brockman in the film Artificial, recalls director Luca Guadagnino’s guidance on how to approach the role: “Greg is all-American,” Guadagnino told him. “He’s like a cheeseburger.”)
While it isn’t uncommon for spouses and partners to turn up in the offices of start-ups, sources who’ve spent time with the Brockmans noted that by the late 2010s Anna was unusually engaged in his work life, having left her own job at the venture capital firm Basis Set Ventures. While, per The Information, Anna has never been formally employed at OpenAI, over the years she has appeared in live product demos; accompanied executives on trips, like one to South Korea to meet with local start-ups; and even been spotted at high-level meetings at the OpenAI office.
When OpenAI’s top executives were appointed drivers and private security, Anna would sometimes be spotted in the car with Greg, taking him to work, according to former colleagues.
As the money piled up, the relationships between the company’s early team—including Sam Altman, Greg Brockman, Ilya Sutskever, Jakub Pachocki, Mira Murati, and Dario and Daniela Amodei—began to fray. While the imaginations of many of their Bay Area peers ran wild with sci-fi visions of uploaded consciousness and unfathomable abundance, and AI doomsayers warned of extinction events, the Brockmans’ hopes for an AI future felt relatively pedestrian.
Greg was firmly in the pro-commercialization camp, according to OpenAI researchers who’ve worked with him, believing that above all, AI can empower individuals to transform their own lives. Anna, meanwhile, is driven by the “idea that AI for all is gonna take all these major social problems, like the extreme cost of health care and education [and] extreme inequality in access…and it’s gonna democratize all of it,” according to Jaswa.
And to do that, the Brockmans understood that AI leaders needed to win over the policymakers that, in their view, could stand in the way of AGI.
Following the release of ChatGPT in 2022, OpenAI’s founding team found themselves on the other side of overnight fame—at least in the insular tech bubble of the Bay Area. While some of the more socially awkward researchers chafed at the attention, Greg—who’d been promoted to president of the company—and Anna have seemed to bask in it.
The Brockmans have dined with Sylvester Stallone at the Kennedy Center, attended black-tie events at the White House, and even walked the blue carpet at the Met Gala, where Anna wore a black-and-white ruffled gown by Korean designer Ashlynn Park.
The power she’d been quietly amassing by Greg’s side played out in a more significant and dramatic way in November 2023, when the board of OpenAI removed Sam Altman from his position as CEO over concerns that he was untrustworthy. Sutskever, who was on the board, also compiled a memo detailing allegations against Greg, and Greg was removed from his position as chairman of the board (but allowed to stay on as president). When the board called Greg to inform him of their decision, the first thing he did was talk to his wife. He told her, “‘We have to leave.’ And she said, ‘Yes, we do,’” he said on the Tetragrammaton With Rick Rubin podcast. He warned her they would have to be prepared for his equity to disappear if he quit. “‘Still want to do it?’ And she said yes.”
But when the board began to reconsider its decision, Sutskever became the deciding vote. In an oft-repeated piece of OpenAI lore, Anna allegedly begged Sutskever in a tearful encounter to reconsider. (Sources familiar with the events have suggested that the impact of this encounter has been overstated.) According to the WSJ, when Sutskever decided to reverse his decision, Anna left him a cactus as a gift.
After a long negotiation over his place at the company, Sutskever left to start his own venture, Safe Superintelligence. “There were some deep and bitter things” that went down during this period, according to one high-level employee at the time.
After this dramatic inflection point, which has come to be known as the blip, Greg took a three-month leave of absence. This period allowed him to dedicate himself to a project he was passionate about: developing a biological foundation model for genome modeling and design at the Arc Institute—“a domain that’s very personally meaningful to both me and and to my wife,” he has said.
Meanwhile, the company had replaced nearly all the board members who’d risen up against Altman with what one former senior OpenAI employee called a “more loyal and ‘normie’” board who were supportive of the company’s commercial push.
The center of gravity began to shift in Greg’s direction, solidifying his place as Altman’s second in command, soon to be tasked with an even more fraught assignment: building political goodwill. What wouldn’t the billionaire couple do to help the technology that they consider vital to humanity’s future?
“He thinks big, but he is also very practical, kind of a realist,” Peter Welinder, OpenAI’s vice president and general manager, says of Greg. Others felt that his techno-optimism, and subsequent push into Washington to defend those ideals, is “naivete,” says the former high-level employee. “I don’t think he’s very carefully thinking through the political and power implications.”
When Donald Trump was reelected in late 2024, OpenAI found itself facing down a new threat. Elon Musk, an original cofounder of the company who left in 2018, had grown increasingly antagonistic toward his onetime colleagues while also growing close to the incoming administration. According to Kevin Roose’s The AGI Chronicles, OpenAI, and Altman in particular, feared political retribution from Musk, who now had his own competitor company in xAI, if he was allowed to take up all the oxygen in Washington. At the same time, OpenAI’s need for energy and compute had grown so insatiable that the company had no choice but to seek the government’s help. In an administration run on quid pro quo, OpenAI would have to make nice.
Brockman found himself uniquely well-positioned to lead the infrastructure-scaling bonanza, thanks to a preexisting relationship with Secretary of the Interior Doug Burgum, who hailed from his home state of North Dakota. Greg was also “the most credible” face of the political donations, said one former OpenAI employee.
And so Greg and Anna Brockman became White House mainstays. They have attended state dinners for foreign leaders like Saudi crown prince Mohammed bin Salman and Xi Jinping, and joined top tech executives at a White House event about the future of AI. Brockman, rather than Altman, flanked Trump during the signing of the White House Accord on Super Intelligence in September.
When the political pendulum began to swing back this summer, it began to appear as though whoever had made the call on those political donations might have made a severe miscalculation. Following a steady drumbeat of negative headlines about Leading the Future’s work and significant internal dissent among OpenAI employees, the Brockmans rescinded their second planned $25 million donation to the PAC. Greg wrote in a Slack message, obtained by The New York Times, that Leading the Future had become a “distraction” from OpenAI’s work.
When I reached out to OpenAI to discuss the Brockmans’ political giving, it directed me back to the Brockmans’ team. In an interview this October with Vanity Fair’s global editorial director Mark Guiducci, Altman said, “I did not ask him to donate or stop donating. He can make his own personal decision.” It seems the Brockmans’ donations are a hot potato no one is eager to catch.
Regardless, their $25 million checks have helped provide the funds to enable a torrent of preelection ads, including warnings like, “This is the final battle” to “liberate America from these villains, once and for all.”
Some observers of the company have come to believe that the plan was “to have Greg be the scapegoat if/when the Democrats come back into power, but to allow OpenAI to privately (with the White House) claim credit for the MAGA PAC donations with the Trump administration while privately (with employees) distance itself from them,” according to the former senior employee. (OpenAI did not respond to a request for comment.)
Meanwhile, Greg has returned more of his focus to the product realm, and he and his wife have found a less controversial way to spend their millions. When the couple found out that the Big Bear Valley habitat of two publicly beloved eagles, Jackie and Shadow, was under threat, they donated over $5.5 million to preserve the land this summer. “[Jackie and Shadow] inspire perseverance and a sense of connectedness to something greater,” Anna said of their donation.
“It was clear in talking to her that she loved her husband very much and that she loved the eagles,” says Jenny Voisard, the communications and PR leader for Friends of Big Bear Valley, of her experience working with Anna on the donation. They never talked about politics.
