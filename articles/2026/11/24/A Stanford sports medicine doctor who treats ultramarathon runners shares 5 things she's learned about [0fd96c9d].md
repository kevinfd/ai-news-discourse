# A Stanford sports medicine doctor who treats ultramarathon runners shares 5 things she's learned about pushing through pain

Business Insider · Jessica Orwig, Jessica Orwig (jorwig@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-24

Man runner running and climbing over mountain cliff. Extreme jogging in remote location rbkomar/Getty Images Dr. Emily Kraus explains how ultramarathon runners reframe shared pain to push beyond limits Research at Western States shows gut training and carb fueling help ultrarunners avoid energy risks Studies find ultramarathon hallucinations and brain fatigue make support crews vital for safety At a 100-mile ultramarathon , the body can start doing strange things. Runners stumble into aid stations depleted and disoriented. Some hallucinate. Others keep moving through pain that would force most people to quit. Dr. Emily Kraus has seen it all from the medical tent. A Stanford sports medicine physician who has helped care for ultramarathoners, she has spent years watching athletes find a way through the point when their minds and bodies are begging them to stop. However, she says the real lesson isn't simply how much pain they can take. It's how they learn to interpret it — and when even the toughest runners need to listen to it. Here are five insights Kraus shared with me into what keeps extreme endurance athletes going. 1. Pain can be easier to bear when it isn't faced alone Drawing strength from others may help. Emanuele Cremaschi/Getty Images During Project FURTHER, a multiday ultramarathon event where women attempted to run farther than ever before, Kraus and other researchers noticed that some runners had found a way to make their suffering feel more manageable. They did it by letting the people around them into their experience — telling crews and fellow runners when they were hurting, accepting encouragement, and drawing strength from seeing others endure the same hard miles. They "reframed the pain as a collective experience," Kraus said, who served as a co-medical director for the event. Pain became less isolating when it was acknowledged out loud, rather than carried alone, Kraus added. That shift — from I'm suffering to we're getting through this together — appeared to help them keep moving. It became "a safe discomfort that we're willing to push through," said Kraus, who directs Stanford's FASTR program — short for Female Athlete Science and Translational Research — through the Wu Tsai Human Performance Alliance . 2. Embrace the 'pain cave' It's all about how you perceive the pain. doble-d/Getty Images Kraus said ultramarathon pain rarely rises in a straight line. Over the course of a 100-mile race, runners can move through what she calls "the lowest of lows" — then, a few hours later, find themselves in "the highest of highs." That's one reason she points to ultramarathon legend Courtney Dauwalter's idea of the "pain cave." Dauwalter has described imagining herself putting on a hard hat, picking up a chisel, and carving deeper into the cave — expanding her capacity for the next time she hits a rough patch. "I'm not going to let this pain cave slow me down or shut me down," she said, describing Dauwalter's mindset. "I'm going to relish in it and just be in this pain cave through the next several hours." There's some research behind the broader idea. A study of multistage ultramarathoners found that runners who relied less on maladaptive pain-coping strategies were more likely to finish the race. A recent review also found that ultrarunners tend to expect and accept pain more readily than the general population — though researchers are still working out exactly how much that affects performance. 3. To go farther, train your gut Everyone's gut and digestion is a little different. Caia Image/Getty Images At a certain point in an ultramarathon, eating can feel almost as hard as running. After hours on the trail, runners may be nauseated, unable to stomach another gel, or simply too depleted to recognize they need calories. "Fueling has been the biggest unlock for a lot of athletes," Kraus said. "And that requires a lot of planning and preparation to make sure that your gut is trained for that level of carbohydrate intake." Some runners may consume as many as 20 or 30 gels during a race; others need liquids or different carbohydrate mixes that their stomachs can handle. In a small study of runners at a 100-mile mountain race, finishers consumed significantly more carbohydrates per hour than runners who dropped out: about 0.98 grams per kilogram of body weight per hour, compared with 0.56 grams. 4. Don't judge a book by its cover Ultramarathons are harder on the body than some realize. The Good Brigade/Getty Images Kraus's research makes one point clear: Running huge distances does not automatically mean an athlete is healthy. Her team studied more than 120 runners at the 100-mile Western States Endurance Run and found that many showed warning signs that they might not be eating enough to support the demands of their training. Nearly two-thirds of women and about a third of men had moderate or high risk for problems involving low energy, disrupted hormones, and bone health. Some runners, for example, reported stress fractures even without low bone density. Kraus said the research is a reminder that ultrarunners can seem extraordinarily healthy while still carrying risks that may not show up until much later. 5. Your brain can become your biggest liability Sleep deprivation can be a major problem in ultras. Goodboy Picture Company/Getty Images Imagine running through the mountains in the middle of the night. You've been awake for hours, your legs are exhausted, and suddenly you start seeing things that are out of the ordinary. One study of more than 1,100 mountain ultramarathon finishers found that roughly a third reported experiencing hallucinations during races. The longer the race went on, the more common hallucinations became: just 5.6% of runners racing for one night reported them, compared with 27.7% of those racing for two nights and 42.1% of those racing for three. The danger goes beyond seeing things that aren't there, though, Kraus said. "Whether it's low sodium, low sugar, even a concussion that's starting to really show some of the effects." Sleep deprivation can also make it harder to recognize an injury or navigate dangerous terrain. Even something as ordinary as a twisted ankle could conceal a fracture. Kraus said this is where support crews become essential. They can recognize warning signs an athlete might overlook while focused on finishing.
