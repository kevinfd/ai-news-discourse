# Behind the Curtain: When reality shouts

Axios · Jim VandeHei, Mike Allen · 2026-11-24

We naturally assume people in business and power are slightly full of it, spinning us wildly to exaggerate their achievements or capabilities. In the case of AI and its ability to do irreparable damage to society, it's time for all of us — the media, the White House and Congress — to believe them: They've truly built something we don't understand and soon, if not already, can't fully control. Why it matters: This is no longer a future problem. It's a today problem, if not a crisis. You see this with near-daily glitches at airports or banks. You see this in our reporting about two dozen researchers inside these companies claiming their AI is too powerful and uncontrollable to release. And you see this in AI execs' own on-the-record warnings that we're at or near the point of the technology surpassing comprehension or control. Just believe them. If we wait for a cyberattack on the U.S. electricity grid or some bad actor to release a pathogen at an elementary school, it's too late. We'll panic and make rushed, emotional decisions and bad policies. It's not too late to prevent this, while still getting the upside benefits of AI. In our conversations with hundreds of people involved in building and regulating AI, there's shockingly wide agreement about the dangers ahead — and ways to protect against the worst outcomes. There's just no mechanism or urgency to bring the right mix of people together to do this. The steps seem clear: Make this a society-wide, urgent call to action. This should be an all-hands-on-deck moment, the only topic Congress prioritizes short-term. Yes, it probably requires presidential leadership, and Trump is reluctant to slow things down. But his top cyber officials privately agree with us: This demands fast, smart, bipartisan action with heavy private sector involvement. Let 'er rip no longer feels like a plausible plan. Focus on safety and cyber first. Most leading AI companies would gladly slow down a bit, prioritize verifiable safety standards, and even accept government oversight with teeth if their rivals did. They'd also allocate money and time to help critical government agencies and businesses fortify software systems from cyberattacks. But someone, either Trump's AI team or the top leaders in Congress, needs to force them. Level with the American public. As a nation and as business leaders, we're doing a spectacularly terrible job of explaining this technology to the public. There's a reason that the most bipartisan topic in America is a shared loathing of data centers. People hate AI. Most simply don't see it as something that will make their life better. We need leaders who can discuss the good, bad and ugly of AI in real time, nearly every day. A society-wide project needs to involve society. Go deeper ... "AI 2027: The reckoning."
