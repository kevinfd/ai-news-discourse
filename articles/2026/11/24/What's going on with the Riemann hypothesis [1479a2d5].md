# What's going on with the Riemann hypothesis?

Ars Technica · John Timmer · 2026-11-24

Last week, news broke that a mathematician had used AI to help crack one of the biggest outstanding problems in math: a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. The news follows several other major breakthroughs in math that have either been enabled by or managed entirely by large language model-based systems, a development that has left the entire field of mathematics in a state of upset.
It’s too early to say much about the proof itself—mathematicians are still in the process of digesting it. But we can say a little about how the proof came about, and what it tells us about the state of math.
Tackling Riemann
The Riemann hypothesis dates from the mid-1800s and was formulated by (you will be surprised to learn) Bernhard Riemann. There’s not an obvious way to describe the details of the actual hypothesis simply, but it has implications for the distribution of prime numbers. This gives it significance for areas ranging from number theory to cryptography. As Wikipedia accurately states, “It is considered by pure mathematicians to be one of the most important problems in the field.”
The proof of the hypothesis was announced by a mathematician named Levent Alpöge, who has been working at Anthropic, and, not surprisingly, used Claude to generate it. His announcement is written in a somewhat florid style (example: “one of which was already new and shocking, making very simple one of the shadows of Riemann over which we mathematicians felt most triumph”), and he has apparently dropped some hints that some of it was meant in jest.
