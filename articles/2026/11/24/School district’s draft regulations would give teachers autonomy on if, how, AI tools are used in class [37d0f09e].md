# School district’s draft regulations would give teachers autonomy on if, how, AI tools are used in class

Bethesda Magazine · Ashlyn Campbell · 2026-11-24

Montgomery County Public Schools (MCPS) released its draft regulation on artificial intelligence Nov. 17, rules that would allow educators to determine if, when and how students can use AI in the classroom.
The regulation, which will be open for public comment until Dec. 18, comes amid calls from parents to ban AI tools in the district, following in the footsteps of school districts such as Frederick County, New York and Los Angeles.
It also comes after a number of developments concerning AI in education, including lawsuits alleging negative mental health impacts due to AI chatbots on school-issued devices, and Google announcing that its search function would not be able to be used in schools without AI tools included.
“The combination of both the policy and the regulations for MCPS just fails to meet the moment,” Betsy Tao, head of the Technology Committee for the Montgomery County Council of PTAs and one of the parents advocating for the ban, told Bethesda Today Wednesday morning. “This is not the kind of policy and regulations we need on AI.”
In a statement to Bethesda Today on Monday, MCPS spokesperson Liliana López said the draft regulation “establishes safeguards for the ethical, responsible and secure use of AI.”
“The priority is, absolutely, to protect students while ensuring AI supports, rather than replaces, meaningful teaching and learning,” López said.
MCPS AI policy, regulation
The school board adopted an updated version of its educational technology policy in May, which outlined some of its expectations surrounding the use of the technology within schools. The policy “recognizes the transformative potential of artificial intelligence” and that the tools are intended to support learning and teaching, but ultimately directs the superintendent to establish regulations to implement the policy.
López told Bethesda Magazine that the regulations would be in place by mid to late September. The 11-page draft of the AI regulations was released Nov. 17.
The draft outlines ways AI can be used by staff and students and addresses issues such as data privacy, vetting and AI literacy instruction.
The regulation explicitly prohibits AI in some areas, including entering personal identifiable information of students and staff into unapproved AI tools, using AI to make a final decision about a student or employee without human review, and collecting biometric, behavioral or location data through AI.
It prohibits the use of AI for the creation of content such as “deepfakes,” or video or voice cloning that falsely portrays someone else, spreads misinformation or misrepresents academic work. Vendors are also not allowed to use district data for AI model training or algorithm development; for systems relying on cloud or generative platforms, “data shall be anonymized or de-identified to the fullest extent possible.”
The regulation frequently references the state’s Artificial Intelligence Ready Schools Act, which took effect in June. The law directs school districts to establish AI policies for their classrooms.
The regulation states that AI tools should be vetted using the Maryland State Department of Education Artificial Intelligence Tool Evaluation Rubric, to make sure tools meet standards for data privacy, pedagogy and safety.
According to the policy, educators can determine “if, when and how MCPS approved AI tools are used in their classrooms.” The MCPS Division of Teaching and Learning would provide guidance, according to the draft, on outlining when and how AI use must be disclosed in student work, clarifying expectations for syllabi and defining and communicating expectations for families.
The regulation states that students and staff must cite and acknowledge when AI is used in their work and that AI generated work can’t replace “original output.”
Students would also participate in learning opportunities on building skills related to AI literacy and the responsible use of AI. Library media specialists would be the key contacts in each building for AI literacy.
According to the regulations, AI tools can be used to assist with instructional planning, data analysis and administrative tasks. Automated systems shouldn’t be used to make “final consequential decisions” on such areas as grading, program placement, academic tracking and special education eligibility without human oversight.
Staff also aren’t allowed to only rely on technologies that identify the use of generative AI for academic dishonesty, “given their documented unreliability.”
AI developments raise concerns in education world
The release of the MCPS AI regulation comes amid concerns from school districts across the country after Google announced Nov. 3 that it would restrict access to its search function if school districts reject AI tools in its K-12 software.
Several lawsuits have also been filed against Google and other AI tools over impacts of the tools on student mental health, including one lawsuit that alleges a 13-year-old made plans to end her life after talking with a chatbot-like feature on her school-issued laptop.
In September, the Wall Street Journal reported that a former student at John F. Kennedy High School in Silver Spring began using a chatbot on her school-issued Chromebook first for homework, then as a confidante.
The developments spurred a coalition of DMV parents, including those in Montgomery County, to issue a joint statement calling for school districts to issue a moratorium on student-facing AI.
“We must adopt a ‘do no harm’ approach,” The statement said. “Products used in education must earn their place in our classrooms and center children’s well-being and educational outcomes.”
Tao, one of the parents involved in the coalition, noted that the move came after some of the largest school districts fought the use of AI in classrooms.
“This push of AI into schools … really doesn’t benefit student learning because there’s no proof that it does,” Tao said.
Parents aren’t the only ones raising concerns.
Catherine Bucsa, a senior and the online editor-in-chief of the Wootton High student newspaper, the Common Sense, wrote an op-ed in September criticizing an increase of AI-created art and posters from staff within her school.
“I had just noticed such a huge influx in the AI graphics in the school … it was very clear, like the first template of what ChatGPT gave them was being published,” Busca told Bethesda Today. “It used to be the teachers were very hard and fast, no AI, no AI, no AI. … So it’s kind of almost disheartening in a way, it’s like the people that are supposed to be caring the most about these things are going against their own word.”
Bucsa said she thinks AI should be regulated — how and when students use it, and how old students are when they are given access — rather than banned outright.
While some are advocating for an outright ban, some experts said a different approach may be helpful, including focusing on vetting tools, finding more purpose-driven AI learning tools and introducing AI literacy.
Jing Liu, director of the Center for Educational Data Science and Innovation and an associate professor in education policy at the University of Maryland, said concerns over AI replacing critical thought or learning from students is valid, but there may be specific AI-based tools that are useful for education.
Liu pointed to tools like AI tutoring systems, which could look at student mistakes and recommend problems for the student to practice with.
“This is still AI use, but you can see that it’s used in a way that’s supporting student learning rather than replacing student learning,” Liu said. “So I feel when we talk about AI, supporting AI or not supporting AI, we have to have a much more nuanced conversation about how is AI used.”
Still, Liu noted that research is behind in producing evidence about the impact of some AI tools, especially as the field continuously, and quickly, develops.
AI literacy, something schools would be charged with in the MCPS regulation, is something some experts, including Daniel Umana, the manager of the Montgomery College Digital Learning Center, have been trying to address with students.
“A lot of the time we see students [just] taking the answer that the tool gives them and just going with it, and that is one of the things that we teach them not to do,” Umana said. “We teach definitely to investigate and interrogate the answers to be more thorough in their use of the tool.”
Reactions to the MCPS draft policy
Since the regulation came out, Tao has argued that it doesn’t go far enough, and the board should adopt an AI ban similar to Frederick County Public Schools. For Tao, the regulation treats AI as other emerging technology like calculators, when in reality it is “kryptonite to learning,” because it shortcuts critical thinking.
“I think you can look through [the MCPS AI policy], and then kind of ask just a very basic question of, ‘Well, this is very similar to their existing educational technology policy. Has that done anything to curb the wild west that is … the way that students use their Chromebooks in school and how digital products are being managed at MCPS?’ It hasn’t,” Tao said.
Concerning the Google changes and if MCPS was considering making any changes in response, López said the company has not provided MCPS with information on the matter related to Google search and AI.
On Monday, López noted that there are concerns and benefits to AI and the regulation released Nov. 17 was a draft and would be open for public comment until December.
Marissa Walker, the co-chair of the MCCPTA technology committee, is also advocating for a ban on AI. Walker said that the regulation MCPS released feels “careless” to the harms of AI on students.
Walker said the district also didn’t include a system to report any harm from AI tools and while the regulation notes that AI shouldn’t be the final determining factor in many areas, it would be better to not allow AI to be involved in certain processes at all.
When “we’re talking about crunching data, I would be fine with that,” Walker said. “But if we’re talking about … threat assessment or something that really requires human judgment, I don’t think AI should be involved at all.”
Liu said the draft regulation provides a lot of autonomy for educators, which may be beneficial, but also means the district must support and educate teachers on what is good and responsible use of AI.
Tao raised concerns that the amount of autonomy would result in a lack of follow-through.
“This is pushing it down to teachers, which means it’s not going to be effective because there’s no ability to enforce classroom by classroom what’s happening,” Tao said.
What’s next?
While Tao and Walker have argued that the regulation isn’t enough, Tao said the ball is in court of the new school board. Three new members will be joining the school board in December, including Omar Lazo in an at-large seat, Sally McCarthy for District 3 and Elma Lorraine-Diggs for District 1.
Julie Yang, the outgoing board member for District 3, who spearheaded several initiatives on the board concerning education technology, told Bethesda Today that the board hadn’t discussed the recent developments in AI.
“I haven’t completely [made] up my mind about AI in school yet, right? I think the question to ask is: Is it enhancing learning?” Yang said. “I am passing on the resolution to my new colleagues who are coming to the board of education, and so I hope that they will take on this work and continue to ask the question of how we use technology [and] not let the technology just lead the way.”
McCarthy told Bethesda Today on Nov. 17 that AI and technology use was one of her priorities when she joins the board. McCarthy said her current concern with the policy is that technology is ever changing, so there needs to be a timeline for revisions and review.
“That’s the piece that’s missing in the ed tech policy that was adopted in May. And that ed tech policy is, in my view, a little too vast and broad,” McCarthy said. “And I think there’s a lot of emerging data science and brain science around the effects of technology on the developing brain. And I want to make certain that we hold that up there as a priority, specifically when we’re looking at what’s the cost and the benefit to our kids and our staff.”
Tao said AI has to become front and center in order to address the harms it could cause.
“The new board has to take strong action because … their job is to set the policy. … If AI is kryptonite to learning, then it can’t be an afterthought,” Tao said.
