# Threat of Lawsuit Over "AI Music Is Copyright-Free": 4 Actions BGM Sellers Should Take Now

Threat of Lawsuit Over "AI Music Is Copyright-Free": 4 Actions BGM Sellers Should Take Now
Many people likely sell BGM created with Suno on stock websites or to physical stores. "Copyright-free" is an extremely convenient phrase to use as a sales pitch at such times.
However, on November 24, a music copyright collective issued a notice to a company in the Netherlands that was selling AI-generated music using this phrase, stating that they would sue if it didn't stop.
This article summarizes what happened, the ruling behind it, and four things people who sell BGM or songs generated by AI should do right now.
What Happened
According to the news program Nieuwsuur on Dutch public broadcaster NOS, BumaStemra, a Dutch music copyright management organization (an organization similar to JASRAC in Japan), issued an ultimatum to a Dutch company called YourNeuroTune.
- YourNeuroTune provides an AI music subscription for gyms, restaurants, and retail stores.
- On its website, it advertises the music as "rights-free."
- According to BumaStemra, the music is generated using Suno.
- BumaStemra claims, "This company is urging customers to stop paying creators."
- It stated that it will file a lawsuit if sales are not halted by noon on Wednesday (November 25).
YourNeuroTune stated, "We are aware of the notice, but will respond to the substance if a lawsuit is actually filed."
Why They Can Take a Strong Stance: The German Ruling
The basis cited by BumaStemra is the July 31, 2026 ruling by the Munich Regional Court. In a lawsuit where the German copyright collective GEMA sued Suno, GEMA's claims were upheld (the ruling is not yet final).
According to a commentary by the law firm CMS, the court found infringement at all three of the following stages:
- Training: The act of collecting songs for training in the United States
- Memorization: The fact that the songs are "memorized" within the model
- Output: Generating songs that contain identifiable elements of the original tracks
What warrants attention is the perspective on liability for output. What was used in this trial were simple prompts consisting only of lyrics, style, and title, and Suno was held liable for that output. On the other hand, it also states that if a user repeatedly steers the output by crafting prompts to mimic a specific song, a different conclusion could be reached.
In the United States as well, Eight Mile Style, the co-publisher of Eminem's music, added 108 alleged instances of infringement to its lawsuit against Suno on November 19. According to Music Business Worldwide, Suno admitted in court documents for a different case that "users have been able to include artist names in prompts since January 2026."
"Commercial Use Allowed" and "Copyright-Free" Are Completely Different Things
This is the most important point. According to Suno's official help documentation, songs generated under a paid plan (Pro/Premier) are owned by the creator, and commercial use is permitted even after cancellation. Songs generated on a free plan are for non-commercial use only.
However, that same help documentation explicitly states that commercial use rights do not guarantee copyright protection.
In other words, "having commercial use permission from Suno" and "not infringing on anyone's rights" are two completely different matters. In this recent Dutch case, the company was called out precisely for bridging that gap with the single phrase "rights-free."
4 Things People Who Sell BGM and Songs Made with AI Should Do Right Now
1. Do not use terms like "copyright-free" or "rights-free"
Remove these phrases from your product descriptions and profiles. If you write anything, stick strictly to the facts.
Sunoの有料プランで生成し、DAWで編集した楽曲です。
生成AIを使用しています。
2. Keep records from when you created the tracks
Suno's help center states that commercial use rights apply to songs created (downloaded) while enrolled in a paid plan. To ensure you can explain your process later, keep notes for each song detailing the generation date, subscription plan, prompt, and any edits you made yourself.
3. Do not include artist names or song titles in your prompts
As demonstrated by the Munich ruling, prompting the model to emulate a specific song can leave you, the user, potentially liable. In my own workflow, I regularly specify textures using mood or material descriptors (e.g., cheap drum machine, TR-707 style flat dusty) rather than artist names. It is much safer to adopt this approach for songs you intend to sell.
4. Re-read the terms of service of the platforms where you sell
Stock audio sites and distribution services handle AI-generated music differently. If there is a field to declare AI usage, fill it out honestly, and avoid distributing to platforms that do not accept AI-generated music.
Common Mistakes
- Assuming it is acceptable to sell songs created under a free plan simply because you upgraded to a paid plan later (the help center states that commercial rights are not granted retroactively)
- Telling customers that "because it is an AI song, it isn't anyone's copyrighted work"
- Including the name of a hit song in your prompt and selling the result as "[Artist]-style BGM"
Summary
- In the Netherlands, a copyright collective served notice of an impending lawsuit against a company selling Suno-generated music as "rights-free"
- In the background lies a German court ruling that recognized infringement across all stages: training, retention, and output
- Suno's "commercial use permitted" does not equate to "copyright-free." Review your wording, records, and relevant platform terms now before issues arise
