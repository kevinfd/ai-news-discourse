# ​How AI Agents And Human Expertise Can Strengthen Manufacturing Performance

Forbes · Jerry Dolinsky, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-24

Jerry Dolinsky is the CEO of Dozuki, a leading connected worker solution for enterprise-level manufacturing companies.
AI is becoming a critical skill in manufacturing, and employer expectations are changing quickly.
A September 2026 Federal Reserve study found that AI-related skills appeared in 11% of manufacturing job postings, compared with 8% across the broader economy. The researchers also found that manufacturing production jobs requiring AI skills have carried an average advertised wage premium of approximately 30% since 2023.
When I visit manufacturing facilities, I see why this matters. Many organizations struggle to capture that expertise and make it available across their workforce. AI agents offer an opportunity to address this challenge. They can help manufacturers document expertise, develop employee skills, identify procedural inconsistencies and support quality improvement.
Starting With The Knowledge Employees Already Have
Every manufacturing facility depends on experienced employees who understand the details of production. Operators know how equipment behaves under different conditions, technicians recognize early signs of mechanical problems, quality specialists understand which process variations deserve closer attention and trainers know where new employees struggle and which explanations help them become proficient.
Much of this expertise remains difficult to access. Critical information may live in employees’ heads, outdated documents or disconnected training materials. Capturing it requires time from the same people who are needed on the production floor.
AI can help manufacturers turn recorded demonstrations, existing documents and technical information into structured work instructions. Specialized agents can then evaluate those instructions for clarity, compare approved procedures and flag inconsistencies that require human review.
Consider an experienced technician who understands a complex equipment changeover. They demonstrate the process while a colleague records it. AI helps organize the recording into preliminary visual instructions. A qualified employee reviews the draft, corrects inaccuracies, adds missing details and approves the finished procedure. A content quality agent can identify ambiguous language or inconsistent terminology that deserves additional attention.
The technician supplies the expertise. AI helps make that expertise accessible to others.
For manufacturing leaders, the business value extends beyond faster documentation. Reducing authoring time allows experienced employees to devote more attention to validating procedures, developing co-workers and improving operations.
Keeping Human Judgment At The Center
Manufacturers should avoid treating AI-generated instructions as production-ready simply because they appear complete. A system can organize procedural steps and identify differences between documents. Qualified employees must determine whether the information accurately reflects approved procedures and actual operating conditions.
Imagine a quality engineer using an AI agent to compare inspection procedures across multiple facilities. The agent identifies differences in measurement sequences, equipment settings and acceptance criteria. Some differences may indicate inconsistent standards. Others may reflect legitimate variations in equipment, materials or customer requirements. The engineer evaluates the findings and determines whether changes are necessary. This distinction becomes especially important in regulated or quality-sensitive environments.
The lesson is straightforward: AI can accelerate documentation and analysis, while experienced employees retain responsibility for technical accuracy and approval.
Connecting AI Agents To Workforce Development
Once manufacturers establish reliable digital procedures, they can use that knowledge to improve training. Training agents can help organize approved instructions into role-specific learning activities. Assessment agents can support knowledge checks. Skills agents can analyze qualification records to identify employees who may need additional instruction.
Consider a manufacturer introducing a revised inspection procedure. A change agent could identify differences between the previous and current instructions. A training agent could help prepare updated learning materials. A skills agent could identify affected roles and employees who require reassessment. Supervisors would review those recommendations, assign training and verify employee competency. This creates a stronger connection between changing production requirements and workforce readiness.
It also gives manufacturing leaders a practical way to approach AI literacy. Employees who use AI-enabled workflows need to understand how to retrieve approved information, recognize inaccurate recommendations and know when to involve qualified personnel. Manufacturers can develop those skills alongside existing technical training.
How Manufacturing Leaders Can Put AI Agents To Work
1. Selecting A Process With A Measurable Business Impact
Choose an operation with lengthy training requirements, recurring defects, inconsistent instructions or heavy dependence on experienced employees. Document current performance before introducing AI. Useful measures include instruction authoring time, time to competency, first-pass yield, rework and training assessment results.
Define the improvement you want to achieve and assign an operational owner.
2. Preparing The Knowledge Before Introducing Agents
Review existing instructions for accuracy, completeness and revision status. Identify which documents are approved and which require updating. Experienced operators, trainers and quality specialists should be involved in validating the information.
This preparation matters because an agent can produce convincing recommendations from incomplete or outdated records. A common implementation failure is to connect AI to large collections of documents without establishing which information employees should trust.
3. Introducing One Agent-Assisted Workflow
Start with a narrow application, such as converting recorded demonstrations into instructions or comparing procedures across production lines. Define what the agent should produce, what information it can access and who must review its output.
Test the workflow using actual production examples. Record inaccuracies, missing information and situations where the agent requires additional context.
4. Training Employees And Establishing Accountability
Explain how AI assistance fits into existing responsibilities and show employees how to identify questionable outputs and escalate concerns. If resistance arises, involve front-line experts in workflow design and demonstrating how the technology supports their work.
Establish permissions for sensitive operational information, maintain revision controls and define who can approve procedural changes.
5. Measuring Results And Expanding Deliberately
Compare pilot results with the original baseline and evaluate both efficiency and effectiveness. Faster documentation matters, but approved instructions must also be accurate and useful. Faster training matters when employees can demonstrate the required skills.
Review the findings with operations, quality and training leaders. If the pilot delivers measurable value, extend the approach to similar processes and establish a repeatable method for training employees, maintaining knowledge and evaluating AI performance.
That’s a practical foundation for bringing human expertise and intelligent assistance together in manufacturing.
