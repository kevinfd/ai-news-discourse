# The AI hardware loop that threatens Nvidia's future, even with Jensen Huang back

Business Insider · Geoff Weiss,Stephen Council, Geoff Weiss,Stephen Council (gweiss@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-24

Nvidia cofounder and recently reinstated CEO Jensen Huang Markus Schreiber/AP Think the Jensen scandal is bad for Nvidia? A powerful new AI research loop could be worse. OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic are using AI models to design AI chips that may replace Nvidia GPUs. Nvidia's answer was to bet heavily on open models. Jensen Huang's scandal shook that ecosystem. While Jensen Huang is back at Nvidia , a bigger threat is emerging from the very AI technology he helped unleash. Some of the company's biggest customers, including OpenAI , Anthropic , and Google , are using AI to help design their own chips. That reduces their dependence on Nvidia's hardware and threatens the company's position in the AI trade. The industry has been chattering all year about recursive self-improvement, or RSI . This is where an AI system improves its successors' capabilities, and those gains are used to make further progress. This has mostly happened in the software realm. Now, a similar dynamic is coming for hardware. The smarter these companies' AI models become, the better they get at the complex work of designing next-generation AI chips. This could create a cycle that eats into Nvidia's dominance, no matter who is CEO. OpenAI's chip breakthrough OpenAI CEO Sam Altman. Jeff Chiu/AP OpenAI is becoming an Nvidia competitor in plain sight. In a November 18 research report , the ChatGPT developer described how its AI models are becoming increasingly skilled at designing chips. OpenAI started using AI to help develop a custom chip, called Jalapeño, in early 2025. In chip design, improvements often come from finding ways to fit more circuits and calculations into less space. OpenAI's models cut the space required for two important parts of the chip by roughly 10% and 13%, according to the company. In one case, AI redesigned a circuit to use about 15% less space while halving the time it took to perform its calculations. OpenAI also worked with Nvidia rival AMD to improve existing chips, and reported gains of up to 14% on some measures of performance and efficiency. AMD has incorporated some of those improvements into the designs it's sent for manufacturing. OpenAI also tested successive generations of its AI models on two publicly available processor designs, instructing them to make the processors run faster. The results point to a strength for OpenAI: newer, more powerful models generally produced faster processors. The models actually performed even better when told to design processors from written instructions rather than simply improve existing designs, the company said. That suggests AI can begin to take on more of the fundamental work of designing chips. Google got there first Demis Hassabis, chair of Google DeepMind. Paulo Nunes dos Santos/Bloomberg via Getty Images Google has been pursuing this approach for years. In 2024, its DeepMind research division described AlphaChip , an AI system that helps arrange the components inside processors. The company said AlphaChip could complete work in hours that previously took engineers weeks or months. After chips fueled AI progress, "AlphaChip returns the favor by using AI to accelerate and optimize chip design," the researchers wrote. Google has used the technology to help design every generation of its own AI chips, known as TPUs, since 2020. Those chips let Google train and run its Gemini AI models efficiently. They're also a business in themselves. Late last year, Anthropic signed on for a TPU deal worth tens of billions of dollars, solidifying the chips as a major challenger to Nvidia's GPUs. Anthropic's new chip team Dario Amodei (left), CEO of Anthropic, with Daniela Amodei, president of the company. Getty Images Anthropic is moving in the same direction as OpenAI. The Claude developer told Business Insider in August that it was building an in-house chip design team, and engineers from OpenAI and Google have already come aboard. In the lead-up to Anthropic's IPO this month, it released its most advanced AI model yet and said that it showed major improvement in designing electronic circuits. Anthropic takes this capability seriously. It only blocks a few areas of work on its most advanced models — one of them is building operating systems for some specialized AI chips. Why customization matters Jensen Huang introduces the RTX Spark GPU during a keynote address on the sidelines of the Computex in Taiwan. Ann Wang/Reuters Nvidia's chips are extraordinarily powerful, but they're designed to be useful across many different kinds of AI workloads. That isn't always necessary for companies like OpenAI and Anthropic, which can design chips tailored to their exact needs and cut costs by eliminating other features. Small savings can add up over billions of requests. In addition to chip design, AI is also making it easier to write the software needed to run AI models. Nvidia has long relied on its CUDA moat — a software framework that lets developers run AI applications on its chips, effectively locking them into its ecosystem. But AI could eventually make it easier to write CUDA-like software for competing chips. Business Insider reported that the startup Infinity used AI coding agents to recreate this kind of software for rival chipmaker D-Matrix in just 10 hours, per its founder. This could eventually help companies break out of Nvidia's ecosystem. Nvidia's counterattack Clem Delangue, Co-Founder and CEO of Hugging Face, Win McNamee/Getty Images Huang appeared to understand the danger. Before his ouster, Nvidia was aggressively expanding into open-weight AI models, which businesses can download, customize, and operate independently — unlike the flagship services sold by OpenAI and Anthropic. The company developed its own family of open models, dubbed Nemotron, backed open-weight startups like Reflection AI and Poolside, and moved to acquire Hugging Face , a major platform for distributing open AI technology. While this strategy threatens the business models of Anthropic and OpenAI, it could serve to expand Nvidia's customer base by encouraging more businesses, startups, cloud providers, and governments to run open models in the name of greater control and lower costs. For Nvidia, the more models running on its chips, the more it stands to sell. The catch TSMC CEO CC Wei I-Hwa Cheng/AFP/Getty Images None of this means Nvidia's dominance is about to disappear. Designing a chip is only part of the challenge. Companies still need to manufacture, test, and produce enormous quantities of them reliably. That requires partners like Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co., sophisticated engineering teams, and billions of dollars. Chips can take years to go from blueprint to data center, while AI models can change dramatically in months — meaning that custom processors could quickly become outdated. And Nvidia is hardly standing still. It uses AI in its own engineering, including to speed up development of its CUDA software, the company previously told Business Insider. Still, the direction of travel is becoming clearer — and the technology that Nvidia helped make possible is now helping its biggest customers build the hardware that could threaten its dominance.
