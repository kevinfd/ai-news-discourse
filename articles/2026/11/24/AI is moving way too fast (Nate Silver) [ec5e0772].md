# AI is moving way too fast (Nate Silver)

Silver Bulletin · abnry · 2026-11-24

AI is moving way too fast
I think AI coding tools are miraculous. So why do I feel so unsettled?
I’m not even sure where to start. So I’ll begin by quoting Scott Aaronson, the computer scientist, from his post after OpenAI announced a solution to the Navier-Stokes problem in September:

The thing is, I would have sworn the Navier-Stokes proof and Aaronson’s post happened longer ago than they did: Wasn’t that in June or July, maybe back when the World Cup was going on? Nope, it was barely two months ago.
I more precisely remember the date1 of another AI inflection point: in February, when the White House designated Anthropic a supply-chain risk. That was a different kind of story, about the intersections between AI, politics and national security, rather than the progress of the technology per se. But that also feels much longer ago. A lot has happened since then. The United States went to war with Iran. Democrats won in the biggest midterm landslide in 50 years. (Also, in truly important news, the Knicks won the NBA championship.)
The implications of the midterms are vast, and the newly elected members won’t be seated until January — but hell, even Nov. 3 is starting to feel like a long time ago. Because over the past three weeks, the news has been fucking crazy.
On Nov. 12, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang resigned after he acknowledged an affair with a woman reported to be a Chinese spy. Last week, Anthropic had the largest IPO in history. Then the next day, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpoge announced that, using a superpowered version of Claude, he’d identified a solution to an even more challenging mathematical problem: the Riemann hypothesis.2 Then, on Monday morning, under pressure from President Trump, Nvidia announced it had restored Huang as CEO, sending its stock up more than 30 percent and putting the broader NASDAQ so far ahead that trading on NASDAQ-100 futures briefly had to be halted because they triggered a “circuit breaker”.
I’ve been in the news business for a long time. There’s not supposed to be this much news this quickly. And nobody seems to know what to make of it: Anthropic’s share price has already fallen by around 25 percent since its debut in public markets last week.
I can vouch for how fast AI is progressing, at least when it comes to coding
As much as I don’t want to commingle what’s important to the broader world with what’s important to our business at Silver Bulletin, it’s hard not to, because there’s a common thread: AI. Not only is it a subject we cover — although we probably don’t cover it enough — but AI coding tools have become a productivity multiplier for our business and, in particular, for the various statistical models we build and are well known for.
Right now, things are kind of perfect. The AI models are very good at precisely the most tedious and annoying parts of building models like FLIPR and PELE. However, while the LLM’s “research taste” is improving, it remains “mid”, not great. Our experience and domain knowledge, for now, remain highly important in building these models and provide us with a “moat” of sorts.
We’re able to get far more productivity out of the same number of human hours. Between March and September, we launched or re-launched four models: COOPER (NCAA basketball), PELE (the World Cup), FLIPR (the midterm elections), and ELWAY (NFL predictions). Of these, only PELE was built totally from scratch, but the other three also received major upgrades. Some of this simply wouldn’t have been possible without Claude and ChatGPT.
Indeed, there’s a certain type of person I know, maybe someone I haven’t caught up with in a while. But I know what they’re going to say: I’ve never felt more productive in my life. I feel like I suddenly have 26 hours in a day. Or something along those lines.
These are people using AI in highly productive ways for their work in categories that more or less fall under the umbrella of “programming”. They’re sometimes also using it for personal tasks, but that varies. What’s more consistent is a certain set of characteristics. These people tend to be self-starters, jacks-of-all-trades, highly competitive, technically proficient (but not necessarily technical experts). They’re people who gain high marginal value from additional productivity. And they don’t face a lot of constraints; they probably work for themselves or with a small team. There may be some sort of phase change later. But right now, in late 2026, AI is probably making everything slightly more winner-take-all, though with some bias toward individuals rather than firms that face larger institutional constraints.
I share some of the characteristics I described above. It seems important to report from my “lived experience” here because, while the impact of AI on the broader economy remains ambiguous beyond the immense capital expenditures, I’m in a high percentile (99th+) for someone whose economically productive use cases for AI happen to coincide with the LLM’s greatest strengths, namely coding. (And increasingly also tasks that are “coding-adjacent”, like retrieving and preparing data.)
For what it’s worth, I think people in Silicon Valley, an empire built on 1s and 0s, often mistake superintelligent coding for broader (super)intelligence. But let’s save that conversation for a future post. I’ll say for now that I’m not sure how much one should extrapolate from an extraordinary, exponential increase in coding acumen to an overall “intelligence explosion”.
However, having worked on complex statistical forecasting models for more than 20 years now — back to the PECOTA days — I have some good baselines for evaluating how long these models take to build and the workflow around them. What I’m telling you is that AI coding tools are helping us accomplish things faster than I’d have imagined possible. The progress is obvious. It’s like having some teenage nephew/niece that you see a few times a year on the holidays and every time, they’re discernibly taller.
Back in the old days — you know, like way back in September 2025 — I found the AI coding tools at best marginally useful for the sort of models we build at Silver Bulletin. They were kind of like a commuter train that runs once every 40 minutes but on a completely unreliable, unpredictable schedule. If the train happens to be there exactly when you need it, well, great, but you’d never want to plan your day around it.
However, the LLMs have been getting smarter, faster, more reliable, and more persistent across a wider array of tasks. There were notable improvements in LLM capabilities from basically our winter/spring model season (COOPER and PELE) to our summer/fall products (FLIPR and ELWAY). Now we’re already working on COOPER 2.0, with substantial revisions/improvements, and the AI coding tools have clearly taken another leap.3 The LLMs are substantially better in November than they were in August and September, when we wrapped up work on FLIPR and ELWAY. I don’t necessarily assume that progress won’t stall out, but I’m not sure how much farther we can go along this curve before things get even weirder.
I don’t know how to demonstrate any of this to you other than through the finished products we produce. We’ve been able to publish some extremely cool and intricate and accurate models (they’ve had a good year against Vegas/prediction markets, for what it’s worth) with a staff of just four people.4
Whether they involve programming or not, project timelines are notoriously overoptimistic, i.e., what you think you can get done in 50 hours will probably take 100. But I increasingly find myself almost having to recalibrate in the other direction. You have to raise your ambition because you suddenly have these coding superpowers. “This would be super cool, but it would take me a week to program it correctly” becomes a different equation when you can assign the task to Claude or Chat and supervise it while you’re out on a run or out at a Knicks game. While there are still bottlenecks, and while I might be understating the learning curve in employing AI coding tools5, the productivity gains are highly tangible and way more than a rounding error at this point. Hence the I-feel-like-I-have-26-hours-a-day feeling.
This feels disconcerting
But man, when I see ChatGPT or Claude accomplish something in a few minutes that would once have taken hours to days to build, I have a bittersweet reaction.
I guess you could say it feels like a “cheat code”, but I think that’s slightly the wrong metaphor. We’re basically cutting out all the boring parts. Unlike, say, a college student using ChatGPT or Claude to cheat on an exam, where deeply grappling with the text is the whole point, a larger fraction of my time is spent on things like hypothesis testing and model architecture: understanding things as opposed to more mundane tasks. The statistical equivalent of “grunt work” is sometimes satisfying in its own way, but I don’t think I particularly get smarter from manually cleaning datasets or from coding things (as a mediocre programmer) once I know what I want to accomplish already. But now you can cycle through various model designs remarkably quickly, though you need to be alert that LLMs are prone to some degree of overoptimization and overfitting. (I often find myself pushing back on Claude or ChatGPT when they’re trying to maximize some implicit loss function through backtesting and getting slightly too cute in the process.)
But while there’s perhaps a “skill issue” here — honestly, I feel fortunate that I learned to build and steer forecasting models with the equivalent of my own hands before the AI coding tools provided a warp drive — I also find myself having a strong emotional reaction to the moment. It’s not one of the especially common emotions, like anger or sadness or fear. The best word for it is probably an uncanny feeling.
For me, the uncanny feeling combines feeling disconcerted with some feeling of prescience. There’s almost a feeling of déjà vu, even though I don’t think humans have quite experienced anything like this moment before. A lot of smart people predicted very rapid growth in AI; in particular, this has been a major obsession of people in the broader “rationalist” community for years. They predicted that things would get very weird, and they’ve been right.
I’m sympathetic, up to a point, with people who feel like AI tools are overhyped. Although only to a point: I think the “stochastic parrots” people are basically as wrong as the Roman Inquisition against Galileo. Still, I’m not sure how I’d feel if I weren’t using LLMs for coding. For non-coding tasks, AI is increasingly helpful for both business and personal work, but it’s well within the spectrum of “normal technology.”
But I’m not sure the “normal technology” paradigm holds for coding and coding-adjacent work. Things that once took three days now take three hours. Although a technological miracle in one subfield doesn’t necessarily reshape society.
Some of the eerie feeling is selfish, like the Titanic is about to crash, but I’ve secured a prime spot in one of the lifeboats. Right now, things are great; we humans are in charge. But what if/when LLMs develop great aesthetic/research taste? I’ve already been burned too many times by “the models are bad at this,” only to find out they just weren’t good at it yet. If the world got stuck in late 2026, the situation would be highly favorable, but AI capabilities growth continues to meet or exceed timelines that would have struck most people as unreasonable, so I’m not sure how long that will last.
But I’m also a student of politics and public opinion, and while I believe the American system is remarkably robust in some ways, I’m not sure that it’s designed to handle this degree of rapid technological change.
I’m just not sure what to think — other than that AI is very obviously a moving target. Society could probably adapt healthily and productively to AI as it exists in late 2026, though it might take a little time. But the AI tools in late 2026 are much better than a year ago, and a year ago they were much better than they were two years ago. I’m not sure, from a technical standpoint, whether the growth in AI capabilities is linear, exponential, superlinear, or sublinear. I’m also not sure it matters. It hasn’t stalled out, and it’s plausibly too fast for the political system to handle. And yet, the notion of a “handover” to AI seems worse on basically every level. The current situation is increasingly unhinged. It seems insane to me that OpenAI, Anthropic, and other labs are racing toward recursive superintelligence when they don’t really have a confident plan to control it, let alone align it with broader human interests.
It happened when I was in Paris; for some reason, I have a distinctive memory of where I was when various Silver Bulletin articles were posted.

I’m not going to adjudicate those distinctions here. A second mathematician unaffiliated with Anthropic, Alessandro Masullo, also claims to have a proof.
For what it’s worth, I’ve been using Claude Opus 5.5 for COOPER 2.0, since Claude was the lead model for our spring update to COOPER. FLIPR and ELWAY were mostly ChatGPT projects, conversely. I thought that as of summer/fall, GPT was a little bit better than Claude for programming, but it’s possible the lead has shifted again. Still, the fact that these various models are competing with one another contributes to the frontier moving faster.

A short list of best practices that I’ve found helpful: having multiple threads or agents working on different parts of your project at once, having various AI models vet one another’s work, mostly using voice rather than text to communicate with them (it’s faster), and steering the agents mid-prompt while they’re thinking. And then there’s some workflow-related stuff like saving more complicated requests for times when you’re going to be offline for some period of time.
