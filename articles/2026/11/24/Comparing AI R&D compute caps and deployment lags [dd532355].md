# Comparing AI R&D compute caps and deployment lags

Alignment Forum · elifland · 2026-11-24

I’m publishing this a bit rougher than usual because it’s very pertinent to Evan Hubinger’s recent shortform , which advocates for blocking internal deployment as a pacing mechanism over compute reallocation (he mostly discusses it in the context of a risk-assessment-based regime, while this post discusses the policies standalone). (see my response to Evan which also summarizes this post.) I compare 2 classes of policies: AI R&D compute cap : A cap on the percentage of compute used for capabilities R&D, as proposed at pacing-ai.com . Deployment lags/freezes: An X-month lag means that you have to use models X months old for AI R&D (or, AIs at most as capable as the AIs you trained X months ago). [1]  A freeze means you pick a capability level above which you can’t use models to do AI R&D. I sometimes separate out internal-only lags/freezes from internal+external (“uniform”) ones. If I just say lag, I mean it as shorthand for lag/freeze. Overall, I think compute caps are similarly good or slightly better than deployment lags/freezes (very possible I’ll change my mind in the future).  Ignoring political feasibility, I think the best would be to combine both of them in order to get the immediate slowdown of the cap and the long-term + structural benefits of the lag/freeze. [2]  I think it’s close enough that if one is significantly more feasible than the other, we should push for the more feasible one. I prefer internal deployment lags to external deployment lags because I think the societal awareness benefits are larger than the verification costs. Factor Cap vs. lag/freeze Reasoning Capability curve shape Compute cap (weak) Let’s consider lag 6 months vs. cap 10% implemented in Mar 2027, which result in similar TED-AI dates. Compute caps slow now->SAR more than lags, while lags slow SAR->TED-AI more.  Here's a quick screenshot of an illustrative example comparing the 2 policies: Aggregating uplift-years, varying point of no return (PONR): The higher the capability level of the PONR, the better lags look relative to caps; if it’s ~SAR then caps look slightly better, if it’s ~TED-AI lags look much better. With lags, you delay higher capability milestones more than you do with caps. We’ll look at total uplift-years divided by a racing baseline, with a baseline of SAR in Jul 2027 and TED-AI in Nov. Uplift-years = ∫ (AI R&D uplift of the best model trained) dt from Jan 2027 until the PONR capability level is passed. [3] No China, SAR PONR: Cap ~2.2x, lag ~2x No China, TED-AI PONR: Cap ~1.8x, lag ~3x No China, uncertainty over PONR [4]  (35% before SAR, 65% before halfway from SAR to TED-AI, 80% before TED-AI; same PONR for both policies): Cap ~2x, lag ~2.2x With China + TED-AI pause until China catches up, uncertainty over PONR: Both ~2.4x Other considerations in favor of relatively stronger immediate pacing relative to later pacing: China might just not race in the first place, even if the US did. Pacing is more cooperative than racing. Stronger immediate pacing means more serial time to make a deal, or adjust the pacing regime to be better at higher capabilities. AI improvements harm the ability to do international deals even if they aren’t directly dangerous. See here . Going slower now is more robust against others failing to slow down further in the future. Other considerations in favor of relatively weaker immediate pacing relative to later pacing: Going slower now leads to more overhang, meaning faster progress if the regime is lifted. The effect size of this appears to be fairly modest in our modeling; e.g., if both policies are ended right before the lag gets to SAR, the lag takes 2.1 months to go from SAR->TED-AI while the cap takes 1.9 months. [5] Differences in point of no return capability level Deployment lag Not using your best AIs for AI R&D potentially pushes back the point of no return substantially because it makes it harder for AIs to adversarially prevent humans from aligning future AIs.  This effect seems potentially quite large if you’re using e.g. current-level AIs or lower for your AI R&D. Some quick modeling using my highly uncertain estimated PONR gap distribution says that this effect might be very important, e.g. changing lag 6 months vs. cap 10% from roughly equal geomean of uplift-years to lag 6 months being ~2.5x higher. Verification ease / robustness Uniform lag > compute cap ~= internal-only lag. Uniform lag > compute cap ~= internal-only lag. See below  for more. Societal awareness Against external deployment lag (Pushing in the opposite direction of verification ease/robustness) Enabling safety research Compute cap (weak) In both proposals, there’s the question of handling safety research: In the AI R&D compute cap proposal, can you have a carve-out for using the non-AI-R&D compute for safety R&D? In the deployment lag proposal, can you have a carve-out for using the non-lagged model to conduct safety R&D? Both seem like similarly difficult problems. Let’s consider each situation in turn: If both have a safety carve-out, then an AI R&D compute cap is better than a deployment lag.  The situation is similar except that under the AI R&D compute cap, most compute is forced to be spent on either safety or external inference, leading to more compute being spent on safety than in the deployment lag world. If there isn’t a safety carve-out, then it’s unclear, but my best guess is that an AI R&D compute cap is worse than a deployment lag. There’s a tradeoff where there’s probably less compute used for safety in the compute cap world, but a better model. It’s unclear to me how that shakes out, but at least Evan Hubinger has the intuition  that it’s better in the deployment lag case because models are differentially worse at safety. Maximum slowdown amount Compute cap A well-enforced compute cap of 0% could result in very nearly a full pause, while banning use of AIs for AI R&D would not. Verification In terms of ease of verification: Uniform lag > compute cap ~= internal-only lag. Our guess is that with a uniform lag, ~5% verification error can be achieved with significantly less effort / auditing access, though we’ve done less analysis than for the compute cap. [7]  We discuss this attack surface here . Attacks against compute caps and internal deployment lags Depending on their implementation, the verification problem for compute caps and internal deployment lags via auditors will need to deal with three overall classes of attacks: Attacks that free up compute. These are attacks that allow the company to use compute in a way that auditors don’t have visibility into. These attacks include getting off-books compute (hidden compute), using compute that is declared to be idle (fake idle) and having spare compute by actually running workloads more efficiently than what is declared or observed (hidden efficiency). Attacks 1-3 in our previous work. Attacks that turn external inference into capabilities benefits. These are attacks that allow external inference to be used as if it were internal inference by just directly having employees use the external API (launder internal inference), collaborating with third parties for R&D or data generation, or training directly on outputs generated via external inference. This only applies to internal-only lags (because uniform lags also restrict external inference). Attacks 4-6 in our previous work. Attacks that consist of exploiting the ‘non-capabilities’ exemption. This only applies to deployment lags that include an exemption for safety research. Attack 7 in our previous work. Additionally, internal deployment lags have to deal with a fourth class of attack: Doing internal deployment via training runs. This would involve having AIs do AI R&D during training runs, e.g., as part of RL rollouts in post-training. Attack 8 in our previous work. In our previous work , we analyzed how the first 3 classes of attacks are all applicable to a compute allocation cap, unless you did a version of the proposal that had no carve-out for ‘non-capabilities R&D’, in which case you could avoid attack 7, or if you didn’t allow serving customers as a category (in which case you could avoid attacks 4-6). In this supplement  on internal deployment lags, we identify three possible ways of implementing an internal deployment lag: Uniform Deployment Lag — covered models must be in cold storage, with no internal or external deployment. Internal Deployment Lag — covered models cannot be internally deployed. Internal Deployment Lag with safety carve-out — covered models cannot be internally deployed except for a carved-out application area (e.g., AI safety research). #1 requires defending against attacks 1-3 and 8, #2 against attacks 1-6 and 8 (it avoids attack 7), and #3 against all eight. Verification measures Quantitatively, we are most worried about attacks #5 and #7. This makes a uniform deployment lag highly attractive from a verification perspective.
