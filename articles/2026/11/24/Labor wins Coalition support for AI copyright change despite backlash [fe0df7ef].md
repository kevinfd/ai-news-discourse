# Labor wins Coalition support for AI copyright change despite backlash

ABC News Australia · Clare Armstrong and Cam Wilson · 2026-11-24

AI inquiry backs creator payments, tougher safeguards and data centre licensing
In short:
A parliamentary inquiry has recommended a new payment scheme for Australian creators whose work is used to train AI.
The music industry has argued tech companies should be required to negotiate deals under existing copyright laws.
What's next?
The federal government is expected to release draft laws on AI standards early next year.
Labor has secured cross-party backing to change Australia's copyright laws to ensure creatives are paid for their work when it is used to train artificial intelligence (AI), despite fierce resistance from the music industry.
The Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence released its report today, making 46 recommendations to the federal government, including a payment scheme that would compensate individual artists and copyright holders when their material is used to train AI models in Australia.
But the music industry has rejected the premise that existing copyright protections were inadequate, while News Corp Australasia warned that if the push toward commercial rights deals remained voluntary, it would amount to "state-sanctioned theft".
Labor committee chair Jo Briskey, Liberal deputy Jonno Duniam and crossbench member Kate Chaney all marvelled at the level of agreement the committee had achieved, even though they failed to reach consensus on which energy sources AI data centres should be required to use.
Ms Briskey said the report was about Australia "taking control" of the "most transformative technology of our lifetime".
"We know that these AI companies want to come here," she said.
"We have power, we have a stable government, and so we want to leverage off that."
Independent ACT senator David Pocock was also on the committee but ultimately wrote a dissenting report, breaking with the group over copyright, energy and concerns that his colleagues had taken an "overly optimistic" view of the benefits of AI.
The government is expected to unveil draft legislation covering data centre standards, AI safety and a proposal on copyright early in the new year across what would likely be a series of bills.
Labor and Coalition split on renewables-only data centres
The committee report agreed data centres must bring their own power, which should primarily be the cheapest form of generation available, be net contributors to Australia's energy grid and that households should not face higher electricity prices.
But there was no consensus on the extent to which the government should "specify" the energy source requirements, though Ms Chaney added comments supportive of a renewables-only approach to data centres.
"We can't let AI companies drive up our prices or our emissions, and so it's going to be really important, in my view, that renewables are powering data centres in Australia," she said.
Asked about splitting the AI legislation into different parts, Senator Duniam said it made "sense" to have issues like copyright dealt with "separately" from "matters relating to planning and environmental approvals, for example".
Music industry rejects any change to copyright
The committee made eight recommendations on copyright, arguing laws should be "strengthened" to facilitate payments to Australian creators, that there be transparency around the data being used to train models, along with strong penalties for breaches.
But advocates in the music industry and other rights holders have continually asked the government to leave the existing copyright system in place and force AI companies to do deals.
Australian Recording Industry Association (ARIA) chief executive Annabelle Herd said the recommendations appeared "detached from the strong and repeated evidence of rightsholders".
"We reject the notion expressed in the majority committee report that existing copyright law does not work and that we need a so-called 'fairer way to ensure creators get paid'," she said.
Ms Herd said piracy on a "mass scale" in another jurisdiction was not a reason to "legalise unlicensed copying in Australia".
"Copyright owners have asked again and again for government to make AI companies come to the negotiating table," she said.
News Corp Australasia executive chairman Michael Miller said encouraging commercial deals "sounds right" but feared the report lacked the detail rights holders need to "set their own terms of trade and receive fair payment".
"Any framework that lets trillion-dollar AI platforms scrape content without voluntary agreements isn't innovation, it's state-sanctioned theft," he said.
Defending the committee's approach, Ms Briskey said the current system was not working for creatives.
"We need to deliver a better outcome for Australian creators," she said.
"We need to see them have control, and we need to see them be paid.
"That is not happening right now."
Pocock says committee is 'overly optimistic' about AI
Senator Pocock said today he was concerned about the "heroic assumptions" around the benefits Australia would gain from hosting frontier AI training.
"[The committee report] relies on evidence from big tech, from those who are really pushing hard for data centre investment, for Anthropic and OpenAI to set up shop here, training their models," he said.
"I think we need to take a more cautious approach based on … the concerns from communities around AI and data centres, and the dire warnings we're having from these very companies that are creating the technology."
Senator Pocock said he also wanted a three-year freeze on any changes to copyright to test if current laws were able to force the AI companies to pay for works they were using to train their models.
Finally, he took issue with the failure to mention diesel in the final report, arguing millions and millions of litres would be stored and used in data centres across Australia.
Tougher safeguards and a push for homegrown AI
Across the rest of the 263-page report, the committee made dozens of other recommendations to address gaps in how Australia currently regulates AI.
The majority's report called for a licensing system for data centres; that proposal would be used to enforce the government's proposed rules for power and water usage as well as the location of the infrastructure.
The committee also asked for obligations around monitoring and reporting AI-related incidents.
While the report reiterated its interim advice to reserve computing capacity, the language around the final recommendation was softened to "consider" making it a condition for significant AI infrastructure projects.
It also urged the government to boost the funding for the government's AI Safety Institute by at least tenfold, up from its existing $30 million budgeted for four years.
A recommendation to promote the development of Australian AI models urged the government to buy more from domestic companies as a way of support.
"We must be makers, not simply takers of this technology," the report said.
