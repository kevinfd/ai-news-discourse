# Secrecy surrounds claims of Russia’s push for nukes in space

Scientific American · Adam Kovac · 2026-11-24

Recent media reports—allegedly based on assessments from Western intelligence agencies—that Russia is planning to launch an antisatellite nuclear weapon into orbit by the end of the year are raising alarm around the globe. Although these claims have yet to be corroborated, several experts warn that the threat such a move would pose cannot be underestimated and that the situation should be taken seriously.
Russia supposedly took a significant step toward deploying a space-based nuclear weapon with its launch of a satellite named Kosmos 2553 in 2022. That satellite is believed to now be nonoperational after it was reported to be spinning out of control last year. According to a joint investigation by multiple German news agencies, the “new” satellite is called Sputnik-S and is presently stored in the Moscow area, where it has been awaiting launch for years. Whether the Russian military is actually preparing to put a working nuclear weapon into orbit—or merely to continue conducting tests along the lines of Kosmos 2553—is unknown, says Victoria Samson, chief director of space security and stability at the Secure World Foundation.
“It is possible that they’re thinking about doing it; it’s possible they're thinking about doing a replacement to Kosmos 2553,” she says, adding that she is not privy to classified intelligence reports. “It’s possible they’re not doing anything.” Such sweeping situational uncertainties are the norm for civil sources speculating on secretive, highly classified military activities in space.
On supporting science journalism
Actually placing a nuclear weapon in space would be a violation of the Outer Space Treaty, which currently has 118 countries as signatories, including Russia. Not only would launching the weapon be illegal, but its potential use would also be “relatively impractical,” says Mark Melamed, deputy vice president for the global nuclear policy program at the Nuclear Threat Initiative.
“The use of a nuclear weapon in space would cause widespread and very indiscriminate damage, so it’s bad for everybody,” he says. “It’s not a capability that can be targeted against any specific adversary.”
The immediate impact of a nuclear detonation in orbit would be a sensor-frying flash of optical light, x-rays, and gamma rays blinding and disabling any satellite within line of sight, Samson says. Next would come an expanding, drifting cloud of radioactive plasma that would rapidly incapacitate most satellites that passed through it, regardless of their nation of origin or function. The lingering radiation would, depending on the size and altitude of the detonation and various other factors, make a huge part of low-Earth orbit a no-go zone for satellites for the better part of a year, she says.
That’s not even factoring in the countless pieces of debris, all traveling at thousands of miles per hour, that would result from dead satellites crashing into one another and pose additional threats to any remaining active satellites in their path.
While their use is extremely problematic—to say the least—putting nuclear weapons into orbit is, in principle, as simple as a rocket launch. And it has, in fact, been done before: At the height of the Cold War, in the late 1950s and early 1960s, the U.S. and the Soviet Union alike experimented with nuclear explosions in space. The U.S.’s notorious 1962 “Starship Prime” test detonated a nuclear bomb some 250 miles above Earth, knocking out several of the small number of satellites in orbit at that time and causing blackouts on the ground below.
Besides the prohibitions of the Outer Space Treaty, the enormous risks and drawbacks of such weapons in space has limited their use ever since—until now, perhaps. The rise of massive megaconstellations of satellites, such as those belonging to SpaceX’s Starlink system, may have changed the equation. The Starlink service has proved vital to Ukraine’s homeland defense against Russia, which began a full-scale invasion of its neighbor in 2022 and has suffered grievous battlefield losses in subsequent years.
“If you were to put a weapon into space, it’s because you want to get rid of a lot of satellites all at once” while still creating a lot of indiscriminate collateral damage, Samson says.
“That's why ... I think [it] is very low-probability they would actually do it, because I think it'd be very escalatory,” she adds. “But it’s one of those low-probability, high-impact type of scenarios.”
The effects would be felt globally because the loss of a large number of telecommunications satellites would heavily impact access to the Internet. One modern convenience would possibly survive, however.
“I’m not so sure GPS [global positioning system] satellites would be affected, just because there are a few satellites that are protected against nuclear radiation,” Samson says. “These things are usually military satellites that are intended to operate in times of conflict.”
Nuclear bombs are far from the weapon of choice in space, where many other, less extreme options exist. The U.S., Russia, China and India have all conducted tests of antisatellite missiles launched from terrestrial bases across the past two decades. And in September a high-ranking official at the U.S. Air Force announced the U.S. had already placed “on-orbit space control” weapons around Earth. Melamed says the timing does not seem to indicate that Russia’s possible decision to place nuclear weapons in orbit would have been a direct response to such actions.
“I definitely think we are in a period of military competition in outer space; that's undeniable. And I think the comments in September by the Air Force secretary are a reflection of that reality rather the launch of a new phase of that competition” Melamed says. “I don’t think it’s fair to link this latest reporting around the Russian capability to that announcement because this Russian capability, at least the reporting on it, is that they were developing this capability back in 2024, and who knows how long before that the intelligence might have existed.”
The potential decision to launch a nuclear weapon into space may instead just be an effort for Russia to project power and reclaim its position as a force to be reckoned with.
“Russia has a historical legacy of being a major space power going back from the Soviet times,” Samson says. “This is something they’re very proud of. I don’t think it’s something they’d give up lightly.”
