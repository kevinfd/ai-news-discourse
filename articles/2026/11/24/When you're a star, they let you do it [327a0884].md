# When you're a star, they let you do it

Business Insider · Sarah E. Needleman, Sarah E. Needleman (sneedleman@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-24

Sean Gallup/Getty; BI The meme may be the most succinct symbol of the past two weeks in Corporate America — and a nail in the coffin of the #MeToo movement. A few hours after Jensen Huang was reinstated as CEO of Nvidia, President Donald Trump posted an AI-generated image to Truth Social: a remake of the most indelible moments of his political life — the bloodied, fist-raised aftermath of the first attempted assassination . Except the man rising out of the arms of the Secret Service is Jensen Huang, clutching a red bra. Huang was forced out of Nvidia after reports surfaced indicating that he had lied to his board, had affairs with two employees, and was the subject of a foreign-intelligence investigation. Soon after, The New York Times reported that he was a volatile and demeaning manager, telling one executive his mother should have aborted him, and telling another employee he would rip off the top of their head and pee on their brain . Now, Huang has been recast as the survivor of a malicious attack, and the bra isn't a smoking gun — it's a trophy of his conquest over a sex and spy scandal. The company's chairman said on an earnings call on November 17 that the board "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO." On Monday, the board said it was satisfied with Huang's "candor," and that it had received "input from senior levels of the United States government." Trump said Huang's reinstatement was "the correct and proper decision," and called him a "National Treasure." The president presaged this moment years ago when the Access Hollywood tape came to light on the eve of his first election. "When you're a star, they let you do it," he said. "You can do anything." Welcome to the era of impunity. For decades, American companies operated on a simple rule: CEOs who get involved with subordinates get shown the door. McDonald's ousted Steve Easterbrook in 2019 after determining that he violated company policy by having a consensual relationship with an employee. BP CEO Bernard Looney resigned in 2023 after admitting he had not fully disclosed personal relationships with colleagues. And last year Nestlé fired Laurent Freixe following an investigation that found he had an undisclosed romantic relationship with a direct subordinate, breaching its code of conduct. The rule was always more about power than it was about sex. When the boss is sleeping with someone who reports to him, everyone else's career is quietly rigged. "There's no way that you can have a sexual relationship with a subordinate and have that be an equal playing field," says Julie Roginsky, cofounder of Lift Our Voices, a nonprofit that advocates for safer, more equitable workplaces. "It is unfair to the subordinate, and it is unfair to others who did not engage in that kind of relationship with him." The Jensurrection adds an asterisk to that rule around CEO and employee relationships. It no longer applies if you're a founder-mode founder, if the market believes the company can't survive without you, and if the President wants you back. Notice what Huang's defenders, from Elon Musk to the 200,000-plus signatories of the "Stand with Jensen" petition, mostly didn't argue: that he hadn't done these things. Their case was that it didn't matter. Nvidia is the engine of the AI boom , and you don't fire the engine. The shareholders are the big winners of Huang's return. Then there's everyone else in white-collar America. "People who are not CEOs are going to feel like CEOs can get away with more," says Jessica Kriegel, chief strategy officer of workforce and labor at Culture Partners. "That's going to be incredibly frustrating." Huang, of course, is no ordinary boss. He is one of tech's most recognizable founders, synonymous with $10,000 black leather jackets and known throughout Silicon Valley by his first name. His fans treat him less like a conventional chief executive and more like a deity: A jacket he signed sold at Sotheby's this year for $960,000, and parents have dressed children as Huang for Halloween . The timing makes it worse. The predominant narrative about US white-collar workers today is that they're replaceable — by the inevitable, unstoppable rise of AI that Nvidia is key to developing. Meanwhile, the lesson of Nvidia is that the more replaceable you are, the more accountable you're held. The more indispensable you are, the fewer rules you have to follow. For most people, that's not a reassuring equation. "Regardless of what was intended, a message was sent to shareholders, employees, customers, policymakers, and founders about where the line exists between the value a leader brings and the conduct the company is willing to accept," says Bradley Akubuiro, a partner at Bully Pulpit International and a former chief spokesperson for Boeing. Highly successful corporate leaders have long gotten away with bad behavior by dismissing, excusing, or recasting it as virtue. Steve Jobs's bullying was absorbed into the mythology of the visionary founder. Musk's defenders similarly cast his harshness as the price of building great companies . The difference here is that Nvidia said the quiet part out loud: If you're important enough — especially in tech, especially when a world-historic bag is up for grabs — you can do what you want. Huang has plenty of supporters inside and outside Nvidia. At the company's Santa Clara headquarters Monday, employees chanted "Jensen! Jensen!" as he returned to the office . Chamath Palihapitiya, the founder and CEO of investment firm Social Capital and one the "besties" on the All-In podcast, said on X on Friday that Huang's "ridiculous expectations, impatience and vision" would weed out "mediocre people" and help build a team capable of "greatness." Still, some employees — and US workers in general — may read Huang's return not as a rescue but as a warning that conduct is negotiable for sufficiently valuable leaders. Episodes like this can deepen people's belief that "the world is corrupt" and "unsafe," says Kriegel. How a company's board treats its leaders helps set the standard for everyone else because culture is set from the top down, says Robert Mason, the longtime CEO of SPOC Energy, a manufacturer of power systems for data centers. "Restoring someone to leadership cannot mean restoring them to a place beyond accountability," he says. "The higher you go in an organization, the more accountable you should expect to be, not less." In his appearance at headquarters, Huang promised the Nvidia employees who cheered his comeback that he'd be a "better CEO the second time around."
