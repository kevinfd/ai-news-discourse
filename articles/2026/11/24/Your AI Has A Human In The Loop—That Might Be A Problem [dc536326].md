# Your AI Has A Human In The Loop—That Might Be A Problem

Forbes · Nitin Agarwal, CommunityVoice · 2026-11-24

Nitin Agarwal leads Enterprise AI & Risk at Luminace, governing cyber, AI, data privacy and regulatory risk.
Every AI deployment I review eventually reaches the same question: Where does a person step in? Most teams answer quickly with “A human will review it.” In practice, that answer often means a busy employee clicking “approve” on outputs they don’t have the time, context or authority to challenge.
Human-in-the-loop (HITL) oversight is one of the most cited controls in AI governance and one of the least understood. Done well, it is what keeps AI useful and accountable. Done poorly, it creates a false sense of safety and can introduce new bias of its own.
What HITL Really Means Today
HITL used to mean a person labeling training data or checking a model’s predictions. Today, most enterprises consume AI rather than build it. Employees use copilots, vendor tools with embedded AI and, increasingly, agents that can take actions on their own. The human role has shifted from training the model to judging its output and deciding whether it should be acted on.
That shift matters. The human is no longer a quality checker at the end of a pipeline. They are accountable decision makers. If an AI drafts a contract summary, flags a security alert or recommends a vendor, the person who accepts it owns the outcome. Regulators are moving the same way: Article 14 of the EU AI Act expects the people overseeing high-risk AI to be able to override or stop it and to stay alert to over relying on its output.
The Risk Of Taking The Human Out
The biggest risk I see isn’t a dramatic AI failure. It’s quiet overreliance. Business users often treat AI like a “magic button” and accept its answers because they sound confident.
Even seasoned professionals fall into this trap. In 2025, Deloitte Australia agreed to partially refund the Australian government for a report costing about $290,000 after it was found to contain apparent AI-generated errors, including a made-up quote attributed to a federal court ruling and citations to research papers that don’t exist. The AI wasn’t the only failure. The review process that should have caught those errors was.
When the human element is removed or reduced to a rubber stamp, several things break at once:
• Errors Scale: A single flawed output can be copied into reports, contracts and decisions across the business before anyone notices.
• Accountability Blurs: When something goes wrong, “The AI said so!” is not an answer regulators, auditors or customers will accept.
• Context Disappears: AI doesn’t know about the side conversation, the client relationship or the operational constraint that changes the right answer.
• Agents Amplify The Impact: When AI can send emails, change records or trigger workflows, a missing checkpoint turns a bad suggestion into a bad action.
How The Human Can Introduce Bias
Here’s the part many leaders miss: Putting a person in the loop doesn’t automatically make a decision fair or accurate. As the NIST AI Risk Management Framework points out, people bring their own individual and group biases into AI systems, and in some tasks, AI can amplify those biases rather than cancel them out.
Automation bias is the most common. A 2026 review of 35 studies in the journal AI & Society found that heavy workload, task complexity and time pressure all increase reliance on AI advice, and that even experts aren’t immune. It also found that showing AI advice before a person forms their own view makes them more likely to accept its errors. The reverse problem shows up too: Reviewers sometimes follow AI only when it confirms what they already believed, which reinforces assumptions instead of testing them.
Reviewers can also shape the system over time. If the same small group corrects outputs, their preferences become the standard the AI learns from or the business adopts. And when reviewers are measured on speed, approval becomes the path of least resistance.
Making HITL Work In Practice
Here’s what I recommend to leaders who want human oversight to be more than a checkbox:
• Match Oversight To Risk: Not every output need review. Define which use cases require a human decision, which needs spot checks and which can run with monitoring. Reserve the deepest review for decisions that affect people, money, safety or legal obligations.
• Give Reviewers Authority And Time To Say No: A reviewer who can’t reject an output (or who has 30 seconds per item) isn’t providing oversight. Build review time into workflows and make overrides a normal part of the job.
• Show Reviewers Why, Not Just What: Ask AI tools to cite their sources or show the clauses, data or evidence behind an answer. It’s far easier to catch an error when the reasoning is visible.
• Put Hard Stops In Front Of Agent Actions: For AI that can act, require human approval before irreversible steps such as sending external messages, changing financial records or modifying systems.
• Watch The Reviewers, Too: Track override rates, approval speed and patterns across reviewers. An approval rate near 100% is a warning sign, not a success metric. Rotate reviewers and use second-level review for high-impact decisions to reduce individual bias.
• Train For Skepticism: Teach employees that AI can be confidently wrong, and give them simple prompts to check: What’s the source? What’s missing? Would I sign my name to this?
The Takeaway
Human-in-the-loop should be a design decision about where judgment, accountability and context must live. Keep people in the loop where it matters most, give them the tools and authority to challenge AI and measure whether their oversight is real. The goal isn’t to slow AI down. It’s to make sure that when AI moves fast, someone is still steering the wheel.
