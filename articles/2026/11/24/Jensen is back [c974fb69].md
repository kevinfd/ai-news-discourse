# Jensen is back

Platformer · Casey Newton · 2026-11-24

Jensen is back
But the Nvidia he's taking charge of looks very different from the one he just left
Programming note: And with this, we're off for Thanksgiving. See you next week.
In the early 2000s, when I was a cub reporter chronicling the latest developments in Scottsdale, AZ, there lived a kindly city councilman who occasionally took the time to explain to me how the world worked. The councilman had a darkly comic worldview, and whenever I expressed surprise at the venal behavior of one political figure or another, the councilman would laugh politely and say to me: Casey, in politics, you can never be cynical enough.
Well, here’s Bloomberg:
Nvidia’s board reinstated Jensen Huang as head of the world’s largest chipmaker in one of the most startling corporate comebacks in history, sending the stock on its biggest rally in decades.
Less than two weeks after Huang left Nvidia under the cloud of scandal, he is reclaiming the titles of president and chief executive officer, the board said in a surprise statement Monday. President Donald Trump had pressed for the move, and the board cited the US government’s input when making the announcement.
“The board believes Jensen is the right person to lead Nvidia, and that his reinstatement is in the best interests of our stockholders, employees, partners and customers,” Chairman Harvey C. Jones said in the statement.
In hindsight, the startling part is that the board ever pressured Huang to resign in the first place.
There is a reason I have been openly wondering when Huang would come back since the day he left, and it is not my unshakeable faith in Nvidia’s board to do the right thing. I think it is fair to say that the Nvidia board has no idea what it is doing. After all, according to the board’s own statement, a committee of independent directors is still investigating whether a foreign intelligence service targeted Huang through his affair with Alice Huang. Huang had denied to the board that there was anything improper about his relationship with her, Wired reported. Reuters also reported that he had intimate relationships with two Nvidia employees.
And now the board is bringing Huang back before its own investigation is over.
What gave the board the confidence that they could do that and withstand the shareholder lawsuits that are about to bury them?
Why, President Trump, of course. Here are Maggie Haberman and Jonathan Swan at the New York Times:
Mr. Trump asked Vice President JD Vance, who came to office with close ties to Silicon Valley, to talk to Mr. Huang and work with Nvidia’s board. Over the weekend, Mr. Vance spoke privately with Mr. Huang, people with knowledge of the matter said. And he worked to get the board two things it would need to bring back a leader it had forced out little more than a week earlier amid a global scandal and had subsequently made clear it had no interest in rehiring.
One was an assurance that the Trump administration did not believe that Mr. Huang, who has admitted the affair, was a threat to national security. The other was a statement that Mr. Huang was not facing criminal charges. [...]
“I just thought he should be running the company, and I didn’t know it was going to have this kind of an impact,” Mr. Trump said in a brief telephone interview with The New York Times soon after the news became public. “I’ve never seen anything quite like it, actually. The stock is up.”
Ahh yes, the stock price: the only job-performance metric that seems to matter to Trump, and to the faction in and out of the White House who lobbied for Huang’s return. And so he personally called Nvidia’s board members, told them to rehire Huang, and they did. (Pablo Torre: “‘Capitalism,’ you see, is when the president personally intervenes in the operations of private companies and enriches himself and his undisclosed web of foreign and domestic business partners.”)
You could call it a scandal, though these days it’s not clear that any of us are capable of being scandalized. Charlie Warzel is on to something when he likens it to a hostage situation: let Huang come back, or the economy explodes. In any case, it seems I got a bit over my skis when wrote that the ‘lol nothing matters’ era of American politics was over.
Still, it seems like a strange time to make Huang even more central to the fate of the US economy, given everything we now know about him. (Three days ago, to pick a fact at random: “he told another employee he would rip off the top of their head and ‘pee on your brain.’”)
And something else we know: Huang is not going back to business as usual at Nvidia. What he is going back to, in a word, is: lawyers.
The Securities and Exchange Commission is investigating whether Nvidia’s disclosures misled investors, the Wall Street Journal reported. The Justice Department is leading a criminal investigation into whether Megaspeed violated export controls. Commerce investigators have already asked Nvidia for records of its sales to Megaspeed. And California’s attorney general has issued a subpoena to Nvidia asking whether China obtained advanced chips or confidential information through the company or Huang, and whether Nvidia misled investors and the public about that risk.
(And here it’s relevant to point out that the Chinese military is seeking Nvidia chips to put in its weapons, according to a report from Congress.)
Meanwhile, lawmakers from both parties have questioned Huang’s return. Huang is due before the Senate Banking Committee on Dec. 3 for a public hearing on export controls, and on Dec. 1, he’ll sit for a closed-door interview with the House Foreign Affairs Committee, the Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party, and the House Intelligence Committee.
The legal morass will extend well into next year, when Democrats assume control of both houses of Congress. Before long, the directors may wish they’d stuck to their guns.
Of course, that’s assuming that a majority of the current board sticks around. It’s more likely that, over the next few weeks, they’ll be purged, along with Colette Kress, the CFO who briefly took Huang’s place, and anyone else Huang suspects of disloyalty. Also, don’t be surprised if Huang’s children and son-in-law soon get promoted, in the founder's hopes that Nvidia’s key-man risk can extend into the next generation and beyond.
In the Financial Times, Robert Armstrong argues — persuasively — that in the end Nvidia’s board had no other choice.
“Nvidia had no board chair, no evidence of board independence, no succession plan,” Armstrong writes. “Its org chart is a mirror of the Chinese Communist Party’s: a mess of arrows all leading to one man. Huang was reinstated not because he is the right person for the job, but because his failures, plus presidential support, left his company with no other options.”
Microsoft, Apple, and other tech giants understood what Nvidia did not: that it is not a great company if only one person can run it. I suspect someone else could have run Nvidia, too. But now it seems we’ll have to wait a bit longer to find out.

The Riemann hypothesis, mathematics’ biggest problem, falls to AI
What happened: Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge published a Claude-assisted proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the most famous open problem in mathematics. The Riemann hypothesis outlines a rule for the distribution of prime numbers.
After several days of checking the proof’s formalization, the Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative — organizations governing mathematics’ main proof software and library respectively — released a joint statement. They wrote, “We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct, and we congratulate him on this achievement.”
In his statement announcing the proof, Alpöge wrote, “Claude and I have had a great time together with such breadth in recent months, but this time Claude far outpaced me near the finish line.”
The Riemann hypothesis is considered so difficult that few of mathematic’s best minds actually work on it. This May, MIT mathematics professor Andrew Sutherland said to Scientific American, “An AI that can prove the Riemann hypothesis is not one I’d want to meet.”
Why we’re following: When I first became interested in AI, the Riemann hypothesis was the thing AI researchers, mathematicians, and other nerds I talked to at parties held up as an outlandish benchmarks AI might not reach in our lifetimes — a sign of true intelligence, and a sign that AI had actually pushed the frontier of knowledge.
I am frankly flabbergasted that the Riemann hypothesis has been proven. While no human currently understands the full proof, scores of mathematicians have now examined the code and statement of the proof, and all have found it to be sound so far.
What people are saying:
Alex Kontorovich, a mathematics professor at Rutgers University, told the Times that after the proof, he told himself: “Well, that’s it. Now that we know that A.I. can do mathematics at the highest levels, then I can’t be shocked anymore.” He added, “I’m absolutely, absolutely shocked.”
Andrew Sutherland told Scientific American, “This is indeed an historic moment.” He added, “The world has changed.... I know there will be a wide range of reactions in our community, but to me this is a human triumph, and a day for celebration.”
“The implications of this hypothesis extend across large parts of mathematics, and hundreds of other mathematical results have been proved assuming this hypothesis is true,” Queen Mary University of London mathematics professor Abhishek Saha told Al Jazeera. “A proof would settle a question that has occupied some of the greatest mathematical minds for over 160 years.”
In an X article, Meta’s chief AI officer Alexandr Wang argued this proof was the beginning of a phase change in AI capabilities — one that called for a greater focus on AI safety. He wrote that “nothing humanity has ever accomplished compares to proving the Riemann Hypothesis in terms of sheer intellectual might and strength.”
Wang wrote that now, “nearly every field could be accelerated at a pace beyond what humans can understand or monitor.” He added that work on AI safety is urgently needed, because it is “hard to imagine a world where humanity maintains control with superintelligence that is 1000x smarter and more capable than humans in every field.”
In an X reply, Rep. Yassamin Ansari (D-AZ) wrote, “How many more of the engineers and scientists at the world’s leading AI companies need to warn us about the catastrophic consequences of inaction for us to regulate this industry?!”
