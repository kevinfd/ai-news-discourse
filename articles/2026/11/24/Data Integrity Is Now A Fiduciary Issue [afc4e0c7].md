# Data Integrity Is Now A Fiduciary Issue

Forbes · Amit Shivpuja, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-24

Amit Shivpuja is a data, AI exec, and best-selling author. He writes on AI readiness, data integrity, and the foundations of enterprise AI.
Open any public company annual report and you can see how seriously we take money. You will find audited financial statements and management’s assessment of internal control over financial reporting. Under the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, the CEO and CFO personally certify the financial reports. Since 2023, you will also find a dedicated cybersecurity section in Form 10-K.
Now look for the equivalent account of data integrity. You will not find it. Data appears as an aspiration in the business description and as a generic risk factor, if at all. Nothing tells an investor which data the business depends on, whether it has integrity or what a failure could cost.
The evidence is already in.
Gartner reported in February 2025 that 63% of organizations either do not have, or are unsure whether they have, the right data management practices for AI, and predicted that through 2026, organizations will abandon 60% of AI projects not supported by AI-ready data. Gartner’s test is blunt: If the data has issues, it is not ready for AI.
Anthropic shows what that means in production. Without curated skill files encoding governed definitions and sources of truth, Claude’s accuracy on analytics questions did not exceed 21% on Anthropic’s evaluations. With them, it runs consistently above 95%. When maintenance lapsed, accuracy drifted to about 65% within a month. The model did not change. The integrity of the context around the data did.
Integrity is the right word.
By data integrity, I mean data that remains accurate, complete, consistent and traceable from source to decision, under named ownership, with its definitions, metadata, lineage and changes controlled. Quality is one dimension of integrity. Integrity is the whole assurance.
It is a stronger word than quality, deliberately. Data has been treated as the stepchild of corporate accountability for too long: funded after the systems, governed after the decisions and questioned only after the failures. A stepchild does not get a quality program. It gets held to a standard.
Quality asks, “Is this data correct?” Integrity asks, “Can this data be trusted, traced and accounted for, end to end?”
If you cannot account for your data, you cannot govern your AI.
Boards now ask how AI is governed: which models are in use, how they are validated and who approved deployment. They’re the right questions, but they’re incomplete ones. Every answer rests on the integrity of data. A model validated on unreliable data is not well governed. An AI system whose source data has no named owner, no quality threshold and no exception process is not under control.
Put plainly, if a company cannot manage the integrity of its data, there is no credible basis for expecting it to manage its AI properly. AI governance without data integrity is governance theater.
We solved a version of this before. SOX did not appear because companies lacked accounting systems. It appeared because investors could no longer trust financial reporting without defined controls, named accountability and formal attestation. Cybersecurity disclosure followed the same logic once cyber risk became material. Data integrity is next. AI has made it unavoidable.
Put data integrity in the annual report.
Give data integrity a dedicated section in the annual report. Call it “Data Integrity and AI Risk.” Place it alongside the cybersecurity section, and cover six things:
1. Material Data Domains And Business Dependence: Which data does the business materially depend on, and what outcomes and AI systems depend on it?
2. Governance And Ownership: Who owns each material domain, what management structure oversees integrity and standards and which board committee owns the risk? If no one can be named, that is itself a disclosure.
3. Controls, Quality And Integrity Posture: What controls exist for critical data elements: definitions, metadata, lineage, quality thresholds, monitoring and change control? Showing that controls exist and are measured is enough. Sensitive detail stays out.
4. Material Integrity Failures, Risks And Incidents: Have integrity failures materially affected, or are they reasonably likely to affect, results, operations, customers or compliance? A serious integrity failure can be as consequential as a serious systems failure. It deserves the same candor.
5. AI Dependency And Traceability: Which AI systems rely on which data, and can an output be traced back to its source data, definitions and transformations? Traceability is the line between AI that can be audited and AI that must simply be believed.
6. Investment And Remediation: Where is the company investing to improve data integrity and AI readiness, and which material weaknesses remain open?
Together, these six elements would do for data integrity what financial reporting does for money: make impact and risk visible, in a consistent place, year after year.
Start with disclosure, then move to attestation.
This does not need to begin as a heavy compliance regime. First, disclose: Companies voluntarily add the section. Second, assess: Management formally assesses controls over the integrity of material data, as it already does for financial reporting. Third, attest: Over time, for the largest companies and the most material data, independent assurance follows. Accountability without verification eventually becomes narrative.
The objections are predictable. Integrity is too subjective to report. So was cybersecurity risk, until a standard structure made it reportable. Disclosure creates competitive risk; the cyber rules already balance transparency against sensitive detail. It would slow innovation. In my experience, well-designed governance accelerates delivery, because teams stop relitigating definitions on every project.
Until a standard section exists, boards and investors can ask directly: Which data is material? Who owns it, and who attests to its controls? What do we know about its integrity today, in numbers? What failure would be serious enough to reach this board?
Money is accounted for. Risk is disclosed. It is time the integrity of data, the asset AI runs on, was treated the same way. Because AI scales on readiness, not on technology and models alone. And readiness, like financial control before it, should be something a company can demonstrate, not just declare.
