# The number of babies getting vaccinated for hepatitis B at birth is dropping precipitously

Scientific American · Adam Kovac · 2026-11-24

The number of babies born in U.S. who received a vaccine for hepatitis B within a day of birth has dropped sharply, according to a new study.
Researchers at the health data firm Truveta and Callen-Lorde Community Health Center in New York City examined anonymized electronic health records from more than 1.3 million infants and found the percentage who received the shot within 24 hours of being born had fallen from 80 percent in 2022 to just 65.6 percent in 2026.
The decline extended to babies who were most at risk: up to 98 percent of mothers who tested positive for hepatitis B got their babies vaccinated in 2022, but that had fallen to just under 94 percent in 2026, the study found. The findings were published in the journal NEJM Evidence on Tuesday.
On supporting science journalism
“The hepatitis B birth dose was designed to protect every newborn, including those whose mothers may not know they’re infected,” said Nina Masters, principal research scientist at Truveta and the study’s lead author, in a statement. “No screening system is perfect. That’s why it’s so concerning to see vaccination declining across the board, even among babies whose mothers have tested positive for hepatitis B.”
Hepatitis B is a liver disease that can, if left untreated, lead to liver cancer or even death. The disease is transmissible through body fluids and can be passed from mother to baby in the womb.
By extrapolating their data to the 3.6 million births that occur in the U.S. each year, the researchers concluded that as many as 360,000 infants are likely going without the shot. While prenatal screening is widely available, they also estimated that roughly 640 unvaccinated babies may be born to mothers who have been infected with hepatitis B but have not been screened.
The number of unvaccinated infants is “alarming,” says Michael Osterholm, director of the Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy.
“They represent children who will develop chronic hepatitis B infections, cirrhosis of the liver and liver cancer,” he says. “They also send a clear signal to federal health leaders that they need a plan to address increasing rates of vaccine hesitancy other than misleadingly blaming it on the pandemic.”
In December 2025 the CDC’s vaccine advisory panel eliminated a decades-old recommendation that the hepatitis B vaccine be given universally at birth. Instead the panel recommended parents consult with a health care provider about when to give their baby the child’s first dose of the vaccine as long as the mother has tested negative for the disease.
The change quickly drew fire from public health experts, who said the decision ran contrary to the evidence and would increase infants’ vulnerability to infection.
The new study shows that vaccination rates were already declining before that policy change. The number of unvaccinated babies identified in the study grew from 20,473 in 2018 to more than 34,000 in 2022, peaking at more than 64,500 in 2025.
“We’re talking about a vaccine that has protected generations of children from a potentially devastating infection,” said study co-author Demetre Daskalakis, chief medical officer at Callen-Lorde and former director of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s National Center for Immunization and Respiratory Illness, in the same statement. “And now we’re seeing fewer newborns receive it—even those we know are at greatest risk. These numbers aren’t just a trend line. Each data point represents a real child who may be left vulnerable to a preventable disease. At a time when vaccine policy is changing, we need more evidence guiding our decisions, not less.”
