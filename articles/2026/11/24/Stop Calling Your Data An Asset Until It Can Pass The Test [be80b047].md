# Stop Calling Your Data An Asset Until It Can Pass The Test

Forbes · Daniel Fallmann, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-24

Daniel Fallmann is founder and CEO of Mindbreeze, a leader in enterprise search, applied artificial intelligence and knowledge management.
”Data is your most valuable asset” has been repeated in boardrooms so often that almost no one notices it’s usually false. On a balance sheet, something earns the word “asset” only when it’s identifiable, controlled by the company and expected to produce future economic benefit.
Most of what enterprises call their data asset fails at least one of those tests. It’s inventory nobody has counted, in a warehouse nobody has walked.
That used to be a philosophical nicety. Generative and agentic AI have made it an operating problem. AI doesn’t benefit from the mere existence of information. It only benefits from information that’s relevant, current, trustworthy, governed and connected to business context. Everything else it retrieves is a cost and increasingly a risk.
The word ‘asset’ has a definition. Most data fails it.
The experiment has already run. In 2023, China became the first major economy to allow companies to book data as a balance sheet asset, provided it was identifiable, its benefit probable and its cost measurable.
A study led by researchers at the University of Toronto found that 2.6% of listed Chinese firms actually capitalized any data in 2025, tripped up by valuation and by who even owns it. When a regulator formally invited companies to treat data as the asset everyone insists it is, nearly 98% of them couldn’t clear the bar. The same gap sits unmeasured in most Western enterprises now.
Columbia Business School’s Shivaram Rajgopal offered one detail worth keeping: a multibillion-dollar company carrying 16 separate identifiers (registration required) for the same customer. A human analyst reconciles the duplication. An agent retrieving “the customer record” finds 16, can’t tell which is authoritative and blends them into something true of no one.
The investors have noticed, too. Investment in intangible assets has overtaken tangible investment across developed markets, yet standards barely recognize it. The CFA Institute has pressed the FASB and IASB on exactly this, warning that financial statements are losing relevance because the intangible investments driving enterprise value—data among them—are nearly invisible in the numbers investors receive. 
AI removes the human filter that was hiding the problem.
Poor information quality was survivable when a person stood between the data and the decision. AI removes that filter at machine scale, acting across the enterprise in seconds with no instinct to doubt a document’s currency.
IDC’s research is blunt: Agentic AI can’t run on stale data because the lag between creation and action degrades the decision, and most agentic use cases will depend on the real-time, contextual data most estates lack. A stale document a person retrieves is an inconvenience; feeding one to a workflow propagates the error at speed.
Most of the warehouse is empty calories.
Seagate’s Rethink Data research across 1,500 enterprises found only about a third of available data is ever put to work; the rest is stored at real cost while contributing nothing. Before AI, that idle majority was a storage line item. Now it’s a liability: Every duplicate and superseded version is something an AI system can retrieve and treat as authoritative.
As systems act on information rather than present it, data and AI governance become one discipline. You can’t govern the model and neglect the data it consumes because the data is the behavior.
Treat information like a portfolio, not a hoard.
Deleting the junk isn’t enough. History holds much of an enterprise’s value, from the engineering decision six years ago to the regulatory rationale a blind cleanup would destroy. The discipline resembles portfolio management.
Some information is productive and should be easy to reach, while some carries obligations and must be kept. Some is declining and should be archived before an agent mistakes it for current, while some is pure liability and should go. The skill is continuously knowing which category each piece belongs to and which takes context: ownership, age, authority, permissions, relationships, relevance.
Knowing the categories is the easy part. Changing how an organization behaves isn’t.
Almost no executive argues with the premise. They argue with the cost of acting on it, and the objections are mostly rational:
1. Sunk Cost Dressed As Prudence: A company that spent a decade consolidating everything into one lake doesn’t want to hear that volume was the wrong target.
2. Security And Privacy: This is a fair concern since exposing information to autonomous systems can widen the attack surface.
3. Implementation: Many executives have watched enough multiyear governance projects stall.
4. Ownership: This quietly ends most initiatives. Ownership is split across legal, security, IT and the business lines, each accountable for a slice and none for the whole.
That last point is the industry’s cue. To earn adoption, we have to stop selling the destination and start removing the risk from the path. We must allow organizations to classify and connect information where it already lives rather than demanding a migration first, governance and permissions that travel with the data and measurement a CFO can read.
The hardest part isn’t technical. The providers who matter next will help a customer stand up an owner and a decision right, not just a platform.
Every CEO should apply this test.
If a body of information improves a decision, preserves knowledge that the company would be poorer without or gives an AI system trustworthy context, it’s an asset and should be resourced like one. If it multiplies complexity, buries the authoritative version beneath the obsolete or feeds a system content that should no longer influence anything, it’s a liability.
The advantage in the AI era won’t go to the enterprise with the largest data estate. That contest was won long ago, and it turned out not to matter. It’ll go to those that can tell which information earns the name asset, govern it accordingly and deliver it when people and systems need it.
Data becomes an asset the day it can pass the test. Until then, it’s just something you’re paying to keep—regardless of what the boardroom slide says.
