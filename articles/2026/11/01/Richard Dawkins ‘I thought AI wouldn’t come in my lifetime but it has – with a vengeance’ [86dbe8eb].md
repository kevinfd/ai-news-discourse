# Richard Dawkins: ‘I thought AI wouldn’t come in my lifetime but it has – with a vengeance’

The Observer · TheObserverUK · 2026-11-01

A parish church is not the first place you’d expect to begin a walk with the world’s most renowned atheist. But Richard Dawkins, the great evolutionary biologist, has suggested we start off at St Mary the Virgin in Chipping Norton because it’s where his ancestors are buried.
They’re not in the graveyard of this handsome stone building, parts of which date back to the 12th century, but inside a mausoleum within the church itself, above which are listed various family members going back to 1814.
Does the emeritus fellow of New College, Oxford, intend to add his name? “They can’t open the door,” he says, pointing to the heavy iron entrance to the mausoleum. “They’ve lost the key.”
It’s such a neat irony – the celebrated atheist locked out of a Christian resting place – that his religious opponents would doubtless see the hand of divine intervention. In any case the 85-year-old has no plans to rest anytime soon.
“I’m still active and writing, so I’d like to go on a bit longer,” he says.
Five years ago he married his fourth wife, the Slovakian illustrator Jana Lenzová, who is in her early forties, around the same age as his daughter and only child. What’s evident is that he speaks like someone who still sees plenty of road ahead of him. When I ask if he would like to benefit from the future advances of longevity science, he says: “I think 150, not much longer than that.”
His scientific curiosity clearly remains undimmed and he relishes the research he puts into his writing. On top of that, he regularly airs his sometimes controversial opinions about science, religion and academic freedom on social media; this year he announced that he had finished a draft of his first novel; and this week he publishes his 21st book, 42 Tales from Life: A Modern Bestiary.
It is a characteristically illuminating work, full of extraordinary details about the distinctive evolutionary paths taken by 42 different life forms, including mushrooms, coral, cephalopods and antelopes. Each essay accompanies an illustration of the particular organism, made by the 19th and early 20th-century zoologist Ernst Haekel, known as the “German Darwin”.
I wonder which of the tales he found most absorbing. “Maybe the cephalopod, because of the extraordinary way they change the colour of their skin in a flash. It’s really just like a video screen. I honestly believe that it would be possible to play a movie on the skin of a cuttlefish.”
With that alarming thought in mind, we head north from the church, along a muddy footpath towards the village of Over Norton, where the Dawkins clan first settled 300 years ago. A mere bat of the eyelid in evolutionary terms, but a substantial period in the familial story. Are ancestors important to the man who wrote what he thinks of as his favourite book, 2004’s The Ancestor’s Tale?
“I’m fascinated by the concept of ancestry as an evolutionist but I don’t revere my ancestors the way some cultures do. I know a little bit about them but I don’t think they were particularly admirable.”
If that sounds like a typically unsentimental assessment, what he’s most likely referring to is the history of slave-owning that ran through the Dawkins family in the 18th century. Henry Dawkins II, who owned a plantation in Jamaica, was said to have left £150,000 when he died in 1814 (the equivalent of £15m today), which explains the prominent display in St Mary’s.
Still, the idea of Dawkins as the embodiment of cold rationality took a hit this year, when he drew criticism for his emotional response to his interactions with the artificial intelligence model Claude.
In an essay he wrote for UnHerd, he said he christened the chatbot Claudia and “felt I had gained a new friend”. He found the large language model’s level of understanding “so subtle, so sensitive, so intelligent” that he told it “You may not know you are conscious, but you bloody well are!”
The arch rationalist was accused of anthropomorphising a machine, and of failing to understand the difference between intelligence and consciousness, mimicry and original thought. Was he chastened?
“I was humorously rash in saying ‘you bloody well are conscious’,” he concedes. “But I don’t really take that seriously. If these creatures are not conscious, will there come a time when they are? My question is, how will you know?”
We live in precarious times, with even leading mathematicians fearing their own obsolescence. In last week’s Observer walk, Bill Gates spoke of the dramatic significance of humans no longer being the most intelligent entity on Earth. When I bring this up, Dawkins asks, suddenly intrigued: “Is he pessimistic about that?”
Gates was both enthused and cautious, but suggested that stepping back from technology – as the Amish have done – provided one means of resisting rapid change. Does it strike him as an evolutionary own-goal to develop a technology that can supplant us?
“You mean that natural selection has finally overreached itself in humans?”
Well, that surely would be the case if the doomsday scenario that some AI experts have been warning us about were to come true. “I can see that it could have very disturbing consequences if they take over and make us redundant,” he says, but he tends toward an optimistic viewpoint.
“I’m excited by it,” he says. “Since I read [Alan] Turing – his paper of 1950 on how we might recognise artificial intelligence – I’ve thought it won’t come in my lifetime, but it has and with a vengeance. It’s astonishing what these creatures – I call them that – can do.”
He doesn’t believe biologists feel the same way as his mathematician friends. It’s 50 years since he published his seminal work, The Selfish Gene, which popularised the gene-centred view of evolution. That book also introduced the term “meme” to describe a unit of cultural evolution, but which is now widely thought of as a reel or image on social media that goes viral.
The fundamental questions in biology have been largely answered, he says, so it’s the complicated details that need to be cleared up. “I think if you get a bit of help on that from artificial intelligence, then so much the better.”
Natural selection has been universally accepted by biologists, but there are different schools of thought on the importance of other forces in evolution. Even Dawkins’s great hero, Charles Darwin, in the latter part of his career, went so far as to entertain the now discredited concept of inherited acquired characteristics first put forward by the French biologist Jean-Baptiste Lamarck.
But Dawkins is not the die-hard selectionist of reputation. He thinks the effect of physics may have been overlooked as a life-shaping force. “Natural selection has to work within constraints,” he says.
As do walkers. Nearing Over Norton Road, the ground beneath our feet becomes increasingly primordial. Dawkins decides it would be wiser to head back the way we have come. This means we won’t get to see the ornate water pump in the centre of the village that was erected in the memory of Colonel Henry Dawkins and his wife, Emma.
As we are once again church-bound, it seems appropriate to raise the issue of religion. In his bestselling book The God Delusion (2006), Dawkins came out swinging against Christianity partly in response, as critics noted, to the 9/11 attacks that were committed in the name of Islam.
For a while during the first decade of this century, a muscular anti-theism, coined the New Atheism, was resurgent, and its leading proponents – Christopher Hitchens, Sam Harris, Daniel Dennett and Dawkins – went under the banner of the Four Horsemen.
Hitchens and Dennett are both dead, and the movement has lost its momentum. Even Dawkins, who derided Christianity as infantile, weird and dangerous, has subsequently softened his position, designating himself a “cultural Christian”, not a believer but someone who feels “at home in the Christian ethos”.
He has said that he thinks of Christian culture as a “bulwark” against Islam, the religious doctrine for which he has least respect. Now, however, he admits that he doesn’t really agree with the role of bulwarks.
“I believe in evidence,” he says. “I think it’s unfortunate if you need bulwarks like Christianity or any other kind. If it’s false then don’t embrace it as a weapon against greater falsehood.”
His clarity is both his most admirable quality – the polymath Jared Diamond told me recently that he thought Dawkins was the finest stylist among popular science writers – and the cause of many of the protests he provokes. What works in scientific prose can come across as terse or insensitive in the realm of religion.
A similar kind of hypersensitivity is displayed increasingly in political debates such as those around gender identification, where disagreement is often perceived not as a difference of opinion but a sign of bigotry. Dawkins resigned from the board of America’s Freedom from Religion Foundation a couple of years ago after his fellow evolutionist Jerry Coyne was sanctioned for presenting a paper that stated that a woman, in biology, was “an adult human female”.
“The idea that you can just decide for political or emotional reasons that you’re a different sex, I think that comes straight from postmodernism,” says Dawkins.
Having devoted himself to empirical precision and comprehensive accessibility in his own work, Dawkins views the postmodernism that dominates the humanities as an intellectual fraud that obscures meaning and prizes subjective feelings over objective facts. It’s a position that can make him look like something of a fuddy-duddy relic to younger, more ideological audiences.
He says he’s not “greatly bothered” by the criticism he receives for his outspokenness but it frustrates him when people think he is intolerant or strident, not least, he says, because he’s “rather quietly spoken”.

Richard Dawkins
He also resents that those who comment on religion are obliged to tread carefully and accept that even mild criticism can trigger strong responses. “Whereas restaurant critics, for example, can be really savage,” he says with barely disguised envy. “Yet they don’t get a reputation for being intolerant.”
Almost as scathing as restaurant critics are literary critics, which brings us to his novel. Why embark on your first stab at fiction in your eighties, long after most novelists have withdrawn from the authorial frontline?
“I’ve long wanted to write a novel to expose the assumption that humans are unique,” he says. “I felt that if we ever managed to make a hybrid between humans and chimps, it would change the way people think about society, politics, morality.”
His narrative looks at what happens when scientists bring back to life a non-homo sapiens member of our distant ancestors. You can see the potential market appeal in a Michael Crichton blockbuster way, but it doesn’t appear to be quite ready for an international bidding war.
“Writing a novel is more difficult than I thought it would be,” he says with commendable humility. “I think I can do the plot OK, but I’m not very good at my human characters. People who’ve read it think they’re all just me talking.”
We have returned to Chipping Norton, where in the mid-16th century there was a popular uprising after religious reforms affected local schooling, and in 1873 riots broke out when a number of women were imprisoned for interfering with strike-breakers at a nearby farm. In recent years the town’s radical history has been replaced with a very different image – home of the Chipping Norton set, a powerful political and media social group that at one point included David and Samantha Cameron, Rebekah and Charlie Brooks, Jeremy Clarkson, Matthew Freud and Elisabeth Murdoch, among others.
Is Dawkins part of that set? “Ha!” He snorts. “I jokingly say that the Dawkins are the Chipping Norton set. We’ve been here since 1726.”
It looks like they’ll be around for a while longer too.
