# Doomsday prepping goes mainstream

Axios · Avery Lotz · 2026-11-19

The internet has caught the emergency preparation bug , fueled by fears of AI-led cyberattacks , power grid vulnerabilities, transportation standstills and international threats . The big picture: The "prepper" mindset is resonating with larger slices of society — who are packing emergency bags, stocking up on water and taking inventory of non-perishables. Michael Mills, a University of Greenwich senior lecturer who studies doomsday preparation, says some people are turning to "lightweight versions" of traditional prepping amid doubts about global stability. By the numbers: Sales at Survival Frog, a survival retailer that sells gear like emergency food and radios, saw sales jump 450% between Oct. 27 and Nov. 16, compared to prior weeks, CEO and founder Byron Walker tells Axios. Competitors saw similar trends, he says. And most recent buyers at Survival Frog were new customers. Food, including canned meats, leads demand, Walker says, followed by products like radios, solar generators, battery packs and water filters. State of play: Systems the world relies on for travel , communications , business , food, health care and more have been disrupted in recent weeks — whether because of tech shortfalls or malicious acts. While officials assure the incidents aren't broadly connected, fears of critical infrastructure failing are growing, and more people considering how to prepare for crises. "It does seem much more mainstream now," Walker says of preppers. "You can't turn on the news without hearing about a new cyber attack or a tech meltdown." Many of the emergency preparation skills people are using have long been observed in disaster-prone areas. And as many Americans learned during COVID, supply chain disruptions mean having extra water, radios and shelf-stable foods around the house isn't a bad thing. Krystal, known online as the "Queen of Doomsday," spends about five hours daily coaching new preppers and told Axios that recent online engagement with her content, which she says has more than doubled across platforms, has been "mind-blowing." "Families are generally concerned —...people who would have normally turned their nose away from it and thought, 'No, nothing could ever happen.' We've seen lockdowns. We've seen supply chains be crippled. We've seen oil crises, inflation," she says. Between the lines: Overlapping tech oddities boosted "a sense of anxiety about social stability around us, knowing that planes can be grounded, or fridges can all of a sudden become heaters," Mills says. Costco has become an apparent cornucopia for concerned consumers, with sales on goods like rice, canned goods and water reportedly surging. Walker attributes the Survival Frog sales boom to the U.K. government and EU urging households to prepare three days of supplies, as well as the Pentagon readiness plan for military bases to operate at least 90 days, per Fox reporting , if civilian systems fail. That plan fueled rumors about a 90-day "blackout" across the U.S. Worth noting: Asked about the outages and Pentagon preparations, Vice President JD Vance told Fox News' Laura Ingraham that friends have asked whether they should be "preparing to build a bunker" or stock up on food and water. "And my answer to that is no," he continued. "We do not see some crisis coming down." The bottom line: But Krystal said in a video response to Vance, "Yes, we should be preparing."
