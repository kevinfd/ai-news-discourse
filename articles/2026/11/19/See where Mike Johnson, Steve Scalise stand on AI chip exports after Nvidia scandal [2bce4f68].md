# See where Mike Johnson, Steve Scalise stand on AI chip exports after Nvidia scandal

NOLA.com · Meghan Friedmann · 2026-11-19

U.S. House Speaker Mike Johnson, a Shreveport Republican, has signaled his support for tightening controls on artificial intelligence chip exports after the CEO of a leading chip producer resigned amid allegations of romantic relationships with employees and a Chinese spy.
The U.S. House has stalled for months on taking action on a set of bipartisan measures that aim to curb China’s access to chips. But Johnson’s public support, which comes after Democrats won control of Congress in the Nov. 3 midterm elections, signals a bigger shift in Washington in favor of AI regulations. Many lawmakers on both sides of the aisle say high-end AI chips could empower China to make gains on the U.S. in the race for superior AI technology.
“The export controls on chips to China has been a big issue around here. I’m in favor of that — of limiting those exports,” Johnson told Capitol Hill reporters in a recent recorded interview. “Especially in light of recent developments, I think it makes sense.”
His comments came the same day that Jensen Huang, the CEO of the tech company Nvidia, announced his resignation. As Huang’s company produced advanced AI chips, he also reportedly had an affair with a tech executive whom U.S. intelligence deemed a Chinese spy, according to The New York Times. He resigned after revelations of another romantic relationship with an internal staffer.
As speaker, Johnson plays a key role in setting legislative agendas, as does House Majority Leader Steve Scalise, another Louisiana Republican. Both have been in lockstep with President Donald Trump, a close ally of Huang’s.
“The ground has really shifted quite a bit towards (chip export restrictions), and I think Mike Johnson’s public position now is a reflection of that,” said Riki Parikh, policy director of the Alliance for Secure AI, a Washington-based nonpartisan organization that lobbies for AI regulations.
Trump, who is in regular contact with Huang and whose investment portfolio includes Nvidia stock, has supported Huang throughout the fallout from his reported affairs and even called for his reinstatement as CEO.
Meanwhile, Scalise appears skeptical of restricting AI chip exports. In a statement, a spokesperson for the majority leader cautioned that too many regulations could harm the U.S. economy.
The statement said Scalise was “working with the White House on legislative text to ensure export control legislation is done correctly without unduly tying the administration’s hands on foreign policy or unintentionally taking billions out of the American economy.”
The Nvidia scandal has heightened concerns that third-country companies could route AI chips to China, sparking outcry from lawmakers over guardrails on AI chip export restrictions. It has also raised fears about the future of the AI industry, which is a major driver of the American economy; Nvidia has played a key role in backing AI investments.
The U.S. permits American companies to export some lower-level AI chips to China, but it does not allow the export of Nvidia’s most advanced chips.
Huang has lobbied against export restrictions, arguing they could hinder American tech companies from maintaining dominance over foreign competitors. The lack of exports could encourage China to develop its own powerful chips, he’s warned.
This week, the Trump administration backed rules that restrict remote access to advanced AI chips and make it harder for Southeast Asian countries to obtain them — another sign that tighter regulations may be in store.
White House officials did not answer questions about whether the administration supports other restrictions proposed by some lawmakers, who want to pass them by the end of the year.
Possible legislation
Three key bills that aim to curb China’s access to advanced AI chips are under consideration in Congress: the MATCH Act, the AI Overwatch Act and the Chip Security Act. All three cleared the House Foreign Affairs Committee during the first half of the year but the full House never debated them, according to Parikh.
The MATCH Act aims to prevent China from obtaining tools to manufacture advanced chips, while the AI Overwatch Act gives Congress more authority to review and block chip exports to certain countries, according to the House Committee on Foreign Affairs.
And the Chip Security Act would require AI chips to include tracking mechanisms in case they end up in unintended locations, U.S. Rep. Bill Huizenga said in a news release last year. Huizenga, a Republican from Michigan, is a sponsor of the legislation.
In the wake of the Nvidia scandal, some U.S. senators have also pushed to include language to limit exports of AI chips in the National Defense Authorization Act, which authorizes military spending.
A bipartisan pair of U.S. senators — Elizabeth Warren, a Democrat from Massachusetts, and U.S. Sen. Jim Banks, a Republican from Indiana — recently said in a joint statement that Congress should “cut off pathways through which China accesses advanced chips.”
The two said the news of Huang's affairs makes "national security concerns even more urgent and raises important questions NVIDIA must answer."
The military funding bill is considered “must-pass” legislation, according to Parikh. The House passed a version in July that does not include the chip restrictions, he said, meaning the Senate and House may need to negotiate a final version of the bill through a process known as reconciliation. The Senate has not yet passed its version of the legislation, which is currently being hotly debated.
A spokesperson for Johnson did not respond to questions about which exact export restrictions the speaker supports.
If the Republican-led Congress does not pass the export restrictions by the end of the year, Democrats appear likely to pursue them once they take over in January. Warren is slated to chair the Senate Committee on Banking, Housing and Urban Affairs, which generally handles export restriction legislation.
Johnson and Scalise are set to lose their leadership roles next year. Rumblings that they could resign from the U.S. House have swirled since Republicans took a beating in the midterms. Neither has confirmed those plans.
Nvidia in the news
The Nvidia revelations burst into public view on Oct. 29, when The New York Times reported that Huang had had a romantic relationship with Alice Huang, a former customer from Megaspeed International. U.S. intelligence had determined Alice Huang was a Chinese spy, according to the report.
The revelations set off several tumultuous weeks of news coverage, including additional reports that Jensen Huang had romantic relationships with two subordinates at his company.
"I owe my family and all of you an apology," Jensen Huang said in an email to employees that came several days before his resignation. "I love our country and our company. I am committed to protecting NVIDIA and supporting America's national security."
Nvidia has launched an independent investigation into Jensen Huang's affair.
Megaspeed, the company where Alice Huang was an executive, was the subject of a 2024 Department of Commerce Investigation amid allegations it was illicitly sending advanced Nvidia chips to China. In December, Megaspeed there was “no evidence” to support those allegations. The Chinese government has denied that Alice Huang is a spy.
Alice Huang and Jensen Huang are not related.
U.S. intelligence briefed Trump roughly a year ago on Alice Huang’s ties to Jensen Huang and the president took no action, The Times reported. Jensen Huang was not briefed, the paper reported.
On Monday, Trump, who has sought to improve economic ties with China, suggested Alice Huang might not be a spy.
“Any woman coming to the country — that’s a beautiful woman that comes from China — they say she’s a spy,” he said.
