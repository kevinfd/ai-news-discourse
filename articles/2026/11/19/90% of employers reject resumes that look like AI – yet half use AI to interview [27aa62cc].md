# 90% of employers reject resumes that look like AI – yet half use AI to interview

ZDNet · Radhika Rajkumar · 2026-11-19

ZDNET’s key takeaways
- New Greenhouse data shows how AI suspicion is impacting hiring.
- Still, companies say they’re using AI to screen more candidates.
- A tough job market may mean candidates are more willing to engage with AI than they would otherwise.
Hiring managers are more suspicious than ever of AI use in applications, and it’s impacting who they consider. At the same time, they’re turning to AI to help screen more candidates — meaning those videos you’ve seen on the internet of people pranking AI interviewers aren’t going away anytime soon.
New data from hiring systems firm Greenhouse found that “nine in ten US employers (90%) believe they’ve turned away real candidates because those applications seemed fake or AI-generated.” ZDNET has asked Greenhouse to clarify whether that 90% of employers reported putting applications through an AI detection tool, or are just intuiting that those applications seem fake.

More from ZDNET
Employers are also reacting to AI by adjusting their hiring processes: 86% of those surveyed reported changes to account for AI’s impact. 57% of them did so by adding another human interview.
“As jobseekers rely more on AI to polish and send out their job applications, employers are finding it harder to tell who’s actually right for the role,” the report stated, echoing an ongoing push-and-pull between applicants and hiring teams.
AI resume screeners automatically toss a majority of applications they deem unfit, so applicants try not to get tossed by formatting their material for the screener, which then backfires anyway.
AI exists on both ends
The report shows how a tough economy and an influx of AI hiring tools are colliding, arguably to benefit employers over applicants. Greenhouse noted that, ironically, AI tools are helping hiring managers while also making resumes untrustworthy, and applicants would rather be interviewed by AI than not at all.
“A growing number of employers are using AI not just to filter candidates out, but to have conversations with more candidates, earlier, through AI-powered voice technology,” Greenhouse said, adding that “half now use AI interviews to hear from more candidates, and 40% of those have found strong candidates they would have missed.”
What we don’t, and maybe can’t, know is whether AI screeners have incorrectly influenced hiring managers to turn away candidates they actually should have considered.

The report surveyed 1,347 hiring managers and recruiters across the US, UK, Germany, Australia, and Ireland from late September to mid-October, and found that half of employers using voice AI “say the early-stage candidate experience has improved.”
It’s unclear what “improved” means there, though, and whether applicants themselves would agree with that. Still, when given the choice, only 7% of applicants in the US would turn down an AI interview, per an earlier Greenhouse report. Additionally, “70% of those who’ve had a live AI interview say it helped them show their skills,” that report said.
But how swayed is that by the state of the US job market? October’s job report was better than expected, but it’s still been a tough year overall for job-seekers. Greenhouse’s findings could be as much a reflection of how desperate applicants are as an endorsement of or comfort with AI screenings.

“For most applicants, a human conversation was never on the table,” said Greenhouse’s Head of Voice AI Ophir Samson. “The real choice is a resume lost in a pile, or 15 minutes to tell their story. Every candidate should get the second.”
What hiring managers are still handling
Greenhouse found that while employers are using AI to screen more candidates, most are maintaining human oversight over the most important decisions in the hiring process.
“Of all the tasks employers would hand to an AI agent, deciding which candidates to advance came last, at just 7%,” Greenhouse said.
A recent California law aims to protect employees from being fired by AI systems that make determinations about their performance without a human co-signing the assessment. As with many AI productivity tools in the workplace, proper adoption ideally equates to more time for human workers to handle more important tasks.

“Of employers who’ve saved time with AI, 85% put it back into candidates, most often by spending more time with shortlisted candidates (45%) and interviewing more candidates earlier (40%),” Greenhouse said.
The main question around AI interviewers remains whether they’re correctly capturing your interview the way you’d hope a human interviewer would (though that’s still deeply subjective and out of applicants’ control anyway).
“Today, only three in 100 applications ever reach an interview, and adding more human interviews can’t close that gap,” Greenhouse CEO and co-founder Daniel Chait said. “The fix is scaling the top of the funnel by opening up a voice AI interview to every applicant, letting more people get a real first conversation sooner.”
His stance is unsurprising given that Greenhouse itself offers a voice AI screening interview tool.
Ultimately, a fuller picture might compare Greenhouse’s data with the number of applicants hired after successfully passing both an AI and a human interviewer.
