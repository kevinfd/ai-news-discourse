# 2026’s Words of the Year, So Far

TIME · Chad de Guzman · 2026-11-19

It can be difficult to sum up a year in a word, but that hasn’t stopped dictionaries from trying.
Last year, they picked terms like “rage bait,” “AI slop,” and even the slang 67 (pronounced six-seven), in attempts to capture the cultural zeitgeist. For 2026, some dictionaries have already begun to announce their “word of the year.”
Each dictionary approaches the selection differently—some track language use or online search trends—and some even offer shortlists of what other words were considered.
Here are some of 2026’s words of the year, so far.
Cambridge Dictionary: human
Cambridge Dictionary, which has picked a word of the year since 2015, announced it chose “human” for 2026 in response to the prevalence of AI and breakthroughs in the technology. “The year was defined by global debates on what it means to be human when AI is blurring the boundaries between people and machines,” the dictionary’s announcement said.
Cambridge noted in a blog post that the word had increasingly appeared in compounds like “human-written” and “human-generated.” In its announcement, Cambridge also cited public figures, such as Nobel laureate Geoffrey Hinton or Pope Leo XIV, who have used the word when speaking about AI and what humanity can do in turn. It referred to Pope Leo XIV’s statements about remaining “profoundly human” at the age of AI, and artists’ rejection of the “assault on human creativity” in recent years.
“AI is changing how we talk about being human,” Cambridge’s lexicographer Colin McIntosh said. “Fears about what humans may lose when we outsource our thinking have made us reconsider the extent to which we rely on AI: from top mathematicians to teachers of young children.”
Tom McClelland, a philosopher of science at the University of Cambridge, said that he thinks the choice is “interesting” because “right now our understanding of humanity is changing.”
“Human” was chosen among other contenders—some of which were also related to rapid technology development, such as “agent” (referring to an AI system that autonomously functions on behalf of humans) and “analogue” (in opposition to digital-centric lifestyles).
Collins Dictionary: mogging
Collins Dictionary, which began selecting its word of the year in 2013, delved into Gen Z and Gen Alpha slang for its 2026 pick: “mogging,” which it defines as “the act of outshining or outperforming someone or something.” Collins said the word is believed to have come from the slang noun “mog,” which meant a dominant male and is, in turn, a shortening of AMOG, an acronym for “Alpha Male Of the Group.”
According to Collins, the word has been used since around 2016, emerging in bodybuilding culture to refer to the gym’s most physically dominant male. But it gained new life in the “manosphere,” the mostly-online cultural network revolving around masculinity, men’s issues, and, often, anti-feminism.
It’s been increasingly used by famous figures, too. In June, the White House’s official X account used a form of the word. “Mogged,” read a caption for a photo of President Donald Trump holding a poster board that compared the length of the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool to the height of popular skyscrapers. U.S. figure skater Alysa Liu, who won gold in the 2026 Winter Olympics, had said that her main goal was “to mog.” Former California Governor Arnold Schwarzenegger also shared on social media that someone had explained “frame-mogging” to him, along with a montage of clips flexing his physique in his bodybuilding days.
Collins’ shortlist of other contenders for the word of the year were carspreading, which it defined as “the tendency of car manufacturers to build vehicles that are longer, wider and heavier,” and firewave, a British English climate change-related compound that means “a series of urban wildfires within a short period of time, usually caused by an extended period of hot, dry weather,” which Europe experienced this summer.
Dictionary.com: maxxing
Dictionary.com, which has announced a word of the year since 2010, picked for 2026 the Gen Z and Gen Alpha slang term “maxxing,” which it said could both stand on its own and be affixed to another word—to indicate that an object, aspect, or activity is being optimized.
“Maxxing” beat others on Dictionary.com’s shortlist, which included “affordability,” “data center,” “odyssey,” and “unc.”
With roots in words like “maximum” and “maximize,” the word “maxxing,” according to Dictionary.com, branched out from the gaming term “min-maxing,” which meant strategizing to shape a game character’s strengths to make weaknesses relatively negligible. Dictionary.com theorizes that somewhere along the way, the “min-” part got dropped, and it found an extra “x.”
“Maxxing” went mainstream in the suffix form of “looksmaxxing.” In 2023, the New York Times reported on so-called “looksmaxxers,” a digital community of mostly young men who are trying to “mog,” in the belief that achieving peak physical masculinity would guarantee them success in their romantic lives. While some resorted to controlled diets and rigorous exercise regimens, others took “looksmaxxing” to extremes, such as repeated cosmetic surgeries and chemical modifications and even “bonesmashing.”
Dictionary.com said in its announcement that searches for “maxxing” and related terms spiked in 2026, and that “maxxing” has also taken hold beyond the manosphere. In health, some people have turned to practices like fibermaxxing, proteinmaxxing, and sleepmaxxing. The word has also been used to describe cultural phenomena: earlier this year, a growing fascination with China and Chinese culture led social media users to embrace “Chinamaxxing.”
The word “captures the very 2026 urge to keep pushing harder while also giving us a way to poke fun at ourselves when optimizing our lives starts to feel absurd,” the online dictionary said. “The open question is whether this need for optimization has peaked or if we’ll push even harder next year. Maxxingmaxxing, anyone?”
