# Why the Senate energy permitting bill might become law

Axios · Ben Geman · 2026-11-19

If the sweeping Senate permitting bill becomes law, it will reflect a rare convergence of political and market forces strong enough to trump the Beltway aversion to big, bipartisan deals. Why it matters: The bill could transform how America builds energy infrastructure, with major implications for everything from electricity costs to data centers. Getting rid of bureaucratic hurdles for energy projects is a top priority for the fossil fuels and renewables sectors. But critics argue the plan would unacceptably weaken environmental protections and the rights of Native American tribes. The latest: A vote on final passage of the White House-backed bill could occur Thursday after the Senate plows through some more amendments. The big picture: Many industries — pipelines, transmission, housing, you name it — call permitting a long bureaucratic and judicial maze that stymies projects. Yes, but: That, on its own, isn't nearly enough to get this far. Here are other reasons the bill has a path to becoming law after years of failed efforts to upend permitting. 🗳️ The cost-of-living election. The midterms were soaked in affordability politics at a time of rising energy bills. While critics can call the projected savings modest, cost is politically front and center. The Rhodium Group estimates that big provisions it analyzed would cut average residential power prices by 3%-4% in 2035. Third Way, a group that favors the bill, supported the research firm's work. 📈 Rising electricity demand. U.S. power thirst has been rising in the last few years after staying largely flat for decades, driven partly by AI and wider electrification of the economy. 🦋 A maturing clean energy industry. As sectors like renewables and storage become more mainstream, " more developers and companies are seeing the real-world challenges" of doing projects, clean energy lobbyist Jeff Navin notes via email. That puts them in agreement with utilities and oil and gas companies. "They all run into problems getting the permits needed to build energy infrastructure," said Navin, an Obama-era Energy Department aide and former Democratic Capitol Hill staff member. Easier permitting is needed for transmission to carry the industries' power, as well as for building new generation itself. 😣 A mercurial White House was focused. The White House's National Energy Dominance Council was engaged with the bill. Co-author Shelly Moore Capito (R-W.Va.) credited then-chief of staff Susie Wiles as a helpful player at the Sept. 30 bill rollout. Axios' Alex Isenstadt reported that new chief of staff Stephen Miller also wants a permitting bill. Trump officials have also allowed some onshore wind projects bottled up by Pentagon reviews to proceed. 🔀 Shifting Democratic coalitions. Multiple analyses show the bill bringing somewhat lower power sector emissions compared to a world without it. But when climate had more political juice a few years ago, a bill backed by oil industry groups probably would not have had this much Democratic buy-in. Democratic co-authors and other supporters in the Dem coalition say easing the clean energy buildout is key to more climate progress in the future. More moderate center-left voices and think tanks have new cachet, while environmental groups opposing the bill haven't made enough inroads despite longstanding ties to Democrats. The intrigue: Data centers have been and remain a wildcard. There's a big political and public backlash, and opponents of the bill have seized on it, charging that the bill would help Big Tech by making the buildout easier. But bill authors and backers are emphasizing language designed to ensure that data centers pay for grid upgrades and have higher transmission service costs. What we're watching: The House. If it can pass the bill before the end of the year, there's a good chance the bill will get signed into law. If not, it's just another failed effort.
