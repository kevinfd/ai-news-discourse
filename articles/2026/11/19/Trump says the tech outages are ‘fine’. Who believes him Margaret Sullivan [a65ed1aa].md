# Trump says the tech outages are ‘fine’. Who believes him? | Margaret Sullivan

The Guardian · Margaret Sullivan · 2026-11-19

As outages and disruptions have piled up over recent days, Americans have been searching for answers.
In less than a week, Microsoft went down, the FAA grounded all flights, banking systems were roiled, and the National Security Agency’s internal network failed.
Is this just a series of random, if troubling, coincidences? Or is it a sign of digital Armageddon – the start of a burgeoning cyberattack that might take everything down?
In normal times, one might expect the president to have some serious answers and to be forthright in either warning or reassuring the nation.
Either way, we would hear something sensible from the country’s leader and the subject-area experts within the administration. If they didn’t know every answer, they would say so and point to how they were investigating, while promising transparency along the way.
But in these times, we can expect nothing of the kind.
Donald Trump, and many in his administration, are practiced and inveterate liars.
Falsehood is too often a predictable part of what we hear from on high.
The only reasonable response on the part of the public is to shrug and hope that maybe someone else eventually will reveal the truth.
So while Trump himself has declared that everything is fine or blamed Joe Biden and the former transportation secretary Pete Buttigieg for problems, we know not to believe this unless we are Maga diehards.
And when the current transportation secretary, Sean Duffy, makes his pronouncements about what caused a nation-wide grounding of flights – first, overloaded switches and then the conversion of copper to fiber-optic equipment at a remote facility – we know to take it with a huge dose of skepticism.
When, following Duffy’s various explanations, dozens of air-traffic controllers expressed their disagreement, it was easier to credit them than Duffy himself. At least they seemed to have some expertise.
The Trump administration and its spokespeople have lost public trust so thoroughly that they simply cannot – and should not – be believed.
During Trump’s first administration, his documented misstatements and falsehoods numbered in the tens of thousands, according to the Washington Post’s Glenn Kessler.
Then, of course, came the Big Lie – Trump’s refusal to admit the truth that that Joe Biden had won the 2020 election – and all that followed it, including the deadly insurrection at the US Capitol.
So now, when disaster strikes, or threatens to strike, why should we believe what Trump dismissively told a group of reporters in the Philippines about the recent outages: “It’s life … When you’re big and powerful and you have all the money, things like that happen, it’s fine.”
This is glib. But there’s a darker side to it, too.
The great German-born political philosopher Hannah Arendt – a student of authoritarianism in the 20th century – warned about what happens when the public is consistently misled by its leaders.
“The result of a consistent and total substitution of lies for factual truth,” she insisted, “ … is that the sense by which we take our bearings in the real world” is destroyed.
In fact, she believed, it’s one of the purposes of political disinformation: to disorient and confuse and thus make a baffled populace more malleable.
In recent days, it hasn’t been Trump or members of his administration who have provided the most clarity.
It’s experts far from the Oval Office.
Nicole Perlroth, who for years led the New York Times cybersecurity coverage, has been a credible and sought-after voice, in part because of the expertise established by her bestselling 2021 book about cyberwarfare, This is How They Tell Me the World Ends.
She filed a detailed post that – in contrast to Trump’s reassurances – provided some worthwhile analysis, and troubling food for thought.
She was responding in part to a Washington Sun report that Wall Street executives and the NSA had told Congress that the outages were caused by efforts to quickly fix vulnerable systems identified by AI. In essence, the hurry to patch things up during the initial period of known vulnerability overwhelmed various networks.
Perlroth didn’t disagree, but she went much further.
“We are now entirely dependent on software systems that almost nobody understands end to end, that increasingly few people can repair, and that attackers can now probe at machine speed,” she wrote.
“We are entering a period of unprecedented technological instability … a period of cascading failures, whether triggered by attackers, bad updates or our own accumulated complexity.”
Not only is her prediction a far cry from Trump’s facile shrug. It’s also a much more sophisticated interpretation than Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s public assurance that the outages are “not inter-related”.
That may be narrowly true in the sense that malign actors didn’t seem to be involved, as many feared. But in a broader sense, the breakdowns surely are interrelated. They are part of a pattern, fueled in part by AI’s growing capabilities.
It’s all worrisome, even scary. If there’s any good news in this situation, perhaps it’s that we’ve been reminded of whom, in a crisis, not to trust or believe.
That part, at least, is easy to figure out.
- Margaret Sullivan is a Guardian US columnist writing on media, politics and culture
