# Replit’s CEO on its stunning turnaround, ‘AI psychosis,’ and the risk Silicon Valley is ignoring

Fast Company · Robert Safian · 2026-11-19

A couple of years ago, Replit was flatlining at a few million dollars in annual revenue and running a mostly empty office after a layoff. Today the vibe coding pioneer is valued at $9 billion, up from $3 billion just six months ago. CEO Amjad Masad reveals what made the turnaround possible. Masad also weighs in on what he calls “AI psychosis,” and says cybersecurity, not AI extinction, is the risk worth focusing on.
This is an abridged transcript of an interview from Rapid Response, recorded live at Masters of Scale Summit 2026 and hosted by former Fast Company editor-in-chief Robert Safian. From the team behind the Masters of Scale podcast, Rapid Response features candid conversations with today’s top business leaders navigating real-time challenges. The growth you’ve seen has been extraordinary. From the inside, what does that feel like? Is it exhilarating when everything is growing so fast? Is it terrifying? Do you have to pinch yourself to say, “Is this real?”
It’s all of the above. The first feeling I had was a little bit of vindication, because I started talking about everyone being able to code and create software back in 2010, when I was building the kind of precursor to Replit as an open-source project. Then I was a founding engineer at Codecademy, where we taught a lot of people to code. And when we started Replit in 2016, we were like, “We want to blur this distinction between learning to code and doing code, and build an environment that really makes it so that anyone with an idea can build software.” And for a long time, people didn’t believe us. Raising capital was hard. Recruiting people was hard.
Because some of the technology wasn’t ready yet.
It wasn’t ready. It was on track, and I could see it happening. There were many moments where the company could have died, or I could have quit. It would have been very easy to quit at many different moments. And so in 2024, when we invented what would later be called vibe coding, when we came up with the first fully natural-language-based programming environment, there was nothing out there at the time. People really appreciated it, and we started seeing these stories of people having ideas.
One of the early experiences I had was someone recording a video of themselves and posting it on Twitter and YouTube where they were crying because they had this idea of overlaying all their memories and their photos on a map and being able to explore their life through this map. It’s a simple idea, but it’s a personal app. It’s not even a commercial app. And he was crying because he said he had this idea for so long. He tried to do it with all these different technologies, and for the first time, he was able to do it.
