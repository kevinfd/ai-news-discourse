# Ram through datacentres no one wants and hope the AI bubble never bursts: that’s the UK’s growth plan | George Monbiot

The Guardian · George Monbiot · 2026-11-19

It should have the status of an economic law: the sectors a government does most to promote in the name of growth are the sectors most likely to tank the economy. In the UK and the US, deregulation was meant to turn the financial sector into a never-ending growth machine. We have not yet fully recovered from the crash it helped cause in 2008. Fossil fuels are the most heavily subsidised sector on Earth. All that public money makes us highly vulnerable to oil price shocks, the whims of autocrats and societal collapse caused by climate breakdown. As for the great growth priority of our time – AI and datacentres – well, don’t take it from me. As Anthropic’s IPO prospectus warns, advanced AI systems could pose “catastrophic or existential risks to humanity”.
One thing we learned from the US midterm elections is that the drive to build datacentres is less a function of real commercial demand than of the need to sustain the share price of the big tech companies. After citizens across the US voted for moratoria on new datacentres, tech stocks, which have become dangerously dominant, plummeted, triggering fears of wider contagion. Whether new datacentres are required or not, we must keep building them, because confidence depends on them, and without that confidence the bubble will pop. The more you build, and the more this value dependency grows, the bigger the bubble becomes.
This could be why, combined with its catastrophic reliance on commercial lobbyists to tell it what to think, the UK government has enabled datacentres to be classified as “nationally significant infrastructure”, allowing them to swerve local planning control and go straight to ministers for approval. This, the government reasons, is because they are critical to future growth. But growth of what? Some good things, to be sure. But also – and perhaps predominantly – of gambling and pornography, which includes paedophiles nudifying children’s images. Reports of this crime have risen by 8,400% in the past two years, and it has doubtless contributed to that infallible measure of public wellbeing, GDP. Other beneficiaries might include shitposters, fascists using algorithms to boost their content and Russian cyber-attackers.
The government has admitted that while it grants critical special dispensation to these datacentres, it doesn’t know what they are going to be used for. But their ostensible purpose is beside the point. Their real purpose is to sustain the growth in tech shares: growth that could trigger economic collapse.
This is why you must do your duty before God and country, by allowing a vast, roaring, windowless box to loom over your home. This is why you must put your objections aside, and let it destroy your landscape and local character, block out the sun by day and flood the night sky with light, suck the water out of your aquifer, shorten your life with pollutants, guzzle energy and accelerate the climate crisis. This is why you must applaud technologies that are likely to destroy your job. Ask not what the tech billionaires can do for you, ask what you can do for the tech billionaires. Who could deny that inflating the bubble is a critical national priority?
Not only are AI companies likely to be overvalued, but datacentres might be overbuilt. It’s possible that one or two companies will win (especially if they locate the holy grail of “recursive self-improvement”), leaving the others sitting on a heap of very expensive and very dead plant. Of course, mere mortals cannot predict which centres are doomed, but the possibility should at least prompt governments to pause while they see how the sector shakes out. Otherwise, we get both landscape blight and decommissioning costs which – if other failed industries are any guide – are likely to fall on the public.
The world might have moved on, but the UK’s growth plans have not. Despite the tsunami of public protest against them, datacentres are still being “turbocharged” by the government, to “make the UK irresistible to AI firms”. At the same time, the war against our irrational desire for pleasant surroundings and good lives that was launched by Keir Starmer continues. I’m told the government is still pushing the regulating for growth bill. It threatens to tear down even more public protections, and to create “regulatory sandboxes” around the UK. These are places where corporations can do things they’re not allowed to do elsewhere. It’s not yet clear whether these sandboxes will include the government’s “AI growth zones”.
Burnham’s government also seems to be pursuing the grim Starmer project of removing the costs cap – established by the Aarhus convention and guaranteed by the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 – for people objecting to damaging schemes. Complain, and you could be landed with a life-changing, home-losing bill. This would shut down a vital aspect of public participation and democracy. Why is it happening? Because the government is not listening to us but to the industry: an industry at odds with almost everyone’s aspirations for a decent life.
An investigation by Spotlight on Corruption discovered that between July 2024 and June 2026, government ministers and officials met representatives from 137 businesses – some of them several times – to discuss their policy on datacentres. In the same period they met just two community groups, and no environmental organisations. We also now have an idea of what such commercial interests have been saying. A submission by the Sovereign AI Leadership taskforce, which represents Nvidia, Microsoft, Cisco and similar companies, warns that the government has one year to force new datacentres through before public objections become overwhelming.
Hence the rush to prioritise them over all other concerns, even – as the residents of Brick Lane in London have discovered – over the prime minister’s proclaimed “defining purpose”, social housing. They are likely both to trash our climate budgets and to make impossible demands on our electricity system. When it comes to mistaking corporate priorities for national ones, Burnham’s administration, it seems, is as easily suckered as Starmer’s.
The government has classified climate activists and Palestine protesters as a threat to national security. But the real threat – to our economy, our jobs, our wellbeing, our democracy, our security, our society and our habitable planet – isn’t hard to locate. It occupies the highest offices of state.
- George Monbiot is a Guardian columnist
