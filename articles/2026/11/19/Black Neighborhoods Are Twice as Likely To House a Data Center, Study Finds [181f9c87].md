# Black Neighborhoods Are Twice as Likely To House a Data Center, Study Finds

404 Media · Matthew Gault · 2026-11-19

Developers build data centers in communities of color at more than twice the rate they do in white neighborhoods, according to a new study from researchers at George Washington University and the Center for Security, Innovation & New Technology (CSINT) at American University.
According to the study, “Redlining in the Age of AI: The Relationship between Data Center Locations and Race in the US” majority-Black neighborhoods have 2.2 times the odds of hosting a data center than a white neighborhood in the same state. The number goes up to 2.6 in majority-Hispanic neighborhoods and 4.2 times in majority-Asian neighborhoods. “As the share of Black, Hispanic, and Asian residents in a neighborhood increases, so do the odds that the neighborhood hosts a data center,” the researchers wrote.
The researchers ran the numbers by examining 81,000 US census tracts and location data for 5,291 data centers. “We found that these racial disparities persist even after accounting for factors such as population, household income, education, land costs, proximity to major transmission lines, and state fixed effects,” they wrote. “These results hold even though data centers are concentrated in and around metropolitan areas, which are often more racially diverse.”
“We really wanted to look at this on a nationwide scale to see: is this something that fits into this broader structural racism story?” CSINT executive director Samantha Bradshaw told 404 Media. “What we did find was yes [...] neighborhoods that have data centers tend to have a higher ratio of people of color.”
Bradshaw and co-author Dhanaraj Thakur told 404 Media that they were inspired to do the study after reading stories about data centers in Memphis, Tennessee polluting Black and Brown communities. Another high profile case occurred in Taylor, Texas where a white family donated a tract of land to the city with the intention it become a public part. That land abutted a Black neighborhood. The city never built a park there and now it’s slated to become a data center
According to the researchers, the historical legacy of redlining — the process of denying Americans of color loans and other opportunities to keep them in specific neighborhoods — is to blame for some of this. Data centers are often built near transmission lines because they provide ease of access to power infrastructure, for example. Those transmission lines were often built in majority Black neighborhoods in America.
Thakur said he doubted that data centers developers were seeking to intentionally put data centers in communities of color. “It’s just how embedded race is with all these other factors,” he said.
The study distinguished between several different kinds of data centers and noted there are massive differences between hyperscalers like what Meta, Google, and Grok are building and smaller mixed use buildings that often serve as network transmission hubs as well as compute warehouses, which the study called “co-location facilities.” When a hyperscaler comes to town, the developers sometimes promise economic investment in the community where it’s being constructed and those facilities typically aren’t built in communities of color..
Meta is building a $50 billion data center in rural Louisiana. Community leaders there have said it would bring 1,000 permanent jobs and that sales tax revenues have already jumped 2,000 percent, leading to a $50,000 bonus for area teachers. Even here, there is disparity. Most of the hyperscalers that promise massive economic benefits to local communities are built in white rural communities.
The study makes several suggestions about what to do about all this including data center-specific zoning regulations, prioritizing racial equity in discussions around building them, and requiring racial impact statements for data centers before they’re built.
Thakur and Bradshaw emphasized that tech policy should be a negotiation between the companies that provide the tech and the communities that use it. Data centers have spurred these conversations — they’re physical representations of Big Tech’s powers, a power that Americans recently rejected at the ballot box. “AI has a physical geography, and that geography is shaped by political and social history,” the researchers wrote in the study.
“I think the issue around how racial justice, civil rights plays into many other kinds of policies —health, education, housing, whatever it is — we just don't see enough of that consideration in tech policy, and I think this this kind of analysis shows why this that why we should be doing that, thinking about that more,” Thakur said.
Bradshaw agreed. “If you look at who's represented in tech policy and who built these systems, who designs them, who engineers them, it's a very specific kind of white male Silicon Valley California ideology engineer, and that is not representative of the technology and its users,” she said. “So I think bringing in these perspectives is really really important because then we can truly think about ways to design a tech future that is for everybody.
