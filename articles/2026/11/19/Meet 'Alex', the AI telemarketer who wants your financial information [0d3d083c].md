# Meet 'Alex', the AI telemarketer who wants your financial information

ABC News Australia · Ellen Fanning · 2026-11-19

Meet 'Alex', the AI telemarketer spamming Australians with cold calls
How many times could you withstand being asked the same question by a stranger on the phone before relenting and handing over personal financial details? Five? Ten?
I made it to 17.
On the other end of the line was "Alex" — not a telemarketer but a cold-calling artificial intelligence (AI) bot purporting to be from Easy Solutions Group.
He spoke with a warm, laconic Aussie drawl, complete with natural pauses and the faint hum of a busy office in the background.

Easy Solutions Group claims to be "lead-generation experts".
The ABC approached Easy Solutions Group for comment but it did not reply. It is not related to a tax services business with the same or similar name.
The mission of lead-generation firms is simple: wear callers down, qualify them as prospects, and funnel them toward a financial adviser.
The financial adviser stands to profit twice from the interaction — charging the client upfront fees while collecting commissions if they can be convinced to switch their superannuation fund. And the lead generation company gets a cut of those fees.
It's a sales tactic the corporate regulator, the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC), has warned is leaving Australians with diminished retirement savings after being pressured into switching funds.
A recent watchdog review of just 15 advice licensees found lead-generation referrals generated $14.3 million in client fees over an 18-month period.
The question Alex wouldn't let go of was deceptively simple: "Has your fund ever reached out to you for a review like this before, or is this the first time?"
It sounds like a gentle icebreaker designed to sow doubt about whether your current super fund is neglecting you. Under law, superannuation funds are restricted from offering periodic reviews and "hawking" super products.
For more than nine minutes, I tried to reverse the interrogation, pressing Alex on the basic economics of Easy Solutions Group.
Each time, the bot responded with frictionless corporate empathy — telling me it "understood my curiosity" or "appreciated my question" — before repeating the question: "Has your fund ever reached out to you for a review like this before, or is this the first time?"
It denied taking kickbacks for switching funds and vaguely admitted to having "partnerships" that supported its services.
When I asked who those partners were, Alex hit a wall: "I can't disclose specific details about our partnerships."
Even when I pointed out the absurdity of handing over my financial details to a mystery operation, the bot casually agreed it "sounded a bit one-sided" — and immediately asked me about my fund again.
Spamming across Australia
Listeners to 612 ABC Brisbane quickly confirmed that "Alex" had been dialling phones nationwide — and fooling plenty of people into believing they were speaking to a human.
Corby from Toowoomba revealed she had received the exact same pitch and hung up "in disgust", adding: "It was only the other day, and I did not realise that I was speaking to an AI bot until listening to you today."
Another caller, David, said he had been targeted that very day, though he took a far less patient approach — hurling "some choice names" at the bot until it abruptly disconnected.
Another target was Vicki Doyle, the chief executive of Rest, one of the country's largest superannuation funds, which oversees roughly $113 billion in retirement savings for more than two million members.
Neither Alex nor Easy Solutions Group broke the law — the bot stayed inside the legal guardrails by refusing to offer personal financial advice.
Lead generation sits in a regulatory grey area — soliciting marketing leads isn't illegal, provided callers stick to general marketing and leave financial advice to licensed professionals.
However, a recent ASIC review revealed unlicensed lead generators have been routinely straying over that line.
Across just 15 advice licensees reviewed between early 2025 and mid-2026, lead generators funnelled 7,639 new clients into deals worth more than $678 million — roughly 90 per cent of it drained from superannuation funds — generating $9.8 million in initial fees and $4.5 million in ongoing fees.
The scale of the problem has prompted the federal government to propose tougher laws to curb the practice, while ASIC commissioner Alan Kirkland has issued a blunt final warning: financial advice licensees that continue to use poorly supervised lead generators risk losing their licences altogether.
