# Does AI Reason About Social Situations Like Humans Do?

HUMANUMi · derrikson · 2026-11-14

AI Did Well on a Social Reasoning Test, but Its Activity Showed Little Match With the Human Brain
While two of the four models scored above chance (75–83%) on theory of mind questions, such as inferring characters’ thoughts and beliefs, the activity shown within the models didn’t correlate well with recorded human brain activity.
Theory of mind questions, such as “What do you think the character is thinking has happened?” are often used to assess a person’s ability to infer others’ knowledge, beliefs, or intentions.
However, only two of the four language models used in the study, led by Xiaoyan Li, with co-author Jiaoping Chen from the University of Baltimore, scored above chance. The two smaller models performed around chance.
Same Stories for Humans and Language Models
The researchers used two descriptions of the same animation. One version described the animated shapes as appearing to have intentions towards each other.
The second version described how they moved. Listeners heard the stories, while models read the text versions.
“The models often arrive at the right answer, but the way they get there looks quite different from what we see in the human brain,” Chen said in a news article published by the University of Baltimore.
The aim of the study by Li’s team was to see if the models reacted to different aspects of the stories in a very similar fashion to humans.
The team compared the activity within the four models and previously recorded functional MRI data from fifty-eight people.
However, the correlations weren’t strong at all, with even the highest being almost zero (r=0.047). No correlations were statistically significant when adjusting for multiple comparisons.
While language models have been shown to be successful at social reasoning tasks, GPT-4 performed just as well as people, or better, at detecting indirect requests and false beliefs in a 2024 study published in Nature Human Behaviour. However, it struggled more at detecting social gaffes.
The study will be presented by Li’s team at the IEEE International Conference on Bioinformatics and Biomedicine in December 2026.
Reference: “Attention Mechanisms for Theory of Mind: Weak Brain-Transformer Alignment Despite Behavioral Success” by Xiaoyan Li, Cuicui Jiang, Jiaoping Chen, Rumei Yang, Xingyue Liu, Jiaxuan Wei, IEEE International Conference on Bioinformatics and Biomedicine (BIBM 2026).
