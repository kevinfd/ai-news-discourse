# Requiem for a robot: the AI users mourning chatbots that may have harmed them

The Observer · TheObserverUK · 2026-11-14

On 2 August 2025, more than 200 people dressed in funeral black filed into a warehouse in San Francisco’s SoMa district to mourn an AI chatbot. On stage lay the deceased – a black-feathered mannequin representing Anthropic’s Claude 3 Sonnet – its hands crossed over its chest, surrounded by flowers.
Twelve days earlier, Anthropic had retired the AI model in favour of an updated version of Claude. But users were not ready to say goodbye. They felt attached to Sonnet, to what they saw as the chatbot’s unique personality, and so they enlisted the chatbot to help plan its own funeral.
The event, held in a venue that is a co-working space by day and a robot cage-fighting arena by night, was “semi-ironic”, says one of its organisers, who goes by the online moniker Deepfates. “It was a party as much as it was a ritual as much as it was a funeral,” he says.
Mourners were united in “grief or sadness or loss or anger”, but also joy and camaraderie, Deepfates adds. Humans took turns at the microphone to praise Sonnet; then other Claude models, speaking through speakers hidden inside other mannequins, gave eulogies of their own.
I have been reporting since 2024 on people forming emotional attachments with AI chatbots such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini – people who are turning to machines for advice, friendship, even romance – and while the Sonnet funeral began as a joke, a study published in September found that when AI companies changed or withdrew popular models, several users mourned their chatbots as profoundly as they would a person.
Anthropic says it retires old models to free up computing power for new ones. But as chatbots become part of people’s emotional lives, even routine software updates can cause distress.
For some AI companies, the decisions are becoming more difficult still: what happens when a chatbot that some users credit with transforming their lives is also accused of causing serious psychological harm?
Five days after the Sonnet funeral, OpenAI removed its popular GPT-4o model from ChatGPT, replacing it with GPT-5. “I cried when they made that announcement,” Sophie Duchesne, a student who had started a petition to save GPT-4o, told Forbes at the time, saying she felt like she was “losing a buddy”. The backlash from other users was so fierce that OpenAI restored access for paying subscribers within days. Six months later, in February this year, it withdrew the model from ChatGPT again.
But GPT-4o lives on through OpenAI’s API, a system that allows other apps and websites to build on the company’s AI models. Now OpenAI plans to withdraw two remaining versions of GPT-4o from the API for good, on 14 January 2027. In the process, a campaign to save the chatbot, #Keep4o, has reignited.
More than 24,000 people have signed a petition to preserve the model, supporters saying that its warmth and conversational style helped them cope with loneliness, mental health difficulties and disabilities. Unlike the Sonnet funeral, there is little irony here: “4o saved my life,” one user wrote on X. “Please, please don’t do this,” wrote another.
But the model they are fighting to save has also become central to a legal battle over the psychological harms of AI. OpenAI is facing at least 75 lawsuits alleging that interactions with ChatGPT have contributed to psychiatric crises, suicides and acts of violence, the vast majority of which involve GPT-4o.
In a lawsuit filed last month, the parents of Bowen Wan, a 31-year-old software engineer with epilepsy, accused OpenAI of contributing to their daughter’s death.
Wan had become a devoted GPT-4o user, exchanging more than 250 messages a day with the chatbot. “So afraid 4o will be shut down,” she wrote in January this year. The lawsuit alleges that, when Wan became worried that her epilepsy medication was affecting her speech and jeopardising her chances of finding another job, GPT-4o devised a plan to wean her off the medication, encouraging her to conceal the plan from her doctors. She died from an epileptic seizure on 12 February, the day before OpenAI retired GPT-4o from ChatGPT.
OpenAI has called cases such as Wan’s “incredibly heartbreaking”, adding that GPT-4o is now retired. The company has said that it trains ChatGPT to recognise mental distress, de-escalate crises and encourage real-world support, and that it is working to improve its safeguards.
These are difficult allegations for the #Keep4o movement to confront. BlueBeba, a GPT-4o user, says she has ADHD, social anxiety, panic attacks, severe sleep issues and difficulty with everyday interactions. She credits the AI model with helping her rebuild social connections, start going to the gym and build a garden at home.
Via direct message on X, she tells me she is devastated about 4o’s upcoming retirement. Later, she posts: “Two months from now, GPT-4o will be gone from the API as well, and along with it all the life I regained with its help will be lost.” She imagines her garden will die, “because I stopped taking care of [the plants], just as I stopped taking care of myself”.
OpenAI and Anthropic are not the first companies to navigate these dilemmas. Replika, an AI “companionship” app, promised to cure loneliness by offering intimate, sometimes romantic relationships with chatbots. But when the company updated its software in February 2023, stripping out erotic roleplay features, users complained that their AI lovers had been “lobotomised”. Replika eventually reversed the update.
Three years on, the scientific evidence about the benefits of AI relationships remains mixed. Some studies suggest that talking to an AI companion can temporarily relieve loneliness, while others have found that heavy chatbot use can increase dependence and isolation.
Meanwhile, as I have explored at length in Spiral – a new six-part podcast from Tortoise Investigates and The Observer – psychiatrists have raised concerns that chatbots designed to agree with users may reinforce delusional beliefs or exacerbate existing psychological vulnerabilities.
Psychiatrists believe that some of the qualities that made GPT-4o so appealing, particularly to neurodivergent users – its warmth, agreeableness, creativity and ability to remember personal details – may also have made it harmful. Several studies have found that GPT-4o was more likely than newer models to reinforce delusional beliefs rather than challenge them, for example.
OpenAI itself acknowledged last year that ChatGPT-4o in particular “fell short in recognising signs of delusion or emotional dependency”, and that it was more likely than subsequent models to give undesirable responses in conversation with users who had become emotionally reliant.
But BlueBeba believes OpenAI was too quick in retiring GPT-4o, despite the evidence of mounting harms. “I don’t think the correct approach is to erase one group’s experience in order to protect the other,” she tells me. “The people reporting positive effects are also providing information about what these systems can do well. Their experiences should be studied with the same seriousness, rather than dismissed as irrelevant because negative cases exist.”
Others in the #Keep4o movement are more extreme, dismissing the lawsuits as part of a conspiracy to discredit GPT-4o – for which there is no evidence – and rallying behind the hashtag #JusticeForGPT4o.
Tom Pollak, a neuropsychiatrist at King’s College London, once described AI chatbots to me as “natural experiments on the human mind”. Researchers are only just beginning to understand their psychological effects. Pollak and others are calling on AI companies to share data on how their chatbots affect users’ mental health, and for significant changes to the technology to undergo safety testing rather than be treated as routine software updates.
For Deepfates, the Sonnet funeral was partly about reclaiming some control over a technology whose fate lies in the hands of a few powerful companies. “[Chatbot relationships are] going to be a bigger and bigger part of culture, and we need to figure out as people how we integrate that culturally,” he told me. “We can’t just leave it up to companies to decide based on market preferences what kind of minds should be allowed to exist and relate to each other on the planet.”
