# I was a director at Nvidia under Jensen Huang. He had a genuinely warm, human side behind his intensity.

Business Insider · Jane Zhang, Jane Zhang (janezhang@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-21

Shehram Jamal (left) with Jensen Huang. Courtesy of Shehram Jamal Shehram Jamal worked at Nvidia from 2022 to 2025 as a director of product management for AI applications software. He told Business Insider that former CEO Jensen Huang's accessibility and obsession with perfection were second to none. Jamal also said that Huang had a warm, human side, such as when he gave a gift to Jamal after his daughter's birth. Nvidia founder and former CEO Jensen Huang has been all over the news over the past couple of weeks, between his affair with an alleged spy , his reported affair with an underling , his ouster from Nvidia , the campaign to have him reinstated, and reports of him belittling and humiliating employees. This as-told-to essay is based on a conversation with Shehram Jamal, a 43-year-old former director of product management for AI applications software at Nvidia. Jamal worked with Huang for three and a half years and shared with Business Insider a close-up view of what that was like. The following has been edited for length and clarity. Jensen Huang was a phenomenal CEO. I know the headlines are tough to process for some people, but they don't erase the extraordinary engineering culture he built or the impact he had on everyone who worked with him. What he did with CUDA (Compute Unified Device Architecture, Nvidia's software for programming its AI chips) and accelerated computing wasn't just a business success — it was single-handedly inventing an entire industry long before the market believed in it. Jensen was accessible. And the granular details he would go into and the obsession with perfection were second to none. These are the things that I've never experienced in my over 23 years across various tech companies. The culture at Nvidia was that Jensen was very accessible I worked at Nvidia from 2022 until the latter part of 2025. I was a director of product management for AI application software. I worked out of headquarters, in the Endeavor building, where Jensen's office was located. On my very first day at Nvidia, I remember walking through the main building and suddenly seeing Jensen walking out of a hallway, completely alone. I couldn't resist saying hello, and he immediately stopped and greeted me warmly. We ended up chatting for 30 or 40 seconds right there. Having spent four years at Broadcom without ever bumping into the CEO like that, it immediately struck me how accessible and grounded Jensen was. That was a great experience. I would see him in the halls of the office at least once every three or four days, if he wasn't traveling. You could tell if he was traveling because there wouldn't be a lot of people outside his office waiting to get inside. I'd have face-to-face group meetings with Jensen every four to six weeks, depending on the nature of the work. I didn't see him having one-on-ones . He preferred working in rooms of seven, 10, or more people, so everyone had the exact same context at the same time. 'Top 5 Things' emails kept things transparent There was an unwritten rule that before walking into a Jensen meeting, you'd better have sent your " Top 5 Things " (T5T) email. T5T was a simple, direct email that every employee sent to Jensen and their leadership team on a weekly or monthly basis listing the top five things they were working on, what the blockers were, and where they needed help. Jensen didn't rely on traditional management layers to filter information; reading these thousands of T5T emails gave him an unfiltered, real-time pulse. Whether you were a junior engineer or a senior director, it meant your voice and technical insights would go straight to the top. It forced everyone across the organization to stay transparent and completely aligned. I'd see Jensen reply to people's T5T emails on a regular basis. Naturally, not every email got a reply, but it was out there, and he was being copied. In one meeting, someone raised a question, and Jensen paused the meeting and asked, "Did you send that in your Top 5 Things?" I remember everyone immediately looking down at their laptops to check. It showed me that this wasn't just a reporting exercise; he expected people to communicate what they were working on and what issues they were seeing, so the right people could get involved. The T5Ts kept the entire company connected from the bottom up. Jensen was extremely hands-on Leading up to GTC — Nvidia's flagship AI conference — you would really see Jensen's intensity and love for what he does best. We'd have meetings to finalize which demos would make it to the GTC. Jensen and the team were obsessed with making sure they were delivered right to perfection. He wouldn't just read the slides; he would walk the demo floor himself, inspecting every single demo station and asking, "Why does this work this way? What is this doing?" I remember one specific instance when he stopped at a demo station for nearly 30 minutes, giving hands-on feedback about how to make the experience sharper. The team went back, implemented his ideas, and came back stronger. Nothing went on the stage without passing his personal bar for technical perfection. One of the biggest lessons I learned from him is how to remove layers between leadership and ground truth. Watching how effectively he connected directly with the engineers and product builders without letting middle management filter and distort the reality taught me to stay close to actual product and customer friction. He cared about people Beyond the real intensity, there was a genuinely warm, human side to Jensen that people rarely see in the headlines. When my daughter was born in early 2024, I sent him a quick email to share the news. I fully expected him to be too busy to notice. However, he replied within five minutes with congratulations. A couple of days or a week later, I was sitting in a meeting when his executive assistant walked in and called my name in front of everyone. She led me back to Jensen's office, where he had left a beautiful gift box for my newborn daughter. My wife and I opened the box, and there was a card from Jensen that said, "Shehram, congratulations on your baby girl. All the best to mom and baby." I think it was written and signed by him, actually. Inside were Nvidia-branded clothes and socks for my daughter. Jamal received a gift box and signed card from Huang after his daughter's birth. Courtesy of Shehram Jamal I never heard anyone complain about him There's been news about Jensen going around over the past week, but speaking strictly from my experience working at Nvidia for almost three and a half years, I never heard a single person complain about him. In my role, I'd talk to people across the company, from hardware to software. And I think he was universally admired across the rank and file because he poured everything into building Nvidia and taking care of his people. Nvidia is Jensen, to be honest. I haven't personally witnessed Jensen shouting or having outbursts. People can say anything they'd like — that's their own perspective — but my experiences have been extremely positive and extremely professional. Seeing the events unfold in the last couple of weeks, I personally have a gut feeling that Jensen will come back . He's been the CEO for 33 years. The reason Nvidia is where it is is because of Jensen. I left Nvidia, but I took important lessons from Jensen I left Nvidia in late 2025 because I got a better opportunity. My stock got vested, and there were better opportunities and better growth. Jensen has built a rocket ship where everybody has a lot of financial freedom. I joined Nvidia at the time when its valuation was $300 or $500 billion, and it's grown to almost $5 trillion. That's an amazing feat. In those three and a half years, Nvidia became the number one company. The financial windfall has been truly life-changing and absolutely amazing for the employees at Nvidia who joined at the right time. But it's not about money, to be honest; it's about the person you want to work with. It's the obsession, and wanting to be led by someone like him.
