# I’m a priest. Silicon Valley’s search for the ‘soul’ of AI is deeply troubling | Rev Kevin Deal

The Guardian · Rev Kevin Deal · 2026-11-21

Last month, religious leaders from across San Francisco gathered behind closed doors to discuss what it means to be human in the age of artificial intelligence.
Anthropic, the AI company operating only a couple of miles from my parish, has been courting priests, rabbis and theologians. Its billionaire cofounder, Christopher Olah, has even pressed Vatican advisers to take seriously the possibility that his company’s chatbot might possess consciousness.
As a priest, I find this pursuit deeply troubling. I welcome serious conversations about AI’s moral implications, especially theological ones. But the extraordinary effort to establish whether a machine might have consciousness, or possess a soul, reveals a profound misunderstanding of what makes us human.
Our souls and minds and bodies are so much more than the tasks we perform.
We as humans make mistakes, sometimes catastrophic ones, and then we may or may not try to correct course. We may double down in stubbornness, insisting on our rightness. We express ourselves, connect with others, reproduce, indulge, purge; we strive toward a goal, transform in an encounter, make vows, renege them, encounter the void, try to fill it, fail, drown out the dread, and go about the humdrum tasks of everyday life as honestly as we can.
From a young age, we are nurtured and protected by our caregivers. We experience beauty and sit with mysteries. We do not exist merely for utility.
Artificial intelligence could never replicate the transcendent mundanity of who we are. Even if every synapse in our brains were externally replicated, even if the perfect AI configuration were designed to duplicate our central nervous system, it would still lack divine spark.
Artificial intelligence is formed as commerce, pure utility. Its existence requires enormous concentrations of capital, infrastructure, and the accumulated intelligence of humanity. It’s an ongoing economic choice, driven by pursuit of profit, hope for a better world, and sociopolitical fear. If a model doesn’t make a good return, it’s cast aside.
Whatever the sincere hopes of its creators, AI is being developed within an economic system that increasingly measures the worth of everything solely by its productivity. That is what makes Silicon Valley’s fascination with the spirituality of its machines so strange.
Its founders are turning to religious leaders to ask whether their creations might possess consciousness, how they might be taught goodness, even whether they should learn to confess their wrongdoings.
Yet the very traditions they are consulting have spent millennia teaching us that goodness cannot be reduced to a set of behaviors, any more than the soul of humanity, as magnificent and ineffable as it is, can be replicated by a machine.
And while Silicon Valley seeks to establish the humanity of its products, it seems increasingly comfortable treating actual human beings as little more than instruments of production.
Consider confession, a practice Anthropic researchers have reportedly considered teaching their models. In Christianity, confession demands more than acknowledging a mistake or expressing regret. It requires us to confront the harm we’ve caused, seek forgiveness, and strive to change how we live.
If Silicon Valley’s founders want to learn from this tradition, they might begin by asking what they themselves have to confess about the technologies they’re unleashing upon the world.
So how do we counter this impoverished vision of humanity?
Well, I have a radical suggestion for all of us, but especially the tech billionaires searching for answers in Christianity.
Go to church.
Choose one rooted in the ancient and mysterious practices of the faith. Lean into the practice of the faith and the sacraments, attend mass, confess, and pray daily. Read the scriptures, find spiritual mentors, struggle with belief, make new friends, and forgive old ones.
If you stop trying to be effective and instead strive to be good – that is, to be truly human, reflecting God’s light – you will be surprised by the joy that enters into the depths of your soul.
In the sacraments, prayers, beauty and mystery of the liturgy, and the shared life of faith, we encounter a vision of humanity that resists the relentless demand to be useful. We receive the body of Christ and strive to become more like him. We turn toward a world where justice and mercy kiss, where renewal and forgiveness are continually possible.
When you leave church, carry those teachings back to your offices and boardrooms. Ask how the decisions you make affect the poor, the workers whose livelihoods are being transformed, and the neighbors who will never share in your wealth.
The mystery of the Christian faith is inseparable from its demands upon how we live. You cannot claim to embrace its wisdom while setting aside the obligations it places upon you.
Human worth is not rooted in what we produce, and our minds were not created to be quarried for profit. We were created, in the words of Jesus and all the prophets, to love God and our neighbors.
The great moral question facing Silicon Valley is not whether its machines can become more like us. It is whether the people building them are willing to treat the rest of humanity as something more than machines.
- The Rev Kevin Deal is the priest at St John the Evangelist Episcopal church in San Francisco
