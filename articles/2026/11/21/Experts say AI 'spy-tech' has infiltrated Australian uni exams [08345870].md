# Experts say AI 'spy-tech' has infiltrated Australian uni exams

ABC News Australia · Hannah Moon · 2026-11-21

Hidden cameras, tiny earpieces and AI: Inside the new era of exam cheating
A hidden camera, a tiny wireless earpiece and an artificial intelligence assistant.
It sounds like something you would see in a James Bond film, but this technology is causing concern in the education industry.
This week, the top scorer in South Korea's national tax accountant exam admitted to using a similar system to cheat his way to a near-perfect score.
So how does high-tech exam cheating work, and how prepared are Australia's education systems?
Monash University's Professor Phillip Dawson has been researching assessment cheating for more than a decade, and says methods of cheating have evolved.
"The history of the exam goes back to Chinese civil service entry exams, and we have multiple hundred-year-old artefacts where people have written exam answers on their clothes, and these were passed down from generation to generation," he said.
"It's changed and evolved so rapidly that trying to make an AI-proof assessment task, I don't think it works in the short term, and it definitely doesn't work in the long term."
Technology is becoming smaller, smarter and more accessible, and experts warn this new era of academic misconduct has already infiltrated Australia.
And this tech is not hard to get your hands on.
Can you buy hidden cameras in Australia?
A brief search on the internet and a few hundred dollars will secure you a high-tech cheating set-up.
The ABC was able to find tiny wireless earpieces and cameras disguised as clothing buttons, pens and water bottles and car keys available to purchase online.
Many products advertise live feeds that don't require internet access.
Instead, they claim cameras can create their own wi-fi hotspot that a phone within about 10 to 20 metres can join directly. This would allow live video to feed out with no internet connection or SIM card.
Some smart glasses appear undistinguishable from reading glasses, and advertise real-time translation and AI assistance even in camera-less models.
The gear is commonly advertised as spy-tech, and some of them appear to be based in Australia, advertising shipping from local warehouses.
Are Australian students using them?
Kane Murdoch is the head of complaints, appeals and misconduct at Macquarie University and a self-proclaimed "assessment security enthusiast".
He says high-tech cheating tools have already made their way into Australian exam halls.
"Button cameras, very small earpieces, a phone in a bag across the room connected to 'pervert' [smart] glasses … If you've seen people propose it, people like me across the sector have found it,"
he said.
"When humans find a shortcut, they'll often use it, and students are doing just that."
Mr Murdoch says new technology is transforming education.
"I've seen more change as a result of AI in the last few years than I have in the kind of 20 [years] previous."
Monash University's Professor Dawson says it is hard to know how common tech-based cheating is in Australia.
"Institutions need to have a forward-looking approach … getting in there and trying to break stuff rather than taking a reactive approach," he said.
"I always tell people that your list of the things you think an AI can't do, at best, is on some developer's whiteboard of things they want their AI to be able to do,"
he said.
How do students cheat with hidden tech?
Ritesh Chugh, an information and communication technologies expert at Central Queensland University, said students don't necessarily need sophisticated technical knowledge to cheat with tech.
"A miniature camera can capture an exam question," he said.
"The image can be processed by an AI system that is capable of interpreting the question and generating a response, and that response can then be communicated back through a connected device."
But he stressed that "just because technology makes something possible doesn't mean that every student will use it dishonestly".
"AI is certainly changing the opportunities for cheating, but I don't think we should confuse opportunity with behaviour. Ultimately, this is about protecting fairness, trust and the value of education," he said.
Why has the South Korean case gone worldwide?
The man at the centre of the South Korean scandal, identified only by the surname Han, scored near-perfect marks on hundreds of questions over two national exams taken in April and July.
After months of speculation, Mr Han confessed to cheating last Sunday.
He said he took photos of the exam questions with a micro camera, sent them to an AI tool, and checked the answers on a smartphone.
The timing of his confession raised the stakes for this week's annual national college entrance exams.
The exams, known as "Suneung," or the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT), are a high-stakes affair in a country notorious for intense academic competition.
Prospects for jobs, social standing and even marriage can depend on which university a person attends.
South Korea goes to great lengths each year to create an ideal testing environment, delaying the start of the workday and stock market trading to ease traffic for test-takers, and pausing flights during the English-listening section of the exam.
After Mr Han's confession, authorities rushed to implement new security measures for the exam.
Emergency alerts were sent to mobile devices across the country to flush out hidden phones, stickers were placed over clothing buttons, and students' glasses were checked.
What can be done about high-tech cheating in Australia?
One proposed solution is a layered approach to exam security, sometimes called the "Swiss cheese model".
The idea is that no single safeguard is perfect, but several layers of protection can make it significantly harder to cheat.
"If we look at the pandemic, we didn't think vaccines were perfect, we didn't think masks were perfect, and we didn't think social distancing was perfect, but we layered these different interventions on top of each other … and reduced the risk that we were exposed to," Professor Dawson said.
From a misconduct perspective, Mr Murdoch said additional security measures could be reasonable and non-invasive, like airport security.
"Whether it's a metal detector, whether it's being asked to throw out liquids … we accept that willingly when we go to the airport and want to get on a plane,"
he said.
Mr Murdoch said he was concerned about the level of intention and planning that goes into high-tech cheating attempts.
He said penalties for such cases were often more severe.
"Degrees are worth defending," he said.
"People out there want to know with total certainty that their doctor knows what the university says their doctor does."
