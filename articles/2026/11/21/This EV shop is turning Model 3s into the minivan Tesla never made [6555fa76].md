# This EV shop is turning Model 3s into the minivan Tesla never made

Business Insider · Ben Shimkus, Ben Shimkus (bshimkus@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-21

This "dorky" vehicle is a converted Tesla. Its creator is hoping Elon Musk doesn't dash his EV van dreams. Hoffman EV Hoffman EV's Badger is a fresh concept: Build a van with the guts of old Tesla Model 3s. It's yet another bet in an auto industry that hasn't convinced Americans to go electric. The company is reliant on Elon Musk — and it needs to fix its highway range problem. Tesla has never sold a minivan. Geoffrey Hoffman wants to capitalize on that gap — but without starting a car company from scratch. Hoffman EV, the six-year-old Wisconsin-based company he founded, recently showcased the Badger at the SEMA trade show in Vegas. It's a "dorky-looking" (as Hoffman describes it) EV that's equal parts off-road toy, cargo van, and Tesla recycling project. "I wanted to create something that had fun elements to it," he said. "I built something that was what I wanted to use for myself." The Badger at SEMA. Hoffman EV American EV adoption hasn't progressed as Detroit once imagined, leaving car companies with billions of dollars in write-downs and a graveyard of discontinued EVs . Hoffman's van arrives as American companies are throwing more pasta at the proverbial EV wall, and hoping to find what sticks with consumers. The bets are all very different from the hulking, expensive electric trucks that struggled to capture American buyers. A few examples: Ford is rebooting its EV efforts with a sub-$30,000 electric pickup called Fathom ; Slate is about to roll out its $24,950 truck; and even Fiat launched its golf-cart-like Topolino this summer. The Badger is another kind of wager. Instead of building its own platform, Hoffman EV reuses the battery, motors, computers, cameras, interior screens, wiring, and seats from older Tesla Model 3s. The company wraps the vehicle in a new aluminum body. Right now, it gets access to the Tesla Supercharger network, Tesla's software updates, and Tesla's semi-autonomous driving tech — so long as Tesla CEO Elon Musk doesn't pump the brakes. Tesla has no involvement in the project. For Hoffman, that risk is worth it. The Badger lets him drive the car with some of the American auto industry's best technology, and helps him avoid the hulking, expensive electric trucks that have struggled to capture American buyers. "I don't know why you need to have a huge, huge pickup truck where you can't even see over the hood," he said. "There was definitely no ego or macho or anything like that in this design." There are still some major caveats The Badger has a Tesla screen and a fully re-cast body. Hoffman EV Hoffman drove the Badger to Las Vegas and back for SEMA, but said its current highway range is only about 150 miles — a figure he called "probably unacceptable." It's set a target for 200 miles of highway range on its next prototype and is now working on aerodynamics to reduce wind noise and improve range. The Badger depends heavily on Tesla, but there is no communication between the companies. Hoffman said Tesla could restrict that access or disable other features in the future. While the Badger technically can use Tesla's FSD (Supervised) semi-autonomous features, Hoffman doesn't suggest it. The Badger is shorter than the Model 3, which might confuse the technology, he warned. Hoffman EV is targeting 2027 for reservations and production, and predicts it can build about 100 units annually. Its current target pricing: about $10,000 for the chassis, $40,000 for a body kit, and $10,000 for the build itself. Add a roughly $20,000 donor Model 3, and the Badger could cost about $70,000 before taxes. The company is also debating whether to start by selling fully assembled cars or kits for customers to piece together. Either way, Hoffman said he knows the company isn't expected to become a mass-volume seller. "I think I have a really good concept here, or at least a lot of smart people have told me so," he said. "It isn't going to fill any gaps in the market at such low quantity or solve our climate crisis, but it should make some people happy." Read the original article on Business Insider
