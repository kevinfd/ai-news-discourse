# Jensen Huang has 5 ways to push for an Nvidia comeback

Business Insider · Thibault Spirlet, Thibault Spirlet (tspirlet@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-21

Jensen Huang still has five ways to build shareholder pressure for an Nvidia CEO comeback. Mark Schiefelbein/Associated Press Jensen Huang said he'd be "honored" to return as CEO, but Nvidia's board said it won't reinstate him. Huang's stake and founder brand give him ways to test Nvidia's board and build a comeback. From a proxy fight to a special meeting, Huang has options, but they're a long shot. Jensen Huang's path back to the Nvidia CEO job may be narrowing, but he still has ways to fight for a comeback. Huang has said he would be "honored" to return if the board decided it was in the company's best interest. Only Nvidia's board can appoint its CEO, meaning the most straightforward route would be for Huang to persuade the board to change its mind. Publicly, the board has thrown cold water on the idea . However, Semafor reported on Wednesday that three Nvidia directors have pushed for Huang's return. A  petition calling for Huang's reinstatement  has over 200,000 signatures, while Nvidia has shed more than $1 trillion in market value since Huang's resignation, putting further pressure on the board. Still, Huang's roughly 3.5% stake and founding role give him additional ways to build support for a comeback. Here are five ways Huang could challenge Nvidia's board and seek to reclaim the CEO job. 1. Take his case directly to shareholders Former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang Sean Gallup/Getty Images Huang does not need to wait for a formal vote to start building pressure on Nvidia’s board . Michael R. Levin, founder of shareholder-activism advisory firm The Activist Investor, said Huang could begin rallying investors now by making the case that he deserves to return and showing the board that major shareholders agree. If enough investors signal that they want him back, Levin told Business Insider, the board could act before the next annual meeting rather than risk a damaging public fight. But he called that a "big if." "The board may take seriously that sentiment and act sooner, at least if they don't want a contested election in 2027," he said. 2. Turn the annual vote into a referendum on the board Colette Kress, chief financial officer of Nvidia Nvidia Huang could use Nvidia's next annual meeting to show that shareholders have lost confidence in the board that accepted his resignation. Huang could use his shares and public profile to urge investors to vote against Nvidia’s board nominees. His roughly 3.6% stake would not decide a vote on its own, but it could matter in a close contest or help signal broader investor discontent. In an uncontested election, Nvidia's proxy statement says a director who receives more "against" than "for" votes must offer to resign. The board has the final say, but a major public vote against its directors would put it under pressure. For Huang, the drawback is timing: The next annual meeting is likely to be in 2027. Levin said persuading shareholders to support Huang's return would take "a huge effort and much luck," making the odds of a comeback "pretty remote." 3. Put up two directors of his own Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang Joel Saget / AFP via Getty Images Huang could use Nvidia’s proxy-access rules to place up to two independent director nominees on the company’s own annual-meeting ballot. The rules require a shareholder to have held at least 3% of Nvidia’s voting power for three years. Nvidia’s SEC filings show Huang has remained above that threshold for more than three years, although the board would ultimately decide whether he met every technical condition. His nominees would still need shareholder backing. And Levin said that, even if elected, two new directors would be unlikely to make much difference on their own, as they would not alter the balance of power on Nvidia's board. Nvidia has not yet set an annual meeting date for 2027, but its proxy materials indicate Huang would probably need to submit a proxy-access nomination between February 24 and March 26. 4. Escalate to a full proxy fight Jensen Huang, chief executive of Nvidia. Bloomberg/Getty Images Huang could go further by recruiting major Nvidia investors to elect a broader slate of director candidates who could be more receptive to his return at the 2027 annual meeting. Unlike a proxy access bid, a full proxy fight would require Huang or allied shareholders to run and pay for their own campaign. Under the SEC’s universal-proxy rules , a dissident shareholder must solicit proxies from holders of at least 67% of a company’s voting power. That is a requirement to contact those investors, not proof that 67% would back his candidates. Nvidia’s two largest disclosed institutional shareholders , BlackRock and Vanguard, held 7.43% and 7.31%, respectively, as of March 23. But it is not publicly known whether either — or any other major investor — would support Huang's return. 5. Force the issue at a special meeting Jensen Huang Jose Sarmento Matos/Bloomberg via Getty Images The fastest formal route would be to call a special meeting, Andrew Freedman, chair of Olshan's shareholder-activism practice, told Business Insider. Nvidia's bylaws allow shareholders or a group that has held 15% of its voting power for at least a year to demand one. Huang would need allies with another 11.42% beyond his 3.58% stake. Freedman said the "cleanest" approach would be a two-step proxy campaign: first win enough support to call the meeting, then seek a vote to remove directors without cause. That would require support from at least two-thirds of all outstanding Nvidia voting shares, under its Certificate of Incorporation . "If Huang or any investors want to strike now while the iron is hot," Freedman said, "they would do so through a special meeting." Still, Ann Lipton, a professor of law and the Laurence W. DeMuth Chair of Business Law at the University of Colorado Law School, pointed to the scrutiny Nvidia faces, including Huang testifying before Congress on December 1. "That might make it difficult for him to mount a challenge to the board at the same time," she said. Read the original article on Business Insider
