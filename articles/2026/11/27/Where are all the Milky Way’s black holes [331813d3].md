# Where are all the Milky Way’s black holes?

Scientific American · Phil Plait · 2026-11-27

Astronomers can’t seem to find hundreds of millions of black holes in our galaxy. Given these objects’ reputation, you may find that a little troubling.
But don’t panic! These black holes aren’t dangerous to us, though the situation is somewhat irritating to astronomers.
Stellar-mass black holes—ones that range from about three to 100 times the mass of the Sun—litter the Milky Way. They form when a massive star runs out of fuel; the core collapses and releases truly colossal amounts of energy. This causes the outer layers to explode away as a fiercely energetic supernova. And if the core is massive enough, it will form a black hole when it collapses.

We can estimate how many stellar-mass black holes there should be in the galaxy. One method is to calculate how many stars our galaxy has produced in its lifetime that have been more than eight solar masses, the critical limit needed to form a black hole. Research published in Astronomy and Astrophysics in 2020 estimated there are about 130 million such black holes in the Milky Way. A different study, posted as a preprint in 2026, got about 170 million, which is pretty similar.
Another way is to look at the gravitational waves emitted as black holes merge with each other and use that to extrapolate their overall population in our galaxy. Research published in the January 2018 issue of Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society used this method and also found there should be some hundreds of millions in our galaxy. Close enough: we can safely conclude there are at least 100 million of these beasts in the Milky Way and possibly many times that number.
How many have we actually found? Well, this is the part that’s a bit irritating: fewer than 100. That’s not 100 million, just “one hundred.” And most of them aren’t confirmed yet.
This isn’t embarrassing—far from it; black holes make themselves pretty hard to find. For one thing the problem is right in their name: they’re black. They don’t emit light. In fact, they’re quite famous for exactly the opposite: absorbing everything, including light, that falls on them.
Single black holes that are just minding their own business in space are practically invisible. If they aren’t gobbling down matter—that is, if they’re quiescent—they are extremely difficult to detect. It’s possible, though, for a scant few to reveal themselves in various ways. For example, if one happens to pass directly between a star and Earth, our telescopes will detect the star brightening as the gravity of the black hole acts like a lens, distorting space and magnifying the light from the background star. There have been many surveys looking for such lensing events, but to date, only a single isolated Milky Way black hole has been found this way.
Sometimes, though, they make it a bit easier on astronomers. If a black hole is in a binary system, orbiting a normal star, its intense gravity can siphon material off the other star and draw it in. That matter piles up in a disk just above the event horizon. Friction and other forces inside the disk can heat it to extraordinarily high temperatures, millions of degrees, so the disk becomes incredibly luminous. Such a black hole can pour out high-energy X- and gamma rays which can be seen by our space telescopes, making it almost a cinch to locate in the sky. Most black holes we’ve detected in the Milky Way are in these types of systems, but they are vastly outnumbered by quiescent ones, which still makes a census of galactic black holes elusive.
Recently, though, a new method has been available to look for these hidden lurkers. Gaia was an ESA mission that measured the positions, velocities, brightness and colors of well over a billion stars in our galaxy. The measurements were so precise that it’s possible to actually see the change in position a star undergoes as it and a black hole companion orbit each other. It takes exacting measurements and analysis to tease these black holes out of the data, but so far three such systems have been found, named (somewhat prosaically) Gaia BH1, 2 and 3. In fact, Gaia BH1 is the closest black hole known to the Sun, about 1,560 light-years away. The three Gaia black holes have masses of 9.6, 8.9 and 33 times that of the Sun, respectively.
These represent the tip of the iceberg; there are probably a few tens of millions of black holes orbiting another object (such as a star or another black hole) in the galaxy. Still, it’s a promising method. Even better, a new data release from Gaia (its final one, alas) will have observations spanning a longer time baseline, which will make the telltale stellar motions easier to spot.
That isn’t to say that we understand the ones we have seen. Two of the Gaia black holes—BH1 and BH2—orbit normal stars at moderate distances, and this presents a problem. The stars that formed these two black holes were quite massive, and as they died, they would have expanded into red supergiants. In both cases, the companion stars should have been engulfed by the more massive star, which would either result in a complete merger of the two or, once the massive star exploded, the second star being on a very tight orbit around the newly formed black hole.
Yet in both systems, the stars appear intact, orbiting farther away than models predict. A paper published in July 10, 2026 in the Astrophysical Journal may have found a solution to this conundrum. Typically the supergiant star loses mass rapidly, and this blows it out into space. In a binary system, however, this process is aided by centrifugal force, which helps fling material away, and the physical dynamics of the system dictate that it occurs closer to the companion star. Thie process removes orbital energy, causing the orbits to shrink and, potentially, the two stars to merge.
In the new research, the astronomers found that if the mass is instead lost close to the more massive star, the material doesn’t take as much energy away, leaving the two stars separate. It’s not clear how this might happen, though they posited a few ideas. Perhaps violent internal eruptions in the massive star can blast matter away directly from the surface into space. It’s also possible that the huge mass difference between the stars prevents the more gently blown material from transferring to the smaller companion star, so, again, it essentially leaves directly from the larger star.
Astronomers found a candidate fourth quiescent black hole system in 2024 with a very low mass and unusually circular orbit around its companion star. This system, too, is difficult to explain, and may involve a third star that was engulfed when the black hole progenitor turned into a red supergiant, merging with the other star to form the binary we see today.
Clearly, some black holes are weird, even for black holes. It’s interesting that, of the four quiescent binary black holes we’ve seen, three of them thumb their noses at our initial hypotheses of how they form. This is a pretty good indication that there is still a lot to figure out about these systems. With, oh, 100 million more still left to find, we’ll have plenty of opportunities to learn.
