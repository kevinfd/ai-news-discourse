# Can using public generative AI compromise attorney-client privilege and make confidential legal communications discoverable?

Bar and Bench · Waseem Pangarkar · 2026-11-27

The Viewpoint
Can using public generative AI compromise attorney-client privilege and make confidential legal communications discoverable?
Sharing privileged legal information with public generative AI may jeopardize confidentiality and attorney-client privilege.
Listen to this article
Generative artificial intelligence (AI) has transformed how lawyers, law firms, businesses and individuals access and process information. Platforms such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are increasingly used for legal research, contract analysis, drafting pleadings and evaluating litigation strategies.
However, this technological convenience raises a fundamental question:
Can sharing confidential legal communications, documents or litigation strategies with a publicly available AI platform compromise attorney–client privilege and expose such information to disclosure or discovery?
The concern arises because public AI platforms are neither advocates nor necessarily bound by the professional obligations of confidentiality governing the legal profession. The implications extend to lawyers using AI in their practice as well as clients independently seeking legal advice from these platforms.
In India, attorney - client privilege is principally governed by Sections 132 and 134 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA), which replaced Sections 126 and 129, respectively, of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, with effect from 1 July 2024.
Section 132 prohibits an advocate from disclosing, without the client’s express consent, communications made by or on behalf of the client in the course and for the purpose of professional engagement, documents encountered during professional service and legal advice given to the client. The obligation continues even after the engagement ends. Exceptions include communications made in furtherance of an illegal purpose and facts observed by an advocate during professional service showing that a crime or fraud has been committed since the commencement of the engagement.
Section 134 provides corresponding protection against compelling a person to disclose confidential communications with their legal adviser, subject to statutory exceptions.
The Supreme Court, in In Re: Summoning Advocates who give legal opinion or represent parties during investigation of cases and related issues, Suo Motu Writ Petition (Criminal) No. 2 of 2025, decided on October 31, 2025, reported as 2025 INSC 1275, reaffirmed the importance of professional confidentiality and the protection afforded to advocate–client communications.
The Bar Council of India Rules, particularly Rule 17 of Chapter II, Part VI, also reinforce an advocate’s obligation to preserve professional confidentiality.
The underlying principle is clear: clients must be able to communicate candidly with their advocates, secure in the knowledge that their communications and legal strategies are protected. The difficulty arises when confidential information is voluntarily shared with an external technology platform.
When a client uploads a confidential document, legal notice, internal investigation report, correspondence with an advocate or draft defence strategy to a public AI platform, the information is transmitted to a third-party service provider.
The platform’s terms of service and privacy policies consequently become relevant. Depending on the product, subscription, settings and contractual arrangements, submitted information may be retained, processed for service improvement, used for model training, reviewed in specified circumstances or disclosed in response to valid legal processes.
It would be inaccurate to suggest that every AI platform invariably uses all uploaded information for training or freely shares conversations with enforcement authorities. Different platforms and enterprise arrangements provide different levels of protection. Nevertheless, the possibility of retention, third-party processing and legally compelled disclosure raises legitimate concerns.
The fundamental question is whether information can continue to be regarded as confidential after it has been voluntarily shared with an independent commercial platform.
Under established principles of privilege, voluntary disclosure of confidential information to an unrelated third party may undermine the confidentiality necessary to sustain a claim of privilege. However, Indian courts have not yet conclusively determined whether uploading privileged material to a public generative AI platform necessarily constitutes waiver under Sections 132 and 134 of the BSA.
Importantly, waiver of privilege and discoverability are related but distinct questions. Disclosure does not automatically make every document discoverable. The nature of the information, purpose of disclosure, contractual safeguards and surrounding circumstances would all be relevant.
Recent American decisions provide important insight into how courts may approach this emerging issue.
In United States v. Heppner, No. 25-cr-503 (JSR), 2026 WL 436479 (SDNY, February 17, 2026), the defendant had used the publicly available version of Anthropic’s Claude to generate documents analysing potential defence strategies in criminal proceedings. He had independently uploaded information concerning his legal circumstances and subsequently shared the AI-generated documents with his defence lawyers.
The prosecution sought access to these documents. The defence claimed protection under attorney–client privilege and the work-product doctrine.
The Court rejected these claims. Judge Jed Rakoff held that communications with Claude were not attorney–client communications because Claude was not an attorney. The Court also considered the platform’s privacy terms, including provisions concerning the collection and use of inputs and outputs and potential disclosure to third parties. It was not persuaded that the defendant had a reasonable expectation of confidentiality.
Importantly, subsequently forwarding AI-generated documents to an advocate did not retrospectively convert them into privileged communications. The Court also rejected the work-product claim in the circumstances, since the documents had been independently generated without the direction or involvement of the defendant’s lawyers.
A contrasting perspective emerges from Warner v. Gilbarco, Inc., No. 2:24-cv-12333, 2026 WL 373043 (E.D. Mich., February 10, 2026). The Court declined to compel production of certain AI-related materials prepared by a litigant using ChatGPT, recognising that they reflected internal analysis and mental impressions prepared in anticipation of litigation.
The Court distinguished attorney–client privilege from work-product protection and recognised that the latter may survive disclosure in circumstances that do not substantially increase the likelihood of the material reaching an adversary.
Together, these decisions demonstrate that AI usage does not automatically destroy every form of legal protection. However, independently sharing confidential legal information with a public AI platform, particularly without the involvement of an advocate, can create significant difficulties in claiming privilege.
These American decisions are persuasive illustrations, not binding precedents in India. Nevertheless, their underlying principles merit serious consideration by Indian courts.
An equally important issue arises when a client independently approaches ChatGPT, Claude or another AI platform for legal advice without consulting a qualified advocate.
An individual may upload a termination notice, shareholders’ agreement, investigation report or details of a criminal complaint and seek advice on legal remedies, potential exposure or defence strategies. The individual may believe that the interaction is equivalent to a confidential consultation with a lawyer.
It is not.
There is no advocate–client relationship merely because a person asks an AI platform a legal question. The platform does not become a legal adviser within the meaning of Sections 132 and 134 of the BSA. Its responses do not acquire legal privilege merely because the subject concerns a legal dispute.
The consequences could be particularly serious in criminal investigations, regulatory proceedings, corporate disputes and matters involving allegations of fraud or money laundering. An individual under investigation who uploads internal emails, transaction records and a proposed defence strategy may inadvertently create an electronic record containing sensitive information, factual admissions and their own assessment of the case. Depending on the applicable law and circumstances, such information may potentially be sought through lawful investigative or discovery processes.
Subsequently engaging an advocate does not automatically convert the earlier AI interaction into a privileged communication.
AI may assist an individual in understanding legal issues, but it cannot substitute for a qualified legal adviser or create the legal confidentiality that attaches to a professional engagement.
The implications extend beyond clients independently using AI. Law firms increasingly rely on generative AI for contract review, summarisation of pleadings, analysis of evidence, legal research and preparation of litigation strategies.
If an advocate uploads privileged client communications or confidential documents to a public AI platform, questions may arise as to whether such disclosure is consistent with Section 132 of the BSA and the professional standards prescribed by the Bar Council of India.
An advocate’s statutory obligation not to disclose privileged communications and the separate question of whether the client’s privilege has been waived through third-party disclosure are related but distinct issues. The client’s consent to legal representation should not automatically be treated as consent to uploading confidential material to an external AI platform.
This does not mean that lawyers and law firms should abandon AI. They must, however, distinguish between public consumer platforms and properly governed enterprise AI environments. Enterprise arrangements may provide contractual restrictions on model training, encryption, access controls, data segregation and defined retention periods. While these safeguards substantially reduce confidentiality risks, they do not automatically establish legal privilege or eliminate every possibility of disclosure.
Law firms should adopt clear AI-use policies, prohibit uploading privileged or sensitive client material to unapproved public platforms, assess contractual and data-protection safeguards, and obtain appropriate client authorisation before processing confidential information through external systems.
Generative AI is becoming an increasingly important part of the legal profession. However, technological convenience cannot come at the cost of the fundamental principles upon which the administration of justice rests. Attorney–client privilege enables individuals and businesses to seek legal assistance with confidence and candour. The emerging jurisprudence, particularly United States v. Heppner, demonstrates that courts may be reluctant to extend traditional privilege protections to information voluntarily shared with public AI platforms.
Indian courts have yet to determine this precise question. Nevertheless, Sections 132 and 134 of the BSA, read with the professional obligations of advocates, provide sufficient reason for caution. The issue is not whether lawyers should use AI, but whether they can do so without compromising the trust placed in them by their clients. An AI platform may assist in understanding the law, analysing a dispute or preparing a legal strategy, but it cannot be assumed to provide the confidentiality of an advocate or the protection of professional privilege. In the age of generative AI, efficiency is important, but privilege remains fundamental.
