# CTech's Book Review: Adopting AI without a clear plan risks confusion instead of progress

CTech · Natali Yacobson · 2026-11-27

BiblioTech
CTech's Book Review: Adopting AI without a clear plan risks confusion instead of progress
Natali Yacobson, Founder and CEO of Blueye AI, shares insights after reading “The Coming Wave” by Mustafa Suleyman.
Natali Yacobson, Founder and CEO of Blueye AI, a business automation and customer engagement software company. She has joined CTech to share a review of “The Coming Wave: Technology, Power, and the Twenty-first Century's Greatest Dilemma” by Mustafa Suleyman with Michael Bhaskar. 
Title: The Coming Wave: Technology, Power, and the Twenty-first Century's Greatest Dilemma
Author: Mustafa Suleyman with Michael Bhaskar
Format: Book
Where: Home
Summary: 
A few months ago, I read The Coming Wave: Technology, Power, and the Twenty-first Century's Greatest Dilemma by Mustafa Suleyman. As organizations around the world try to keep pace with the rapid development of artificial intelligence, the question at the heart of this book feels more urgent by the day.
Technological change is now a constant part of business. Suleyman’s message is that artificial intelligence is more than another software upgrade: it changes what people and organizations can do, and how much power they hold. For executives and boards, the book offers a serious look at the rapid development of autonomous technology and the challenge of managing it without putting their organizations at risk.
Many books about technology drift toward hype or distant predictions of disaster. This one is grounded in the decisions institutions face today. Drawing on his experience as a co-founder of DeepMind and Inflection AI, and now as CEO of Microsoft AI, Suleyman connects his understanding of the technology with the difficult questions of how to govern it.
Important Themes:
One of the book’s strongest ideas is what Suleyman calls “The Containment Problem.” The question for the coming decade is not simply how powerful AI models can become, but whether we can keep control of them, protect the integrity of our data and maintain meaningful human oversight as these systems act more independently.
For business leaders, that is a sobering point. Established companies may assume that buying ready made AI tools or running a few isolated pilots will keep them ahead. Suleyman shows why that is not enough. As AI moves from assisting people to making decisions and taking action, the way systems are designed and controlled becomes a central leadership concern.
The book also makes clear that adopting new technology depends on whether an organization is ready for it. Suleyman points to institutional inertia, disconnected legacy systems and weak governance as barriers to using AI safely.
There is a gap between how quickly AI becomes available and how well businesses can put it to work. Suleyman argues that access to powerful models is spreading fast, but the ability to guide them safely and integrate them into complex workflows is much harder to develop. 
What I’ve Learned:
Bringing AI into an established business means connecting systems, adapting workflows and making sure the technology works reliably in practice. Organizations that make intelligent systems part of their everyday operations will be better placed to use them well. Those that adopt tools without a clear plan risk creating confusion instead of progress.
Suleyman makes the case for responsible use of autonomous systems. For established businesses in traditional sectors, “containment” does not mean stopping progress. It means setting clear boundaries, protecting internal data and making sure AI agents can work safely within existing ERP and CRM systems.
Even large companies can lose ground when they add disconnected tools without a clear plan for how they fit together. To make AI useful, they need sound data, sensible risk controls and working processes that let autonomous tools help people without introducing security risks or slowing work down.
Suleyman also examines the move toward AI agents that can plan a series of actions and interact directly with business databases. Rather than spend months assessing software in isolation, leaders need to understand how these agents will behave when they are put to work in real organizations.
Further, the book shows why managing technological change requires more than choosing the next software tool. Leaders need to keep pace with regulation, set clear rules for data and choose partners carefully if they want AI capabilities to deliver lasting value to the business.
Critiques:
The strength of The Coming Wave is its broad view and its willingness to confront the risks of new technology. It asks executives to take a hard look at whether their organizations are ready, how they govern AI and where their technology plans are taking them.
One limitation is that the book mostly looks at history and geopolitics on a large scale. Business leaders may come away wanting more practical guidance for their own organizations. Anyone looking for immediate steps will need to pair Suleyman’s broad argument with a plan suited to their industry and operations.
Applying those broad ideas can be especially difficult in traditional or heavily regulated sectors. These businesses have to pursue innovation while meeting strict requirements and managing operational risk. Turning Suleyman’s principles of containment into everyday IT decisions takes careful work in each organization. 
Who Should Read This Book:
The Coming Wave is well worth reading for executives, IT directors, board members and leaders of established organizations who want to understand how AI is changing business and society.
It is especially relevant to leaders planning for long term digital change. The book helps them look beyond the latest software trend and think seriously about how to build useful, secure AI capabilities within their organizations.
