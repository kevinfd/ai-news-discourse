# AI regulation must precede job losses and economic disruption, says PMI chief

The Hindu · Mini Tejaswi · 2026-11-27

Governments in the U.S., Europe and India are falling behind the rapid adoption of artificial intelligence (AI), often waiting for economic disruption to set regulatory frameworks, safety guardrails and policies, Project Management Institute (PMI) President Johannes Heinlein has said.
In an exclusive interview with The Hindu, Mr. Heinlein said governments were struggling to keep pace with AI’s rapid advancement and its spread across businesses. Rather than anticipating potential disruptions, policymakers often wait for problems such as widespread job losses, failed business investments or financial instability to emerge before intervening.
According to him, a coordinated effort involving governments, industry and academia is essential to managing the transition. “A coordinated coalition of government, industry, and academia is critical to managing the transition. Hope India will act as a leading indicator rather than waiting for job losses and stalled investments to force policy action,” he said.
On concerns over the pace of AI deployment, including warnings from Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates, Mr. Heinlein said he shared many of the concerns but remained an “internal optimist”, focused on ensuring that AI delivers positive societal outcomes rather than triggering a doomsday scenario.
Drawing parallels with previous industrial revolutions, he argued that technological progress required innovation, experimentation and a willingness to fail. However, he cautioned that corporate leaders were committing major strategic errors by prioritising short-term gains over sustainable growth.
Some companies, he said, were using AI as a justification to lay off thousands of employees to conceal shortcomings in their business strategies, thereby undermining long-term human capital.
“Other misconceptions include believing AI will solve every problem, assuming companies can innovate in isolation without strategic partnerships, and overlooking the true financial and environmental costs of the technology—from energy and climate impact to mounting monthly token expenses that often outstrip actual output value,” he said.
AI is also reshaping employment prospects for entry-level developers and technology professionals, with conventional coding skills rapidly losing relevance and career lifecycles shortening. While workers unwilling to adapt risk displacement, new opportunities will emerge for those acquiring complementary skills.
“Human judgment, deep questioning, and project management will remain indispensable, placing the onus on forward-looking leaders to retrain young talent rather than discard them,” Mr. Heinlein said.
He revealed that PMI would soon launch an advanced corporate programme, organisational maturity assessment frameworks and large-scale partnerships, with particular emphasis on Indian companies.
PMI is also working with governments in the U.S., Europe, China, India and Singapore on skills development and sustainable career pathways, independently of political changes, to prepare the workforce for an AI-driven economy.
