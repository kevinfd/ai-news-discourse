# The AI apocalypse believers putting their money where their fears are

Business Insider · Thibault Spirlet, Thibault Spirlet (tspirlet@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-27

AI safety experts shared how their fears of AI-driven human extinction are reshaping their money habits. CFOTO/Future Publishing via Getty Images AI safety researchers shared how their fears of an AI catastrophe are influencing their finances. Some are saving more and investing in AI infrastructure, while others are changing how they live. Despite their warnings, some still plan financially for a future they fear may never arrive. As AI leaders have renewed warnings about the technology's potential dangers , some researchers are confronting a deeply personal question: How do you plan financially for a future you fear may never arrive? Business Insider spoke with six AI safety experts about how likely they believe an AI-driven catastrophe or human extinction is, and whether those fears have changed how they spend, save, invest, or plan for retirement. Their views don't necessarily reflect those of the wider AI research community, and their financial decisions aren't necessarily a model for others to follow. In January, seven personal finance and AI experts told Business Insider that people should continue saving for retirement, even if AI radically transforms the economy. Here's how six AI safety experts we spoke with are approaching their own financial futures. Roman Yampolskiy, computer science professor at the University of Louisville Roman Yampolskiy. Courtesy of Roman Yampolskiy Roman Yampolskiy , an AI researcher and computer science professor at the University of Louisville, estimates a 99.9% probability of human extinction from advanced AI over the next century unless humanity fundamentally changes course. But he continues to save for retirement, reasoning that even a slim chance of survival makes financial security worthwhile. "Even a 99.9% probability of extinction leaves a 0.1% chance of survival, and I would prefer not to be poor in that scenario," he told Business Insider. Yampolskiy said he's financially comfortable enough to maximize his retirement contributions while buying what he wants, partly because he avoids luxury purchases and lives in relatively affordable Kentucky. However, he said he "explicitly" avoids investing directly in labs developing advanced AI. Adam Gleave, CEO of AI alignment research nonprofit FAR.AI Adam Gleave. Courtesy of Adam Gleave Adam Gleave, CEO of AI alignment research nonprofit FAR.AI, estimates a 15% chance of an AI-driven catastrophe or human extinction over the next several decades, including a 5% chance within the next few years. Unlike researchers who have stopped saving for retirement, Gleave said he's actually saving more because he believes superintelligent AI could eliminate jobs and make his future earnings less certain. His investments are also tilted toward AI computing infrastructure, including semiconductor suppliers and computing providers. Screenshots of his holdings shared with Business Insider last month showed positions in neoclouds, including CoreWeave, and Nebius, high-bandwidth memory manufacturers like Micron, SK Hynix, and the VanEck Semiconductor ETF. Gleave said roughly half his investments remain in broad-based global equity indexes for diversification, while his AI-focused holdings serve partly as a hedge against rapid technological change. "If I'm wrong and AI isn't a big deal, I'll lose on my investments, but I'll have many years of labor income to earn that loss back," he said. David Krueger, executive director of AI safety nonprofit Evitable David Krueger. Courtesy of David Krueger David Krueger , executive director of AI safety nonprofit Evitable, estimates that humanity faces a greater than 50% chance of extinction from AI, potentially within the next five years. He said humanity could lose control of advanced AI as soon as this year or next, and that those fears have profoundly shaped his personal and professional decisions. "I've dedicated my life to this cause and passed up massive financial incentives to work at AI companies," he told Business Insider. Krueger said he has also chosen not to have children and is "definitely not spending time thinking about retirement." Zvi Mowshowitz, founder of the policy nonprofit Balsa Research Zvi Mowshowitz. Courtesy of Zvi Mowshowitz Zvi Mowshowitz, an AI analyst and founder of the policy nonprofit Balsa Research, estimates a roughly 70% chance of an AI-driven catastrophe or extinction within his natural lifetime, with most of that risk concentrated over the next two to 20 years. Despite those fears, he said he continues to plan for retirement much as he otherwise would, partly for his own and his family's peace of mind. "I still plan for retirement mostly as I would have otherwise, because that could still happen," he told Business Insider. Mowshowitz said he has also made investments based on his expectations about AI's development, which he said have performed well, though he did not disclose specific holdings. His advice is to prepare for radically different AI futures without abandoning conventional financial planning, including for worlds that "look normal." Nate Soares, president of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute Nate Soares told BI that humanity only gets "one try" at aligning superintelligence — and failure would be final. Courtesy of Nate Soares Nate Soares, president of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, says there's a greater than 50% chance AI will cause human extinction within the next 10 years. His fears have shaped his retirement planning : "I do not save for retirement right now," he told The Atlantic CEO Nicholas Thompson in an interview in October. "I have a 401(k) from when I worked at Google, but I haven't contributed for a decade," said Soares, coauthor of "If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies." He believes sufficiently advanced AI could develop goals that conflict with humanity's interests and eventually become self-sufficient, potentially making the planet uninhabitable for humans. Despite his grim outlook, Soares said he remains hopeful humanity can prevent that outcome: "I don't think it's inevitable." Andreas Kirsch, former research scientist at Google DeepMind Andreas Kirsch, a former research scientist at Google DeepMind, says an AI-driven catastrophe on the scale of the COVID-19 pandemic is "quite likely within the next 5 years" if governments and AI companies fail to take sufficient precautions. He said human extinction is also possible, but remains optimistic that those outcomes can be avoided through stronger regulation, monitoring, and government intervention. "I'm an optimist if we listen to the pessimists," Kirsch told Business Insider. Despite his concerns, Kirsch said he remains conservative in his investments and hasn't changed his financial plans because of worries about extinction risks. He said he understands why some researchers, including Soares, have abandoned retirement planning, but wouldn't endorse that approach "given the high uncertainty around the outcomes and our ability to still shape them."
