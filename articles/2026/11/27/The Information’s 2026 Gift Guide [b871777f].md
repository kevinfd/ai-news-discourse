# The Information’s 2026 Gift Guide

The Information · The Information Staff · 2026-11-27

In a twisted version of future Christmases, perhaps AIs will carry out all the tasks involved in gift giving. But lucky for us, Andy Jassy still has all the bots blocked! So here once again is The Information’s annual Gift Guide, a project that we pursue with as much consideration and rigor as our everyday reporting. None of these items come via a publicist’s pitch, and none of the suggestions contain affiliate links. These are all things that we genuinely love and can picture wrapping up ourselves. Suffice it to say, there’s nothing artificial about our studious consideration and good taste. Entertainment 

 I couldn’t afford most Nintendo 64 games growing up, so I was a frequent presence at the local video game rental store: I distinctly remember taking home the original version of “The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time”—and needing to return it jusstttt as I was on the cusp of saving Hyrule, the game’s fictional realm, and beating the big baddie, Ganondorf. This Switch 2 remake of the game has given me a shot at redemption. It’s one of the most critically lauded games of the year—and for good reason. The graphics are dazzling, and I can only describe them as “wet”: Everyone looks magnificently dewy. I also cannot overrecommend the nostalgia dopamine hit from getting to rummage through those treasure chests again.— Justin McCraw, senior software engineer 

 A couple of weeks ago, the internet was abuzz about an AI-generated trailer that imagined an alt-history in which Nazi Germany and America wage some sort of temporal war. Sure, the clip was cool, but I still have my doubts about whether we’re yet at the point where AI can make a comprehensible feature film. In the meantime, there’s “Fatherland,” a 1992 alt-history bestseller from Robert Harris, the ultra-dependable thriller author. It imagines a 1960s landscape where Nazi Germany triumphed in Europe during World War II, and an aging Hitler clings to power. I think it’s Harris’ best world-building effort, which he accomplished through an enormous amount of research into how Hitler’s chief architect, Albert Speer, hoped to transform Berlin. (Those plans included the Volkshalle, a massive dome; in the novel, Harris supersizes Speer’s own ambitions, imagining the Volkshalle as a structure large enough to have its own microclimate.) “Fatherland” is taut as hell and completely unputdownable—with a weary, disillusioned city police captain, Xavier March, caught up in an intricate plot that runs through the Reich’s highest ranks. As March investigates, he keeps practically tripping over dead bodies. Then there’s the mystery of the terrible secrets kept in a hidden Swiss bank account.— Abram Brown, features editor
