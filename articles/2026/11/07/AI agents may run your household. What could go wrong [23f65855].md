# AI agents may run your household. What could go wrong?

Mashable · Rebecca Ruiz · 2026-11-07

AI agents may run your household. What could go wrong?
Table of Contents
Erica Layer, a mom of a 2- and 6-year-old, has an enviable to-do list for her AI agent.
The busy executive relies on an Instinct agent for low-stakes but time-intensive household tasks like meal planning, calendaring, and birthday reminders.
"I do get a huge amount of time back," Layer says of delegating to an AI agent. "But also I'm doing the things that I just was dropping completely before."

Still, Layer is conflicted about her agent's persistence. The AI has suggested completing tasks on her behalf without explaining what happens to the data it accesses. Recently, her Instinct agent researched a family trip in Portugal, where she lives, and offered to book train tickets.
The catch? It wanted her kids' passports to make the purchase. Layer runs a fractional AI company, operates her own team of agents professionally, and has taught nearly 100 people to build agentic operating systems. She consulted Instinct's data privacy terms, felt they lacked transparency, and worried about how her data would be used. Layer, who wrote about her experience, made the reservation herself instead.
Mashable attempted to contact Instinct's founder for comment but did not receive a reply as of publication.
Like many parents — particularly moms — Layer is eager for an agent to handle the exhausting aspects of running a household. At the same time, she's unwilling to connect her personal data to a platform without clarity about its policies.
Parents are poised to be among the earliest adopters of AI agents for their families, yet unclear risks may compromise their personal and sensitive data. Experts say the excitement over agentic products for families is warranted, but that parents should first grasp how to best protect their data.
"I would love to have this mental load out of my head"
In a few short months, overwhelmed parents in the U.S. suddenly have their pick of agentic products. The September launch of Meta's Muse, reportedly in response to Instinct's early success, sparked instant competition. OpenAI quickly followed with its AI agent Dots, and Anthropic opened a waitlist for its "proactive" agent Claude Duet.
These products have overshadowed startup offerings like Fambot and Fo, both created by working parents and tech executives turned entrepreneurs. Fambot bills itself as a parent's "AI chief of staff" and Fo insists it'll "get sh*t done."
Meanwhile, Marissa Mayer, formerly the CEO of Yahoo, debuted her AI agent Dazzle to viral controversy after sharing with the New York Times that she asked her AI agent to choose three of her twin daughters' standout qualities for a school birthday slide. Mayer said on X the anecdote lacked important context about trying to challenge Dazzle with the prompt.
Either way, Layer wrote on Substack that she didn't appreciate how the response to Dazzle minimized the value of AI tools for households, particularly for working moms.
S. Shyam Sundar, director of the Penn State Center for Socially Responsible Artificial Intelligence, says parents who turn to AI to save time on tedious tasks aren't being selfish.
"I think it's looking out for more the psychological welfare of the household and the relationships within that household," he said.
Amy Wu Martin, a new mom and partner at the early-stage investment firm Menlo Ventures, says she's saved a lot of time letting Muse and Claude research whether restaurants and hotels are kid-friendly.
"I would love to have this mental load out of my head," she says of mundane parenting tasks.
Parents as early AI adopters
Menlo Ventures' survey research suggests parents are indeed AI power users. They are nearly twice as likely to use AI daily as non-parents. Among parents polled by the firm, 40 percent use AI daily for tasks like meal planning, shopping, and logistics. She envisions a future in which AI agents can handle purchases for parents, like ordering diapers.
Personally, Wu Martin has stopped short of connecting her inbox to an agent because of data security concerns. She's open, however, to letting an AI agent coordinate calendars with her husband.

Fambot, co-founded by dad of three and former Uber executive David Reich, turns school emails, sports schedules, and parent chats into a clear daily plan.
The agent gets read-only access to a parent's inbox, calendars, and WhatsApp groups and proceeds to manage every task it perceives relevant. It parses the school newsletters, sports schedules, and parent chats, then generates specific action items, calendar events, and a text summary each evening of what the parent needs to know.
Shivani Poddar, who previously worked as a senior engineering lead at Google DeepMind, recently launched the AI agent Fo through her startup Wajo AI. While it's not designed specifically for parents, Poddar says she relies on it for tasks like reminding her to buy a Halloween costume well in advance and finding a Mandarin class in San Francisco for her toddler. Fo can also bring a human customer service agent into its loop if necessary.
Really, Poddar says, she's looking to gain time back to spend with her child, and she knows other parents want that, too.
AI agent privacy and safety red flags
Kayne McGladrey, a virtual chief information security officer, says parents who want to use an AI agent can take reasonable privacy precautions.
Yet he understands that many consumers have developed "privacy nihilism." They feel like their digital lives will be compromised at any moment regardless of their choices. That can make vigilance feel like a losing battle, especially when privacy and security policies are dense and packed with jargon.
Privacy phrases to watch out for
He recommends evaluating an agentic product's marketing claims, especially those related to privacy and security. Consumers should look for phrases like "zero retention," "zero training," "no storage on the provider's server," and "zero-knowledge encryption."
Such phrases indicate the company's data practices prioritize consumer privacy and security by storing information carefully and limiting whether it's used to train AI models.
McGladrey, a senior member of the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), also says consumers should check to see if a company isolates credentials.
This is critical if an agent, for example, has a consumer's bank account and Gmail passwords. Those pieces of data should be stored and used separately. When saved in the same place, it dramatically increases the risk that they can be stolen by bad actors who can exploit a user's inbox to access their bank account.
Why inbox privacy matters
Generally speaking, parents may underestimate the sensitive information in their inbox. They may want an AI agent to read the weekly school newsletter but forget that their inbox also includes private communication with school staff, health care, and mental health providers, for example.
McGladrey says an agent having access to a child's health care information isn't necessarily an immediate problem. Instead, the longer-term question is where that data is stored and what happens to it over time.
If an AI agent startup goes out of business and lands in bankruptcy court, that data could end up in the hands of another company. Advertisers would also love to access families' lives at this scale for better targeting, McGladrey notes.
While consumers may be inclined to trust an AI agent from a well-known platform like Meta, that company has had its own privacy scandal.
McGladrey says IEEE is developing new standards for agentic security so companies can reference widely accepted privacy and security benchmarks. Those likely won't be ready until 2027, however.
Risk vs. reward
Nevertheless, McGladrey understands that some parents will embrace AI agents for convenience, despite the potential trade-offs.
"I think that it's going to be an individual determination of what we're willing to accept for risk versus reward or perceived reward," McGladrey says.
As much as Erica Layer would love to maximize an AI agent for her family's needs, she presently uses Instinct without connecting it to her email or personal information. Fambot, Fo, and Muse, among other agentic products, are not available to her yet in Europe.
"I am waiting for the company that builds with safety in mind," she says.
