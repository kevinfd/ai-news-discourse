# Generative AI training for leaders is a litmus test for whether you can remain a leader

note · 石原愛信 │ 石川県の生成Ai研修・Dx相談 · 2026-11-07

Generative AI training for leaders is a litmus test for whether you can remain a leader
Training is not graded on the day you attend, but on the morning after you return.
“Let’s have the leaders take it first, and then spread it to their subordinates.” 
This is the most common order I hear when asked for generative AI training.
Companies spend money and time sending leaders to training. 
Therefore, the moment you are told to “go,” the test has already begun.
Two people who took the same training ended up in completely different places a year later.
Today, I will tell you that story.
*The following is a fictional story. The facilities and people appearing in it do not exist.
On the day they were told to “go,” the two head nurses had the same look on their faces
“Kagayaki-en,” a special nursing home in the suburbs of Ishikawa Prefecture.
60 residents, 35 staff members.
Records are kept on paper and in Excel, and the administrative manager also handles ICT.
The board chairman’s catchphrase is, “I’m not saying don’t use AI. But if something happens, I’ll be in trouble if I can’t explain it to the families.”
One day, the board chairman called in A, the head nurse of the 2nd floor, and B, the head nurse of the 3rd floor.
“Next month, there is a generative AI training for leaders. I want you both to go.”
“...Who will cover the shift that day?” asked B.
“I’ll go. What should I do when I get back?” asked A. 
“That’s something you two can think about after you go.”
Both are diligent head nurses. At this point, there is no difference between them.
On the day they were sent off, no one had been graded yet.
The morning after the training, the fork in the road arrived in five minutes
2nd floor. At the end of the handover, A said:
“Honestly, I didn’t understand everything from yesterday’s training. But there was one thing that seemed useful for refining the wording of records. Could someone try it out with me?”
“If the head nurse doesn’t understand it, there’s no way I can,” said a staff member. 
“That’s why we’ll do it together. Next Wednesday, just for 15 minutes.”
The same morning, 3rd-floor break room.
“Head nurse, how was the training?” 
“It was good. I have the materials, so I’ll summarize and share them sometime.”
The materials were put away in a drawer at the office desk.
A didn’t teach it well. 
Without fully understanding it, they just replaced it with one task from their own workplace and decided on a day to try it together.
Breaking things down doesn’t mean teaching them well; it means replacing them with one task from your own workplace.
Six months later, only one of them was mentioned in the office.
Three months later. On the second floor, nursing staff began trying out draft records and coming to ask Person A for advice.
"Is it okay to show this to the family?"
The staff had started using the board chair's favorite phrases on their own initiative.
Around the same time on the third floor, the younger staff were secretly using it on their own smartphones. 
They wouldn't tell Person B.
"Even if I ask the supervisor, they probably won't understand."
And six months later, in the office.
"On the second floor, incident reports are being submitted faster. It seems Person A created a template for how to write them."
"What about the third floor?"
"...I think the training materials are still in the drawer."
Person B was not being lazy.
While thinking, "I'll explain it once I understand it properly," half a year had passed.
The more serious a person is, the more they get stuck here.
While waiting to "understand before explaining," subordinates move on ahead.
Why is there such a difference in one year between two people who received the same training?
Even if the training content is the same, subordinates' questions keep gathering only around the person who told their team, "I've been to the training."
One year later. The board chair said to Person A:
"Next fiscal year, I want to ask you, Person A, to take on the role of teaching the new supervisors." "I'm still not very knowledgeable about it yet."
"I'm not looking for someone who is knowledgeable. I'm looking for someone who brings things back."
Around the same time on the third floor, a nursing staff member said this to Person B:
"It's faster to ask the AI than to ask the supervisor."
It was then that Person B realized for the first time what they had been tested on a year ago.
If you line up the one year of these two people, it looks like this.
- The morning after training: Person A: "Let's try one thing together" / Person B: "I'll explain it all together later"
- 3 months later: In Person A's department, subordinates come to ask for advice; in Person B's department, subordinates use it secretly.
- 6 months later: In Person A's department, a standard for reports is established; Person B's materials are left in a drawer.
- 1 year later: Person A is asked to take on a teaching role; Person B is told, 'It's faster to just ask the AI.'
You might think, 'Was the training too difficult?'
But both people attended the same training on the same day.
It wasn't the content of the training that created the difference.
The grading for training doesn't start on the day you attend, but on the morning after you return.
3 steps you can take starting tomorrow to bring the training back with you
- On the way home from the training, write one line in your smartphone memo about 'one thing to talk about tomorrow morning.'
- At the next morning's briefing or morning assembly, talk about 'one thing you want to try' rather than what you understood. It is perfectly fine to say, 'I don't fully understand it yet.'
- Within one week, set a day to spend just 15 minutes using it together with your subordinates.
The final results will come in one year.
I will summarize today's talk into three points.
- The test begins the moment you are told to 'go and attend.'
- The difference is made not on the day of the training, but on the morning after you return.
- You don't have to be a good teacher. Just say, 'I went and attended.'
It is said that generative AI will make leaders unnecessary.
But in reality, it's not that you become unnecessary.
It just reveals whether you were only managing or actually driving the team.that is.
If, starting tomorrow, all your subordinates could create materials faster than you, 
how would you protect your position as a leader?
Generative AI training for leaders is a litmus test for whether you can remain a leader. 
The final results will come in one year.
Why don't leaders who receive training share it with their subordinates? I wrote about that psychological process here previously.
This talk can be turned into a 90-minute hands-on session for chiefs and managers.
It is structured so that they decide on one thing to tell their team the next morning and take it back with them.
