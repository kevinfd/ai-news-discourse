# Microsoft is winding down its Oracle deal. It could be a sign of what’s to come

Fast Company · Francis Northwood · 2026-11-13

In late September, news circulated that the hyperscaler Oracle had sent a force majeure notice to the developer of its New Mexico data center campus, Project Jupiter, seeking to delay payments on the project should it fail to come on line in 2028 as planned. In response, Oracle took to X, writing that “force-majeure notices are commonplace in developments of this scale. They do not, by themselves, establish a project delay or change delivery expectations.”
The hiccup was one in a series. According to Financial Times reporting from September, loans tied to the project had been “privately quoted at 89 to 91 cents on the dollar by syndicated banks like Santander and Jefferies,” reflecting, more broadly, Oracle’s barely-above-junk credit rating and its place as the company to watch for those who are concerned about an AI bubble bursting soon. That fact is made crystal clear by both press surrounding the subject and the strength of the market for Oracle credit-default swaps (essentially insurance against Oracle defaulting on its debt).
Things have since gotten worse for Oracle. Last week, overlooked by many amid the midterms and unfolding drama at Nvidia, the Financial Times reported that Oracle canceled an estimated $7 billion chip deal with the Chinese tech giant Tencent under pressure from the Trump administration. Two days later, Bloomberg reported that Microsoft had been winding down a 2023 deal to rent AI computing from Oracle that had originally been arranged to meet the computing demands of its Copilot AI assistant.
Microsoft reportedly walked away because Oracle’s Nvidia chips were already outdated—a growing concern for investors financing expensive AI infrastructure that risks becoming obsolete before it can pay for itself. As one fund manager put it colorfully to the Financial Times:
“It’s like financing a fax machine and then someone invented email.”
If Microsoft was in fact dissatisfied with the chips, this marks one of the more significant manifestations of the chip-lifecycle concern, which so far has seemingly lived in analysts’ forecasts. It also bears out the problem of mismatched timelines. Oracle is operating on decade-plus horizons, in one sense, with its data center development. But the pressure to meet short-term obligations with big swings and the need for labs to develop their frontier models mean that the build-out goings-on are happening in large part on very different timelines—year to year or even month to month.
Oracle predictably pushed back on X, saying demand for its AI infrastructure remains strong, even for older Nvidia chips. The company said that in its first quarter (which runs from June through August), it renewed or resold capacity using GPUs that were mostly at least four years old, securing long-term contracts at a 20% premium over previous pricing.
