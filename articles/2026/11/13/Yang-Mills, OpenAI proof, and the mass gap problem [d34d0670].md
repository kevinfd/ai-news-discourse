# Yang-Mills, OpenAI proof, and the mass gap problem

Hugging Face · pretext · 2026-11-13

Yang-Mills, OpenAI proof, and the mass gap problem
A few days ago, OpenAI made an announcement about the existence of Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) and a unified completion of the Standard Model of particle physics. It sounds like a physics problem but this is actually the first half of the Yang-Mills existence and mass gap problem, one of the seven 1-million $ questions from the Clay Institute.
After Navier-Stokes a few weeks ago, my first thought was, OpenAI did it again?
Actually, not quite...yet.
As usual, to share their results with the world, they produced a huge manuscript (1147 pages !) along with a proof verified in Lean, a programming language in which a computer checks every single logical step of a mathematical proof. But Lean is not enough: we need to make sure that what the proof is about is really the problem mathematicians and physicists are interested in. A computer can check that a proof is correct, but not that the definitions written in the code mean what we think they mean.
For this, OpenAI called a special group to examine their proof. And the group just released its first assessment. To understand what is at stake, let me walk you through the Yang-Mills problem, its link to the strong nuclear force, and whether all of this matters for what happens inside particle accelerators.
Quantum Field Theory : our best framework to understand particles
As its name indicates, quantum field theory is about applying the principles of quantum mechanics to fields, like the electromagnetic field. Quantum mechanics is usually well defined when we are dealing with a finite number of degrees of freedom, like a bunch of point particles each having a position and a velocity. But with fields, which have a value at every point of continuous space, things get finicky.
You may have heard about the path integral approach to quantum mechanics, a beautiful idea due to Richard Feynman. The idea involves computing a giant integral over all the trajectories a particle can follow.
It might sound scary, but with a finite number of particles, we have ways to make this rigorous and well defined by taking careful limits. But applying the same idea to fields is complicated.

Quantum Electrodynamics
Let's start with pure electromagnetism, when no matter is present. This is the easy case, because the nasty path integrals can then be shown to be computable and well defined...But the resulting theory is not very interesting! This is because it only contains photons (the particle associated with electromagnetism), and they don't interact with each other. Pure electromagnetism is a theory of free photons moving in space, and passing past each other as if they were alone.
Now add matter, for instance electrons. We can write the path integral that describes the coupling of electromagnetism and electrons. The theory is supposed to be way more interesting because photons provide in principle a mean for electrons to interact...but this time the big path integral it is not well defined. So the correlation functions are in theory meaningless.
The good news is that physicists found a way around, called the perturbative expansion.
The so-called coupling between electromagnetism and matter (it measures how strongly they interact, and is related to the famous fine-structure constant ) is weak enough, at least at large distances, that we can formally expand the ill-defined integrals in powers of this coupling, and truncate this expansion to a finite number of terms that we can actually compute. With the perturbative approach, each term of the expansion becomes symbolized by one of the famous "Feynman diagrams" that you probably have seen.
Along the way, infinities do show up, but physicists learned to absorb them in a systematic way, a procedure called renormalization. And the results of doing this are surprisingly accurate!
Even if the whole perturbation procedure seems crude, the numbers we find this way are in great agreement with particle physics experiments. This is even a big understatement: for the magnetic moment of the electron, theory and experiment agree to about one part in a trillion!!
In this resulting theory called Quantum Electrodynamics (QED), we can compute interactions between photons and matter particles, and the fact that the photon is massless translates into the fact that electromagnetism is "long range", with forces decreasing as the inverse square of the distance, , which we know from macroscopic experiments.
That being said, we know the perturbative expansion trick has its limits. First, if you tried to sum all the terms of the expansion instead of truncating it, the sum is believed to diverge: only the first terms are useful. Second, in QED the coupling slowly grows as you look at shorter distances (higher energies), and at some point the expansion would stop making sense. But in practice, this limit is so far away (at energies absurdly beyond anything reachable) that physicists don't care much, especially since QED gets absorbed into a bigger theory long before that.
The theory is "effective" even if not rigorous. Is there a way to define a rigorous version of QED (electromagnetism + matter)? Probably not! Numerical evidence suggests that if you try, the interactions disappear and you end up with a boring free theory.
Now what about the other forces?
Yang-Mills to rule them all
Since things worked well enough for electromagnetism, physicists tried to apply the same recipe to the other forces, like the weak nuclear force (responsible for some types of radioactivity), or the strong nuclear force, which holds quarks together inside protons and neutrons, and, indirectly, protons and neutrons together inside atomic nuclei. And it turns out there is a beautiful framework to encompass all these cases: Yang-Mills theory.
If you studied electromagnetism, you may remember that instead of manipulating the electric and magnetic fields, you can replace them with another field: the vector potential, often denoted A.

And what makes the idea powerful is that there is a free ingredient in this formulation: a group G. For all practical purposes, you can think of groups of matrices, like the unitary matrices of dimension N (the group U(N)) or the special unitary ones, with determinant 1 (the group SU(N)). When you write a Yang-Mills theory, you have to choose a group, and the field A takes values in the matrices associated with it.
If you pick U(1) (the "one-dimensional" unitary matrices, aka complex numbers of modulus 1), numbers commute, the commutator vanishes, and you get back electromagnetism. What about using other groups? If you use G=SU(2), you get the weak force (well... almost: you also need the Higgs field, but let's leave that aside...). Now if you choose G=SU(3), you get the strong nuclear force, a theory called Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD). That's why our best theory of particle physics, the so-called Standard Model, uses the "product" group G=U(1)×SU(2)×SU(3), which describes all three forces in a single framework. Add the matter particles (quarks, electrons and their cousins) and the Higgs field, and you get essentially everything we know about particle physics, except gravity. Beautiful, isn't it?
Well, actually, getting there wasn't that simple. Let's focus on Yang-Mills with G=SU(3), supposed to represent the strong nuclear force. This idea was initially met with skepticism (Wolfgang Pauli raised the objection as early as 1954): the same way that photons are massless, the corresponding mediating particles of this theory (called gluons) would be massless. So the strong nuclear force should be long-ranged like electromagnetism. But we know it is not! It only acts at very short distances, about the size of a proton (with an exponential decay as the distance increases). How can a theory with massless gluons produce a short-range force? This puzzle is at the heart of the "mass gap" problem.
To make concrete calculations with Yang-Mills SU(3), physicists tried the same perturbative expansion trick that worked with electromagnetism. But this time, there is a surprise. In electromagnetism, the coupling grows at short distances. In Yang-Mills, the commutator term (which means that gluons interact with each other, unlike photons) reverses the trend: the coupling shrinks at short distances and grows at long distances. So while in electromagnetism "small perturbations" work at long enough distances, with QCD it is the other way around: the perturbative expansion tells you about very short distances, where quarks and gluons barely interact, and their interaction vanishes as they get closer. This result is known as asymptotic freedom, and it earned David Gross, Frank Wilczek and David Politzer the Nobel Prize in 2004. And David Gross is now leading the assessment group !
Going on a lattice
What if we want to understand what happens at long distances? And by long distance here, we mean the size of a proton! There, the coupling is strong, the perturbative expansion does not work, and we have to find something else to obtain actual numbers from our ill-defined path integral.
One idea is to discretize the theory and consider it not in the continuum, but on a lattice (think about pixels or voxels in a video game). This is an idea due to Kenneth Wilson in 1974: consider a finite box of size L, discretize space-time using a lattice of spacing , and perform non-perturbative calculations of the correlation functions on a computer.
On the lattice, everything is perfectly well defined: it's just a (very big) ordinary integral. And if is big enough and small enough, you might get a good approximation of the "true" theory.
And it works! But it also leads to surprising results. For that, let's remember that we are now looking at "pure" Yang-Mills theory with SU(3). There is no matter (no quarks), so this is the equivalent of pure electromagnetism. I said that one was very simple and boring, because photons don't interact with each other. But with SU(3) and gluons, the story is different. This is because U(1) is an abelian group, while SU(3) is non-abelian: products of matrices do not commute, so the commutator in the Lagrangian does not vanish. And the consequence is that gluons do interact with each other!
They interact so strongly at long distances that they can never be isolated. This is called confinement: objects carrying the "charge" of the strong force (confusingly called color, hence the chromo- in chromodynamics) can't exist alone at large distances. Only color-neutral combinations can. In real QCD, with quarks, these are protons, neutrons, pions... And in pure Yang-Mills, where there are only gluons, they are called glueballs: particles made of gluons stuck together.
And it turns out that glueballs can interact as well. Remember that in QED, photons were mediating the interaction, and electrons were interacting. Here we only have glueballs, so glueballs interact by exchanging glueballs. This is fine. But simulating this on a lattice leads to a numerical surprise: there is a minimum mass that glueballs can have, about 1.5 to 1.7 GeV (roughly one and a half times the mass of a proton).
A mass gap emerges, even though there is no mass scale at all in the original theory! The gluons of the Lagrangian are massless, yet the lightest particle of the quantum theory is massive. A consequence is that glueballs can only mediate interactions at short distances. In the real world, with quarks, a similar mechanism explains why the nuclear force between protons and neutrons is short-ranged.
Note that this mass is a purely quantum effect: it disappears in the classical limit. This is why there is no "classical" strong force that we could feel at our scale, like we feel electromagnetism.
The problem for mathematicians
As we said, this trick of "getting on a lattice in a finite box, then letting the box grow to infinity while the spacing goes to zero", has been invented to circumvent the fact that we are not sure the fundamental objects (correlation functions defined by path integrals) can be rigorously defined.
There are actual rules to decide whether a quantum field theory is "mathematically well defined": they are called the Wightman axioms (another, essentially equivalent version exists, the Osterwalder–Schrader axioms, better suited to lattice-type constructions). They require things like: a unique vacuum, energies that are never negative, compatibility with special relativity, and the fact that measurements made at places too far apart to exchange a light signal don't influence each other.
Theories satisfying these Wightman axioms have been constructed in 2 and 3 space-time dimensions. But no interacting theory in 4 dimensions (our world!) has ever been shown to satisfy them. I said that pure EM is trivial, and EM+matter (QED) is probably impossible to rigorously construct. But what about pure YM with SU(3)? Or more generally pure YM with SU(N), or even other groups? Pure Yang-Mills is the best candidate, precisely thanks to asymptotic freedom: since the coupling becomes weak at short distances, the limit is expected to be under control.
This is what mathematicians are trying to figure out. A tempting route to a "well-defined" theory is to start from the definition on a lattice, take the continuum limit, and show that this limit exists, satisfies the axioms, is not trivial, and really behaves like Yang-Mills (for instance, it should show asymptotic freedom at short distances).
Bonus question: once you've done that, can you prove there is a mass gap? That is a minimum mass that the glueballs (or their equivalent) must have, leading to finite-range interactions only? In mathematical terms: every state other than the vacuum must have an energy at least equal to .
This is essentially what the Yang-Mills existence and mass gap problem is about. It is stated not only for SU(3), but for any so-called compact simple group (SU(2), SU(3), every SU(N), and a few others). And as you can see, it has two steps: first prove that a rigorous theory exists, second prove that it has a mass gap.
The recent OpenAI claim is only about the first half, the existence. Let's try to understand what they did (or attempted), and what the Yang-Mills group statement says about it.
The OpenAI construction
As I said, the favorite route to a well-defined theory of Yang-Mills is to start from the theory on a lattice (which is well defined) and show that its continuum limit can be rigorously taken. But it involves several challenges.
First, we need to show that the limit exists, and that the "box to infinity" limit exists as well. And by "exists", we mean that the result satisfies the axioms, Wightman or Osterwalder–Schrader (the Millennium statement accepts any framework at least as strong as those).
Then we need to show that the theory we obtain this way is "non-trivial". There is indeed a risk that taking the limit obliterates the relevant physics and leads to a trivial free theory: this is what is believed to happen with QED on a lattice, by the way.
And finally, we need to show that what we obtain does not depend on arbitrary choices made during the construction. There are actually two different issues here. The first one is universality: would a different lattice (a different shape, a different way of writing the theory on the grid, or even a different sequence of spacings a) lead to the same theory? The second one is the uniqueness of the large-box limit: even with a fixed lattice, do all the ways of letting the box grow lead to the same result?
So what did OpenAI achieve, at least according to the most recent statement by the Yang-Mills group? (The group is chaired by David Gross, whom I mentioned earlier)
(1) The limit exists, for any compact simple group, and the coupling behaves exactly as asymptotic freedom predicts.
(2) The box-to-infinity limit exists... but only along a so-called "subsequence". To understand this, think about the sequence 1, −1, 1, −1, ... It doesn't converge, but if you only keep every other term, it does. The problem is that different subsequences can lead to different limits (here 1 and −1). The proof can't rule out that something similar happens here, even though nobody expects it.
(3) The resulting theory satisfies the Wightman axioms. (More precisely, the limit might a priori be a mixture of several "phases", and each of them is shown to give a proper quantum field theory.)
(4) The resulting theory is non-trivial: it is not a free theory in disguise. Nice detail: the proof uses a typically quantum effect. Classical Yang-Mills has no scale at all, but in the quantum theory, the running of the coupling breaks this scale symmetry, leaving a precise fingerprint in the correlation functions that no free theory can reproduce.
(5) ...but there is no proof of universality, nor of the uniqueness of the large-box limit.
(6) And of course, nothing about the mass gap: the assessment explicitly says that the construction provides no evidence for or against it.
Two more things worth knowing. The reviewers write that they don't fully understand the proof yet, and that their assessment is based on the Lean code only, not on the manuscript. And OpenAI also claims an existence proof for QCD itself (Yang-Mills plus quarks), which has not been released yet.
Of course, things will evolve as the Yang-Mills group digests the proof (both the manuscript and the Lean code). But as of today, what is confirmed seems to be a good portion of the existence problem... but remember that this is only half of the Millennium Prize! That being said, things will likely evolve very quickly in the near future (see this recent statement from the group).
So, does all this matter for particle accelerators? Not directly: physicists will keep using the perturbative expansion and lattice simulations, and their predictions won't change. What a proof changes is our confidence that the theory behind the strong force is a consistent mathematical object, and not just a very successful recipe. And maybe, one day, the tools developed along the way will help us understand that mysterious mass gap.
