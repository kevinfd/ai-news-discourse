# Can SpaceX catch its Starship?

Scientific American · Lee Billings · 2026-11-13

SpaceX’s Starship, the largest and most ambitious rocket ever built, is embarking on its most consequential test yet.
Elon Musk says the enormous two-stage vehicle will someday carry humans to the moon and Mars, but more immediately, Starship is central to the company’s plan to build out its Starlink satellite Internet constellation (not to mention its aim to pack a million data center satellites into orbit around Earth). But first SpaceX must show Starship can deliver on its most transformative and critical promise: full reusability.
That’s why today’s Flight 15 is so consequential. The Starship orbiter—officially named “Ship 42”—lifted off atop its Super Heavy booster at 5:47 EST from the company’s Starbase site in South Texas. This test flight has a deceptively simple goal: getting Ship 42 into orbit and then returning both it and the booster back to Earth intact and essentially ready to fly again.

What that will involve is the booster and orbiter separately reentering Earth’s atmosphere and flying back to Starbase, where the launch tower, fitted with enormous mechanical arms, will be waiting to catch them. SpaceX has previously shown it can pluck the Super Heavy from midair in previous tests. But Flight 15 will be the first attempt to catch the orbiter itself, after it has spent nearly a full day in space and circled Earth some 14 times.
If both recoveries succeed, SpaceX will have achieved a major milestone toward its goal of a fully reusable orbital rocket. On Friday afternoon, just hours ahead of the flight, the Federal Aviation Administration issued the necessary launch license.
One Flight, Not Quite Two Catches
The mission began with the ignition of the 33 engines on Starship’s Super Heavy booster, which propelled the spacecraft toward orbit before the two vehicles separated. The booster was then expected to return to Starbase about six and a half minutes after liftoff for a precision catch by the tower’s awaiting mechanical arms, but instead diverted into an offshore splashdown after trouble with some of its engines.
Meanwhile Starship itself continued into orbit to deploy 26 next-generation satellites for Starlink, SpaceX’s fast-growing Internet business. Once it’s fully operational, Starship will be able to loft Starlink satellites in far larger and more frequent batches than the firm’s retiring workhorse rocket, the Falcon 9. That will pave the way for the company’s plans to supplement Starlink with the launch of huge numbers of orbital data centers for artificial intelligence. If all goes to plan, then, about 22 hours later, Starship will attempt its own homecoming back to Starbase, returning to Earth around 4:15 P.M. EST on November 14.
Even if were a flawless recovery of both stages, the real prize remains untested: SpaceX must still show that it can rapidly turn around both the booster and orbiter for a fast return to flight. That would put the company well on its way to fulfilling Musk’s projection of roughly one Starship launch per week in the first half of 2027.
Flight 15 follows Flight 14, which saw Starship successfully reach orbit and deploy a batch of operational Starlink satellites. But that previous mission ended early because of an engine issue on Starship, leading SpaceX to bring the vehicle down in the Pacific Ocean. (The Super Heavy booster splashed down as planned in the Gulf of Mexico.) Besides demonstrating a much more audacious recovery, Flight 15 should also be Starship’s longest-yet sojourn in orbit.
A Moonshot in Waiting
The test is essential prep for Starship to meet its obligations to NASA’s Artemis program, which seeks to return U.S. astronauts to the moon before the end of 2028. SpaceX is developing a modified Human Landing System (HLS) version of Starship to potentially serve as a lunar lander, transporting crews between lunar orbit and the moon’s surface.
Before it can ferry humans on those far-flung voyages, though, Starship has to demonstrate key capabilities closer to home. During NASA’s Artemis III mission, targeted for next summer, Starship is set to launch to rendezvous and dock in low-Earth orbit with a NASA Orion spacecraft carrying four astronauts; SpaceX’s rival Blue Origin is also part of that mission, and a test version of that company’s Blue Moon Mark 2 lander will also link up with Orion.
Future Starship test flights will also need to solve the challenge of in-space refueling because, for the heavy HLS, a round trip to the moon requires more fuel than a single Starship can carry into orbit.
SpaceX’s ambitions are unfolding against a changing political landscape as well, particularly in Texas, where the company’s Starbase facility is based. The state’s governor-elect Gina Hinojosa campaigned against energy-intensive data centers and has pledged to pause new approvals until stronger protections are in place.
The timing is awkward for SpaceX: the company has just disclosed plans for a proposed $24-billion gas-fired power plant and supercomputing complex at Starbase and is seeking local tax incentives for the project.
For now, despite Flight 15’s wave-off of a Super Heavy booster catch, the more pressing question is whether Starship can do something no orbital-class rocket has done before: come home, and be caught in one piece.
