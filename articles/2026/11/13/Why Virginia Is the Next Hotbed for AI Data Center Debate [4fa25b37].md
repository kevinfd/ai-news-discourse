# Why Virginia Is the Next Hotbed for AI Data Center Debate

Newsweek · Lauren Giella · 2026-11-13

Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger laid out her 2027 legislative agenda with the commonwealth's top Democrats this week, and her proposals included taking on data centers and building on a 2026 executive order that requires increased transparency from developers, environmental protections and job creation for Virginians.
The agenda specifically bans nondisclosure agreements that would prevent data center developers from silencing members of local staff, board supervisors, planning commissions or zoning boards. It also allows residents to have a bigger voice in what gets developed in their communities by requiring local approval for any data center using more than 25 megawatts of power, regardless of any prior approval.
As state governments across the country review their relationships with data centers, Spanberger has also pledged to enact stronger enforcement and regulation to protect natural resources and protect workers' salaries from AI tools that would set lower wages based on surveillance data and personal assumptions.

"In the years before I took office, data centers came to Virginia and the Commonwealth did not have a clear or coordinated plan to address their impacts on Virginians—on their electric bills, their water, their land, their air, or their quality of life," Spanberger said in September, when issuing an executive order that outlined her new Data Center Accountability Framework.
"That changes today. I have said many times that Virginia should be leading the nation in setting the highest standards for this industry. And now, I believe we are doing just that," she continued.
According to Pew Research Center, Virginia has the highest number of active and planned AI data centers in the country, giving places such as Loudoun County in the northern part of the state the nickname "Data Center Alley." There are 398 active data centers, with nearly 300 more planned. But in recent years, land scarcity, environmental impacts and community pushback against costs have prompted a new era that aligns with the agenda for the Democratically controlled state government.
The Data Center Oversight Act, a federal bill that Democratic U.S. Representative Eugene Simon Vindman of Virginia recently introduced, would also require data center operators to disclose ownership, water and energy usage and costs that could be passed along to utility customers or risk up to a $1 million fine per day for failed compliance. Spanberger's September executive order also seeks to increase regulations on the AI industry in Virginia and set the standard "for what responsible data center development means."
Striking the Right Regulatory Balance
Joe Luppino-Esposito is the federal policy director of Pacific Legal Foundation and a resident in Loudoun County. As he understands both the benefits and negative impacts of sharing a ZIP code with a data center, he believes the best regulatory strategies will strike a balance between economic growth and community protections.
While residents have raised valid concerns about noise, pollution, land use and rising utility costs, Luppino-Esposito said some complaints fall under the "shaking your fist at a cloud" grievances rather than concrete issues that can be improved upon.
"I think that's really the question that the government has now: Are they going to be doing regulations that are just slowing [data centers] down for slowing them down's sake, or is there really something they're doing that's improving the life of residents?" he told Newsweek.
Virginia’s Data Center History
Virginia has long-been a hotbed for data center development. It began almost 30 years ago when America Online, or AOL, moved to Ashburn in 1997. That move opened pathways for other internet companies—such as UUNet/WorldCom and MAE-East—to plant roots in the area. Favorable tax policies dating back to 2010 also incentivize data center development, reducing operational costs and allowing for more growth through at least 2035.
But as Mital Gandhi, a Loudoun County resident and the president of Community Digital Technologies, told Newsweek: "Virginia didn't win data centers with the tax break. They won them with fiber." The proximity to the nation's capital brought high demand for fast, secure and reliable data processing for government agencies and contractors that laid the foundation for a tech infrastructure that could support development.
Gandhi's company works with people living near data centers to get them direct economic benefits from industrial development in their neighborhoods.
Virginia's Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission (JLARC) said in a 2024 report that data centers generated significant revenues for communities that house them, creating an estimated 74,000 jobs, $5.5 billion in labor income and $9.1 billion in Virginia's annual revenue. According to the JLARC, northern Virginia accounts for about 13 percent of global operational data center capacity and about a fourth of capacity across the Americas.
Former Governor Glenn Youngkin, who left office in January, was a champion for AI development in the state, signing an executive order to integrate AI across government agencies, schools and law enforcement and enacting a generative AI tool to streamline state regulatory operations. He also oversaw a $9 million investment from Google to fund the expansion of data centers to support a growing AI infrastructure and vetoed a 2025 bill that would have enacted greater oversight on high-risk AI systems.
According to state data, by the end of 2025, those tax breaks cost Virginia almost $2 billion. In that time, the state gained only 1,610 new jobs. Early this year, Spanberger enacted a $600 million annual tax on data centers' electricity use. The tax revenue is set to fund other budgetary costs and service improvements across the state.
The Future of Data Center Regulation
As the debate about how to best regulate data center continues across the country, Gandhi said state governments must keep their promise to govern for their constituents, and that data centers must keep their promises to be good neighbors.
For that to happen, the voices of those directly affected cannot be forgotten, he added.
"They need to have a specific focus on the immediate neighbors that house these data centers—that's what's missing, and that's what I'm hoping 2027 will fix, and other states will fix as well," Gandhi said.
This means governments have to make sure the communities surrounding data centers not only feel heard but also see some economic benefit from the tax breaks and other industrial incentives.
Gandhi continued, "That's what our entire premise of our business is: Take care of the neighbors right around the data centers, because when you do that, you're going to turn folks who don't want to live near data centers into people who want to live next to data centers."
