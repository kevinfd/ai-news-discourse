# Could AI reduce shipping costs and grocery bills?

Fox News · Kurt Knutsson, CyberGuy Report · 2026-11-13

You walk into the grocery store and notice that some of your favorite products cost more than they used to. Then you order something online and discover the delivery fee has gone up, too. It's frustrating, especially when it feels like you're paying more for the same things. Now, here's something you probably haven't considered. Part of what you pay for everyday products can be tied to delivery trucks sitting in long lines outside warehouses. Those trucks can burn expensive diesel while drivers wait to check in. Meanwhile, transportation companies face higher operating expenses that can eventually find their way into retail prices.
According to the most recent update from the U.S. Energy Information Administration, the national average diesel price was $6.13 per gallon for the week of Nov. 9. That's still a significant expense for businesses moving products across the country. But what if artificial intelligence could help eliminate some of that waste?
A company called EAIGLE says its AI-powered camera technology can reduce truck check-ins at distribution centers from roughly 10 minutes to under 30 seconds. That could save trucking companies money. And if those savings spread across supply chains, they could potentially help reduce some of the cost pressures behind your grocery bill.

Why expensive diesel can make your groceries cost more
You may never fill your car with diesel. However, chances are that much of the food in your kitchen traveled on a diesel-powered truck. Fresh produce often travels hundreds of miles before reaching your neighborhood supermarket. Many grocery products also move through distribution centers, where trucks unload incoming merchandise before stores receive their deliveries. Each trip costs money. When diesel prices rise, transportation companies often add fuel surcharges or increase shipping rates to cover the additional expense.
Those costs can eventually reach the companies selling products to you. In fact, a USDA-supported study examined how diesel prices and truck-driver availability affect food transportation. The researchers found that higher transportation costs generally increased food prices in the scenarios they studied. Potatoes were especially sensitive to changes in transportation expenses, partly because they're often shipped long distances from growing regions to major markets. So, while fuel prices are only one factor behind grocery inflation, they can have a real impact on what shoppers pay. And here's where AI could help.
How AI could reduce shipping costs before trucks leave the warehouse
Think about driving through an E-ZPass lane. Instead of stopping at a toll booth, technology handles the payment while traffic keeps moving. EAIGLE wants to bring a similar experience to warehouse gates. At many distribution centers, truck drivers still go through manual check-ins. Security personnel may need to review shipping documents and verify that the right truck has arrived for the right load.
During busy periods, those checks can create long lines. And when drivers leave their engines running, they're consuming fuel before they've even reached the loading dock. EAIGLE uses artificial intelligence and existing security cameras to automate much of that process. Its Automated Vehicle Access Control system, known as AVAC, identifies arriving vehicles and compares information against facility records. The company says its computer vision technology can capture more than 25 truck and trailer data points in under 30 seconds. That includes license plate numbers and other identifying details that help determine whether a vehicle should enter the property.
When the information matches, an automated gate can allow the driver to proceed. If something looks suspicious, the system can flag the vehicle for additional review. EAIGLE says this reduces a process that typically takes around 10 minutes to less than 30 seconds. Now, saving a few minutes might not sound like much. But consider a major distribution center processing hundreds of trucks every day. Reducing repeated delays could help companies use less fuel and avoid unnecessary operating expenses.
Could faster shipping mean lower grocery prices?
Here's the question I think is worth asking. If artificial intelligence helps companies spend less money moving products, could some of those savings reach shoppers? Potentially, yes. But there's an important catch. Transportation costs are built into the economics of getting products onto store shelves. If a grocery chain can reduce those expenses, it may have more room to hold prices steady when other costs increase. The same idea applies to online shopping. Shipping companies often include fuel surcharges in their rates, which can affect what retailers charge for delivery.
Reducing wasted fuel could help offset some of that expense. However, there's currently no evidence that EAIGLE's technology has directly lowered grocery prices or delivery fees for shoppers. Retailers could use the savings to offset other expenses or improve their profits. And because freight-yard check-ins account for only a small part of overall transportation costs, any savings would need to add up across many facilities to have a noticeable impact on what families pay. Still, with shipping expenses affecting so many products, it's an area worth watching.
Could AI also help prevent late deliveries and empty shelves?
Price is one concern. But what about finding the products you actually want? We've all gone shopping for something specific, only to discover that the store is out of stock. And there's nothing particularly satisfying about tracking an online order that seems stuck somewhere between a warehouse and your front door. Distribution delays can contribute to those frustrations.
If trucks spend less time waiting at warehouse gates, facilities may be able to process incoming shipments more efficiently. That could help retailers keep merchandise moving through their networks. For example, a grocery distribution center that receives deliveries more smoothly may have an easier time keeping scheduled shipments moving toward individual stores.
However, faster check-ins alone won't guarantee fuller shelves or earlier deliveries. Trucks can still encounter traffic delays, and warehouses may face shortages or loading dock congestion. The potential benefit comes when companies reduce delays throughout the entire shipping process.
DELIVERY DRONES COULD BE WAITING BEFORE YOU EVEN ORDER
EAIGLE says its AI technology could save $726,000 annually
EAIGLE estimates that a facility using its technology could save approximately $726,000 annually through improvements in gate operations and other efficiencies. The company also estimates that a facility processing around 200 trucks daily could eliminate approximately 110,000 pounds of carbon dioxide emissions each year by reducing unnecessary idling. That's roughly 55 U.S. tons of carbon dioxide. These are company-provided estimates, and actual results would depend on the facility and its operations.
AI may also help protect products from cargo theft
There's another expense that can affect the supply chain. Cargo theft. Criminals sometimes pose as legitimate transportation companies to collect shipments they have no authority to take. According to a June 2026 FBI warning, estimated cargo theft losses across the United States and Canada reached nearly $725 million in 2025. That's an enormous amount of stolen merchandise.
EAIGLE says its system can help identify suspicious pickups by comparing truck information with shipping records. If a vehicle arrives with incorrect identification or paperwork, the software can flag the problem. The company claims its technology can reduce theft and fraud exposure by more than 50%. Preventing stolen shipments could help retailers avoid some expensive losses. It may also reduce disruptions when merchandise disappears before reaching its destination.
Walmart and Loblaw are bringing AI into distribution centers
According to EAIGLE, its technology is already being used in major North American distribution facilities, including operations associated with Walmart. Meanwhile, Loblaw Companies Limited, one of Canada's largest grocery and pharmacy retailers, is expanding its relationship with EAIGLE. In a September 2026 announcement, Loblaw confirmed plans to expand the company's AI-powered gate technology across multiple distribution centers. The retailer says early results showed improvements in processing times and data accuracy. The technology also helped improve the experience for truck drivers arriving at its facilities.
Wael Yehia, Loblaw's vice president of national transportation, explained that the company is looking for practical ways to move products more efficiently through its distribution network. That's worth paying attention to because grocery retailers handle enormous volumes of merchandise. Improvements at busy distribution centers could help make those operations more reliable.
What this means to you
Here's why I think this technology is worth following, even if you never visit a freight yard. Every product that reaches your home has a transportation story behind it. Fuel costs affect that journey, and inefficiencies can add expenses long before a retailer puts a price tag on an item. AI systems like EAIGLE's could help transportation companies reduce some of those costs. Faster gate operations may also help retailers keep deliveries on schedule, which could improve product availability. For shoppers, the biggest potential benefit is relief from rising prices. If companies can operate more efficiently, they may be better positioned to absorb transportation costs instead of passing every increase along.
Kurt's key takeaways
I think one of the most interesting things about AI right now is where it's starting to show up. We hear so much about robots taking over jobs in warehouses and trucks that drive themselves. But here's an example of AI tackling something as simple as a long line of trucks waiting to enter a warehouse. And when you start connecting those delays to fuel expenses and the cost of shipping everyday products, you can see why this technology has potential. What I like about EAIGLE's approach is that companies can use cameras they may already have installed. They don't necessarily need a fleet of new trucks or expensive changes to their facilities to start improving operations. For now, I see this as a promising example of AI addressing a real supply chain problem. The next step is proving that the improvements go beyond the warehouse gate. And if those savings eventually help keep grocery prices or delivery fees from climbing even higher, that's something we can all appreciate.
