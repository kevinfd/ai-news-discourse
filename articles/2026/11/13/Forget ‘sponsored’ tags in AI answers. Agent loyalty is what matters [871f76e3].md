# Forget ‘sponsored’ tags in AI answers. Agent loyalty is what matters

Fast Company · Pete Pachal · 2026-11-13

Labels are an SEO-era fix for a GEO problem. As AI agents start taking a cut of what they sell you, the bigger risk is a sketchy agent.
BY Pete Pachal
Pete Pachal is a journalist and the creator of Media Copilot, a newsletter and podcast that examines how AI is changing media, journalism, and the news.
No one loves ads, but generally people will tolerate them if it means getting something free or at a lower price. What people won’t forgive? Being lied to.
A study looking at how consumers see native ads on news sites—which look similar to editorial content, just with labels—found that fewer than 10% of consumers could identify them as advertising. And when people find out after the fact, they don’t take it well. In a Contently survey, two-thirds of readers said they felt deceived when they realized an article or video was sponsored by a brand.
That’s why there’s a whole set of regulations and standards around advertising disclosures that are meant to protect consumers. Now that AI is slowly becoming a central part of our media ecosystem, it’s unclear to what extent those disclosures should carry over into AI answers. After all, if the public is increasingly using AI to shop (47% of U.S. shoppers who use AI started their last product search with AI, according to an impact.com survey, and Meta says shopping is the most common use of its new personal AI agent, Muse), it feels intuitive that the user should see plainly the commercial interests of the sites that informed any answer.
A reliable label in AI sounds like a good idea, but it gets complicated quickly. For starters, the label might cause confusion that the AI platform itself was paid rather than the original publisher. In the case of ChatGPT that would potentially conflict with its own ads, which are separate from the answer and labeled as sponsored. OpenAI’s answer to the disclosure question, so far, is the citation link: since launching ChatGPT search, it has included links to sources in answers so users can check them for themselves.
All of this gets at the fundamental question of judgment, and which parts of judging a source users have handed over to AI, consciously or unconsciously, simply by using it. When you ask AI to come up with, say, recommendations for air fryers, it’s more like hiring a researcher, not looking at a billboard. In fact, some people turn to AI precisely to get away from the billboards: 24% of the shoppers in the impact.com survey said they use AI to avoid sponsored results and ads. You trust it to hoover up all the information it can find about the question, weigh all the factors—including whether or not any sources are sponsored—and give you the best possible answer.
How important is a label at that point? It’d be silly to ask a researcher to stamp every brochure they look at, and pass those brochures over to you in a pile. If AI can solve some of humanity’s most intractable math problems, it can probably reliably weigh the commercial intent of a brand’s FAQ page. In fact, you could argue a label in the answer might cause the reader to overscrutinize an answer the AI has already deemed the most useful possible.
If we’re considering disclosures, we need to talk about affiliate content, and AI’s outsize reliance on it. As you might imagine, for any query about buying decisions, AI seeks out product recommendations. Those articles are often supported by affiliate revenue, meaning the site gets a fee when the reader takes action, typically a purchase. Legitimate sites that monetize via affiliate disclose that fact on any page with affiliate links; indeed, FTC guidelines require that they do so.
