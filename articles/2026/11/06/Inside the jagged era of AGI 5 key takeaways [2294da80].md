# Inside the jagged era of AGI: 5 key takeaways

Fast Company · Next Big Idea Club · 2026-11-06

Below, Kevin Roose shares five key insights from his new book, The AGI Chronicles: The Inside Story of the Race to Create an Artificial Superintelligence.
Roose is an award-winning technology journalist, bestselling author, and cofounder of Machine Gods Media. He previously spent a decade as a technology columnist for The New York Times, where he co-created and cohosted the popular podcast Hard Fork. He now cohosts Machine Gods, an AI-focused podcast produced in partnership with NPR.
What’s the Big Idea?
For years, the world’s leading AI researchers have raced to build a technology that many of them fear. But the finish line keeps moving as AI becomes more capable. The truth is, we’re already living in the early, imperfect years of artificial general intelligence, and our institutions are dangerously unprepared for what comes next.
Listen to the audio version of this Book Bite—read by Roose himself—in the Next Big Idea app, or buy the book.
1. The people most afraid of AI are the ones building it.
This is, in some sense, the founding paradox of the whole AGI story. You’d assume the people building AI are true believers who expect it will do amazing things, like cure cancer and end poverty. And some of them are. But a surprising number of key figures in this race got into AI because they were terrified. They read the arguments about what a superintelligent machine might do to humanity, took them seriously, and concluded that if this thing was going to be built no matter what, it had better be built by people who were scared of it and would take the appropriate precautions.
Ultimately, I came to think of the AI world as having three tribes. There are the scientists, who see AGI as the ultimate intellectual challenge and want to use it to discover new drugs and cure disease. There are the seekers, who see it as an evolutionary turning point for our species. And then there are the survivalists, who think that if this technology isn’t built by the good guys, then the bad guys will do it instead.
The trouble is that getting there first is what everyone is trying to do. And they all end up concluding that the only way to move forward responsibly is to accelerate. Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, left OpenAI partly out of concern that it was moving too fast, and then went on to build a competitor that had to move just as fast to stay ahead. He’s living inside the paradox, and so are many of the people in this book.
