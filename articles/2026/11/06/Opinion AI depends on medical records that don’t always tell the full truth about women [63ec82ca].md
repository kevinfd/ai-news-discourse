# Opinion: AI depends on medical records that don’t always tell the full truth about women

STAT News · Annemarie G. Hirsch and Jennifer Silva · 2026-11-06

In September, the Gallup-Pivotal Women’s Health Survey reported that 34% of U.S. women strongly or somewhat agreed that a health care provider minimized or dismissed their symptoms in the past five years. Thirty-three percent reported being misdiagnosed or unable to get a diagnosis, compared with 21% of men reporting these experiences.
Medical records tell a different story. An epidemiologist and a sociologist, we recently released a study where we paired electronic medical records with detailed interviews with 87 women. We talked to these women about their pain and illnesses, work, family, poverty, violence, and encounters with the health care system.
Fifty-three of the women described a trust-breaking encounter with a health system, but only seven of them had medical records that documented mistrust. Thirty-two women reported experiencing a misdiagnosis, but misdiagnosis was only documented for eight of these women. Thirty women reported that they felt that clinicians treated them with disrespect, but this was not documented in the records anywhere.
Consider Kate (not a real name).
For more than a decade, Kate lived with debilitating pelvic pain. Her doctors repeatedly attributed her symptoms to obesity. Her medical record reflected these assumptions.
“She can’t control what she is eating.”
“She admits to poor eating habits.”
When Kate underwent bariatric surgery and lost a significant amount of weight, her clinical notes became optimistic.
“Ran ½ marathon in October!!!!”
However, her symptoms persisted. Medical records pivoted from linking symptoms from obesity to mental health factors.
“Gastrointestinal malfunctions arising from mental factors.”
The turning point came only after Kate attended an appointment with her new husband. In the clinical notes, the provider documented Kate’s husband’s concerns:
“Painful intercourse for the past year.”
“Pain makes pt stop having intercourse.”
“Pt’s husband had very good questions.”
For the first time in 10 years, doctors conducted the exams needed to finally diagnose Kate with endometriosis.
Kate’s medical record accurately documented years of conversations about weight and mental health. What it failed to capture was equally important: the cost of years spent with an undiagnosed disease, the frustration of not being believed, and the erosion of trust that occurs when symptoms are repeatedly explained away rather than investigated. Kate told us, “If I was tired, it was because I was overweight. If I had cramps, it was because I was overweight. Migraines, I was overweight. … They’re not hearing what you’re saying, and that compounded the issues that were going on.”
We often think of medical records as objective accounts of a patient’s health. They are not. Medical records are narratives, shaped by the questions clinicians ask and the assumptions they bring into the room. Medical record data reflect clinical decisions as well as biology. The language clinicians use and the details they choose to record can reinforce stereotypes that follow patients from one encounter to the next, influencing how future clinicians interpret symptoms, assess credibility, and make treatment decisions.
Medical record data also reflect what patients feel comfortable disclosing. What women share with their doctors depends on how much they trust them and how safe they feel. When people fear judgment, dismissal, or not being believed, important parts of their stories may remain unspoken, and thus, undocumented.
This fear may why 53 women in our data described a trust-breaking clinical encounter in their interview, while only seven of their medical records noted their mistrust. This is particularly a problem for women, who feel the pressure to be pleasant and pliant with doctors for fear of not being taken seriously.
Across the country, clinics and hospitals are adopting AI scribes that transcribe recorded interactions between doctor and patient and produce a clinical note. AI tools can be used to search and synthesize medical records to help with diagnostics and treatment plans, some with great success.
However, these tools are only as good as the data in the medical record. If a woman’s symptoms are filtered through assumptions about her weight or mental health, and the true story of her pain is omitted, AI may learn and reproduce how the woman is framed in the record.
For AI to achieve its full potential in women’s health, medical records need to tell the complete story. Health care settings must be safe spaces where women can tell their stories; clinicians need to listen to women, even when their experiences do not fit clinical expectations; and clinicians need to document in the medical record when diagnoses are delayed or incorrect, without fear of being penalized.
Armed with these data, AI has the potential to do more than replicate biases of the past. AI could help identify patterns of missed diagnoses, reduce disparities in care, and ensure that more women are heard.
Annemarie G. Hirsch, Ph.D., M.P.H., and Jennifer Silva, Ph.D., are authors of “Seen But Not Heard: What Medical Records Don’t Tell Us About Women’s Lives.”
