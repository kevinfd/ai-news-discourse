# ​The Industrial Revolution Of Intelligence: AI Energy Infrastructure

Forbes · Bill Wong, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-06

Bill Wong - AI Research Fellow, Info-Tech Research Group.
In my previous article, we examined the emergence of a new data center asset, the AI factory, which is optimized to run AI workloads. Compared to traditional data centers, AI data center campuses require up to 10 times the total facility power capacity, driven by GPU server racks that consume 12 to 28 times more power per rack and thermal density that requires advanced liquid cooling to handle five times the heat generation.
This new industrial revolution is driving an unprecedented demand for power, transforming the utilities industry from a relatively stable and quiet industry to a growth sector. Nations and organizations alike will need to optimize their AI energy strategy in order to flourish during and after this revolution.
National AI Energy Infrastructure Strategies
After reviewing several national AI energy infrastructure strategies around the world, four distinct strategies stand out. These strategies will shape how nations are planning to build the required energy infrastructure for AI workloads.
• Sovereign Regulation-Driven: These jurisdictions introduce legislation to ensure that AI developers must fund their own power requirements, maintain the reliability of the grid and provide the supporting water infrastructure required for these data centers to prevent rising utility costs. In Canada, the country’s recently published AI strategy champions their grid’s 83% investment in clean power. Australia will require data center providers to generate as much energy as they consume. The EU has introduced a sovereign AI compute platform, EuroHPC JU, which aims to “develop innovative energy-efficient HPC software technologies tailored to exascale and post-exascale supercomputers.”
• Market-Driven: These countries rely on the private sector to drive the building of data centers and leverage easily accessible energy resources. The U.S. has begun by reinvesting into nuclear power plants and the use of small modular reactors. The UK has accelerated AI projects through its AI Growth Zones, fast track permits.
• Localization-Driven: Rather than adding additional workloads to the existing energy grids, some countries are locating new data centers near clean energy sources. Japan’s new collaboration strategy leverages nine designated green energy zones. South Korea’s new strategy directs the movement of 18.4 GW of data center workloads out of Seoul to regional nuclear and small modular reactor clusters.
• State-Driven: China’s government is planning to connect renewable energy resources from its western region to its computing hubs in its eastern region.
AI Energy Value Chain
In order to assess a nation or organization’s AI energy strategy, one must examine its energy value chain. This involves identifying the source of its energy, determining how power is generated, determining how power is managed and deployed, understanding the nation’s thermal management and understanding the sustainability strategy the nation or organization has implemented.
Energy Sources And Generation
Meaningful metrics in this area focus on the total volume, dependability and carbon footprint of the electricity required by the organization in question’s compute infrastructure. Countries and organizations using clean and renewable energy resources will be able to differentiate their energy infrastructure in a positive manner. On the other hand, countries leveraging fossil fuels, like natural gas, may have the short-term benefit of accessing low-cost energy resources, but they will also encounter long-term emissions problems, which expose them to carbon regulation and creates cost volatility.
Power Management And Grid Integration
Metrics in this area focus on an energy infrastructure’s capacity and its ability to connect, control and optimize high-density energy flow between the bulk electric grid (external) and its computing facilities (internal).
From a macro-grid point of view, which focuses outside of the data center, leaders can use ultra-high-voltage transmission lines, battery storage and AI-driven load-balancing software to absorb the violent swings in demand from GPU clusters, which can dramatically improve power management.
From a micro-grid standpoint, which focuses inside the data center, leaders have a few things to consider. Today’s AI racks demand 130 to 600 kW, and pushing that much power through legacy wiring can generate wasted heat and requires multiple inefficient conversion steps. Modern operators can implement ±400V or 800V DC systems and solid-state transformers to reduce internal heat loss and conversion steps.
Thermal Management And Environmental Stewardship
Legacy air cooling can no longer dissipate the heat generated by today’s AI factories. How a company handles this heat is becoming a competitive advantage for any organization running its own sovereign AI infrastructure. I’ve observed some leading operators switching to direct-to-chip liquid cooling in closed-loop systems, which can slash operational water consumption.
Embracing robust environmental stewardship can help nations and organizations keep their data center expansion efforts sustainable, minimizing ecological disruption while preserving critical local environmental resources. Beyond conservation, some advanced operators are turning thermal management into a revenue stream. By establishing facilities in colder climates (like Canada, the Nordics or Hokkaido, Japan) and capturing the 60- to 80-degree Celsius waste heat directly from server racks, those operators can sell that heat to municipal district heating networks.
Recommendations
Which AI energy infrastructure strategy is the best for a given organization will depend on several factors, and not all options are available for a given country or geographical location, especially those strategies that can leverage a country’s colder climate to reduce cooling costs.
Countries and organizations need to prioritize and align their energy infrastructure architecture with their nation’s sovereign AI strategy. For instance, sustainability initiatives will likely have the best success in countries like Canada and Nordic nations.
Finally, organizations need to learn how to address the current backlash from people regarding AI data centers near their communities. Builders and operators should be prepared to address questions and concerns surrounding the AI energy value chain they’re leveraging, along with its regional costs and benefits. Leaders should focus on sustainability, environmental impact and delivering an accountability framework for the operations of their proposed AI data center.
