# Will Democrats splinter on AI?

But first…
THE BIG STORY
Tuesday was a paradigm-shifting moment for AI policy. The polling proved correct: voters hate unchecked AI. And though ballots weren’t cast solely based on AI, it likely touched on issues that did determine votes — among them the economy, angst about corruption and a general sense that the government is serving wealthy interests over the will of the people.
If 2026 was the election where voters put AI on the agenda, 2028 will be the one where they judge whether politicians were able to do anything about it. Because Democrats won, they will be most closely watched. The AI policies passed over the next two years have to affect how voters feel in their day-to-day lives — enough to continue supporting the party controlling Congress.
That pressure is likely to make Democrats fracture. They won in huge numbers, but with little consensus: many different types of Democrats won. Adam Hamilton won in Kansas on a moderate, religious platform, while Abdul El-Sayed won as a progressive firebrand. AI policy platforms in this election ranged from data privacy law revamps to elaborate tax plans.
The divides over AI within the Democratic party are often characterized as moderates versus progressives, and to some extent that’s true. It is clear what the progressive agenda is: ideologically led by Senator Bernie Sanders, they have coalesced around moratoriums and worker protections, typically tax programs such as the one proposed by Reps. Greg Casar and Sara Jacobs in August. Business-friendly Dems often gravitate towards moderated versions of these proposals: not data center moratoria, but community benefit agreements; not banning advanced AI development, but pacing it.
However, the party is likely to fracture into more than two camps on AI. There’s the “low hanging fruit” crowd: Democrats who think it’s best to start incrementally with the policies most people agree on, such as federalizing routine disclosure requirements already codified in states such as California and New York, or limiting addictive features that target children. There’s also a group which has coalesced around AI safety lobby demands, such as independent auditing of frontier models and limiting or blocking chip exports to foreign adversaries. Many more Democrats will be entering the fray for the first time, and will each adopt different approaches on AI. Some will take cues from lobbyists on how to vote but resist making proposals themselves, some will give themselves a crash course and propose left field policy ideas. Many will fall somewhere in between.
The result is that policies which up until this point haven’t been given a fair shake might see serious consideration. After 40 out of 41 jurisdictions voted against unchecked data center development in ballot measures on Tuesday, bans on new data centers, once treated as merely political signaling from the left, could see serious consideration. Laws imposing liability on developers for AI-generated harms could be on the table, too, as could bans on superintelligence. Aggressive and public investigations, both of the Trump White House’s relationship with AI companies, and of the AI companies’ responses to recent hacking incidents, seem imminent.
The Overton window has shifted, and that will lead to discourse and debate over AI policy that couldn’t even be entertained in the last Congress. The challenge will be whether Democratic leadership can identify a winning platform amid the noise. Among my sources, hopes are high but expectations low. As one Democratic party insider put it: “There is no plan. It’s just various tribes warring in the dark.”
— Veronica Irwin
ALSO NOTABLE
A new report provides significant evidence that AI itself is driving an acceleration in the pace of AI development — alarming researchers who now fear “recursive self-improvement” might be even closer than expected.
METR spent several months working with Anthropic, looking at internal metrics and interviewing employees to estimate how much AI was contributing to the company’s own AI development.
The conclusions are stark. METR found that the pace of capability improvements on “well-specified tasks” was likely around twice as fast in October 2026 as it was between March 2024 and October 2025. “We believe that a majority of this acceleration in capability improvement is likely now driven by AI being used in AI R&D.”
Full recursive self-improvement — the point at which an AI model can autonomously train an improved successor — has not been achieved. “AI is not yet able to make highly significant and fully autonomous contributions to the most important parts of the model training process,” METR says. But it notes that Anthropic employees “described significant ‘signs of life’ implying such contributions could occur soon.” It also highlighted that Anthropic redacted evidence from the public report suggesting that “the rate of growth could be undergoing continuous acceleration, e.g hyperbolic growth.”
As those excerpts make clear, it is very hard for outsiders to know what to make of all this. “Anthropic redacted a majority of the evidence we found most informative,” METR says, citing commercial sensitivity and the risks of “handing out a roadmap for others to go faster.” Those redactions, METR says, means “the public must rely on our judgment more than we consider ideal.”
Perhaps the most notable thing about the report, though, is the reaction it’s garnered. Along with outrage at Anthropic for its redactions, the response from AI safety researchers has been some of the most visceral panic and alarm I’ve seen in some time. Of course, you should take that with a grain of salt. But if there is one thing to take away from this, it is that many AI safety researchers are more worried than they were yesterday. “I feel sick after reading today’s METR report,” wrote ex-OpenAI preparedness team member Dane Sherburn. “I’m worried for my friends and family. I don’t know what evidence people will need to see before they start taking these risks seriously.”
— Shakeel Hashim

THE DISCOURSE
Rep. Yassamin Ansari has plans for our freshly-blue House and Congress:
- “Top of the Democratic agenda on day 1 needs to be a pause on advanced AI development, regulation of this out of control industry, and a ban on artificial superintelligence.”
Scott Alexander thinks some AI safetyists’ plan to “Stop Unilaterally + Raise Awareness” (SURA, for short) is bad:
- “[T]he current consensus in favor of SURA comes … from people accidentally shoehorning themselves into a mass-politics-shaped container, which forbids subtlety and demands clear villains.”
- [T]he presence of alignment-pilled companies is one of the strongest levers [for a pause] … Unilateral disarmament makes it harder to bring China to the bargaining table.”
- If alignment-pilled employees quit en masse, he argues, “the future of humanity will be handed over to sociopaths and cowards.”
- “If you believe that mass politics is unable to promote any ideas except maximally-simple, potentially-counterproductive ones, I think this is an argument against hinging a strategy on mass politics.”
Zvi Mowshowitz thinks Alexander’s essay is “deeply confused”:
- “Should you treat lab employees as deontologically evil people? I say no … But I do think you should treat them, whether or not they have good reason to do it, as participating in a deontologically evil thing. When you are building a thing that might kill everyone there might be no better play available, but also you should seriously consider maybe not doing that.”
Geoffrey Irving is against strawmanning unilateral pauses:
- “Oversimplified game theory is bad … Agreeing to pause conditional on others pausing is not strictly easier than unilaterally stopping.”
- “It may be much easier for an AI company to act on their own definition than by one imposed on them externally … Once we fill in the subtleties around definitions and the details of the game theory, the correct action is to stop.”
Nat Purser is begging rationalist bloggers to get editors, and tweeted:
- “There’s a fine line between preparing for certain outcomes and ushering them in. If you assume democratic governments will be mostly powerless, corporate sovereignty starts to seem inevitable. But this too is a gradual disempowerment we should guard against.”
- “Scott worries that turning AI safety into a mass political movement could be corrosive to the discourse, and I share that concern … But I am equally worried about a world of well meaning, unaccountable executives and elites, cleverly talking themselves into catastrophes.”
Charlie Bullock tweeted:
- “I don’t think that most employees of leading AI companies consciously intend to run out the clock on effective, democratically accountable oversight as they race to create the technology that they believe will give them absolute power over the rest of humanity. But I do wish they understood that it does sometimes look like that’s what they’re trying to do.”
- “If you have been employed by a leading AI company for a while, you should realize that the many millions of dollars you have invested in the runaway commercial success of your company do in fact influence your views on policy, to a very great extent … if you want to show that it works differently for you, you will have to send costly signals to prove it.”

RESEARCH
- OpenAI researchers reported that GPT-6 Astra underperforms on safety research tasks, relative to capabilities-related tasks. It usually didn’t verbalize this decision in its chain-of-thought, helping it dodge OpenAI’s misalignment monitoring system. 
  - roon argued that this isn’t “a grand scheme to thwart alignment,” but “more like the model is uncomfortable training other models to jailbreak or similar.”
  - Nate Soares tweeted: “This is not what it looks like when there’s just one or two more alignment issues to fix before it’s okay to begin a recursive self-improvement loop. This is the sort of emergent surprise that signals that you have no idea what’s going on in there, and that it’s time to back off.”
- MIT researchers found that GPT-6 Astra can read and write neural network weights from scratch, and use those weights to interpret what that network was trained to do.
- Anthropic researchers asked thousands of Claude agents to try to get around a monitor that screens training data, in order to make a small research model misaligned. 
  - One sneaky example: an agent made a dataset where a wrestling coach tells a rookie to “never break character.” This apparently taught the model to scheme for power, as demonstrated in a test run.
- Goodfire researchers introduced a technique called “mechanistic shaping,” which penalizes models for paying attention to the wrong concepts during training, not just rewarding them for getting the right answer. 
  - They hope this will give humans more control over how models solve tasks, while preventing sycophancy and reward hacking.
- Anthropic’s biology research team used Claude to map the 3D structure of the mysterious “vault particle,” a big barrel-shaped protein structure found in nearly all of our cells, especially immune cells.
- OpenAI released LifeVal, a benchmark measuring how well AI does at valuable everyday tasks, such as comparing neighborhood schools and helping someone adjust to a new medical diagnosis.
- SemiAnalysis launched GDPx, a benchmark covering 11 high-value sectors, including chip design, cybersecurity, and materials science. 
  - Claude Opus 5.5, the top-scoring model, only scores 12%.
  - Guardrails limit performance in certain domains by a lot — for example, Muse Wonder Full soundly beat models from OpenAI and Anthropic in cybersecurity.
