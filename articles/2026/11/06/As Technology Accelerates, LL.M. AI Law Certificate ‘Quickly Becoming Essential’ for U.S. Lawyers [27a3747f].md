# As Technology Accelerates, LL.M. AI Law Certificate ‘Quickly Becoming Essential’ for U.S. Lawyers

Berkeley Law · 2026-11-06

The innovative program — the first of its kind at an American law school — is increasingly attracting students from domestic law firms eager to acclimate to the many ways AI is impacting the legal profession.
By Gwyneth K. Shaw
Berkeley Law’s AI program for Master of Laws (LL.M.) executive track students is offering lawyers practicing in the U.S. a flexible, up-to-the-minute way to gain the knowledge and skills needed to practice in the exploding sector.
“I wanted to do an LL.M. focused on AI law, and Berkeley offers the best program for that,” Thomas Leo LL.M. ’26 says. “As machine learning and related technologies change the legal landscape in a way unseen since the internet’s advent, I saw the LL.M. as a way to update my knowledge base and skillset. During law’s current transformation, I see such education as critical in this profession.”
The LL.M. Certificate in AI Law and Regulation, which began in summer 2025, is the first of its kind at an American law school and has attracted students from more than 40 countries. Increasingly, those students are coming from domestic law firms, eager to acclimate to the many ways AI is impacting the legal profession.
Built on the bedrock of Berkeley Law’s vaunted law and technology program, the AI and Regulation Certificate is open for applications from students interested in the LL.M. executive track program, which is completed over two summers or through an academic year of remote study with one summer on campus.
Applications for the summer 2027 cohort are open through Dec. 11 for the remote and summer option, and through March 15 for the two-summer track. Prospective students should apply by Nov. 16 for priority scholarship consideration.
“American legal institutions are intensely paying attention to AI right now,” Leo says. “From public regulation to private transactions to legal tech, many aspects of American law — as impacted by AI — are particularly important for U.S. JDs to understand.”
New frontiers
The program’s curriculum has added new courses each year, and the offerings range from The Law and Governance of AI to GenAI in Legal Practice to Corporate Strategy in the Age of AI and Geopolitics. In summer 2027, Professor Sean Farhang is offering Litigating With AI, another new class, to help students navigate how artificial intelligence is changing civil and criminal adjudication.
The program offers an annual lecture series to teach LL.M. executive track students how to use generative AI in their day-to-day legal practice, and guest speakers and resident experts come to present their projections for the future of the intersection of technology and law.
“Artificial intelligence and emerging technologies are transformative tools, but they are still tools,” Director of LL.M. Career Programs Rachel Zuraw says. “At Berkeley Law we train our students on how to understand these tools, employ them to their greatest advantage, and eventually improve on them.”
Former prosecutor Andrea Mauro LL.M. ’27 chose Berkeley Law’s program because of her interest in AI governance.
“I believe that ensuring AI systems are aligned with human values and accountable to the people they affect is one of the most urgent challenges of our time,” she says. “I chose the AI Certificate because effective regulation requires a solid foundational understanding of the technology itself.”
Mauro is an Atticus Fellowship recipient, a merit-based scholarship awarded to students whose work demonstrates a commitment to justice and public interest law.
“Alignment begins with understanding, because how we conceptualize AI shapes how much we trust it, whether we view it as neutral or biased, how we use it, what limits we place on it, and who we hold responsible when it causes harm,” she says. “Those views, in turn, drive our policy choices and our systems of accountability, so the law’s evolution depends largely on how critically we understand AI.
“Berkeley’s program offers exactly that foundation, along with the rare opportunity to learn directly from leading scholars and practitioners who are actively defining this field.”
Through coursework ranging from frontier model laws and the European Union AI Act to privacy regimes and sector-specific laws for biometric, health, financial, and children’s data, Mauro learned not only about emerging regulatory frameworks — but also how AI works, where it adds value, how it fails, how it can mislead, and when it might augment or replace human judgment.
“Additionally, the school offers access to a leading AI policy ecosystem and the broader Silicon Valley regulatory conversation, which is invaluable for both substantive learning and connections with leaders in the field,” she says.
Making connections
Both Leo, who moved into a corporate counsel job at Amazon halfway through his time in the program, and Mauro say Berkeley Law’s stellar faculty and students made the experience valuable and memorable.
“Some of the most important relationships I’ve developed are with my fellow students: a diverse group of experienced professionals from around the world, each bringing their own domain expertise, united by a desire to shape public and private governance of this emerging technology,” Mauro says. “Together, we sacrificed evenings, weekends, and time with family to build the understanding needed to guide this transition.”
Their learning, she adds, has moved between the technical and the regulatory: understanding how a system works, then asking how it should be governed.
They’ve watched a model invent a citation, then debated disclosure and accountability rules. They tested an algorithmic risk assessment tool — the kind now informing bail and sentencing decisions in U.S. courtrooms — then argued over what technical due process should require. They learned to “vibe code,” then debated a fundamental question: How will the next generation develop judgment if AI replaces the apprenticeship that once built it?
“I’ll leave with the foundation necessary to address these difficult questions, alongside a community of colleagues who share a commitment to ensuring that AI is deployed responsibly and in service of society,” Mauro says.
The program’s graduates, whether they’re from the U.S. or abroad, are seeing big dividends from their LL.M. education. Otilia Phiri ’25, for example, created a new role at Microsoft focused on supporting the datacenter infrastructure that powers AI and cloud-based information management across Europe, Middle East, and Africa. Anthony Shallat ’25 pivoted to a new role as the product subject matter expert at Ironclad, an AI-powered contract lifecycle management platform — and published an article in the The Ohio State Technology Law Journal.
“Berkeley Law doesn’t teach our students with AI; instead, we equip our students with the skills and knowledge to navigate it,” Zuraw adds. “After all, AI isn’t going to take over lawyer jobs — it’s the lawyers who know how to use AI who are going to take over.”
Mauro says she’d recommend the program to U.S.-trained lawyers without hesitation.
“Many law schools are only beginning to integrate AI courses into their curricula, and few practicing attorneys have had the chance to study both how AI systems actually work and the existing and emerging laws that govern them,” she says. “For lawyers already grounded in U.S. law, Berkeley’s program offers the technical fluency to ask the right questions of AI systems, and a global perspective drawn from classmates practicing under vastly different regulatory regimes.
“Whether you practice in a firm, in-house, in government, or in the nonprofit sector, that combination is quickly becoming essential.”
