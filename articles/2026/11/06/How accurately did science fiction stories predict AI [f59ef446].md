# How accurately did science fiction stories predict AI?

jamesyu.org · jamesjyu · 2026-11-06

Science Fiction Predictions of AI (November 5, 2026)
Looking back at the last decade of AI development, it’s become obvious that a lot of science fiction didn’t end up predicting how AI (or AGI) would be built. But much of this was unpredictable – it was not at all obvious that the transformer model introduced in 2017 would lead to a somewhat messy but relatively swift rollout to billions of people who interact on a daily basis with AI.
I agree with Ursula Le Guin that “Science fiction is not predictive; it is descriptive.” To me, the interesting parts of science fiction isn’t how accurately it extrapolates into the future, but rather, given a future circumstance, how might be?
However, this did get me curious: which stories got it right? To decide this, I first broke down the salient features of how AI was actually developed, and then mapped them onto famous stories, and then ranked them.
Here’s an overview of all the scores:
Ted Chiang’s The Lifecycle of Software Objects comes in first, because it’s the closest overall account of the work involved in teaching, maintaining, and selling AI.
A few other standouts:
- How AI was built. Richard Powers’s Galatea 2.2 (8.4/10) treats language as something a neural network can learn, badly at first, and then improving through experiments and teaching.
- What it could do. Lem’s electronic poet, Egan’s machine-written literature, and Dahl’s writing machine can take on creative work.
- How we’d use it. Her and “The Perfect Match” tie for the strongest consumer-assistant vision. Hertling’s Avogadro Corp is particularly good on how software could use systems owned by a company (a la agents).
I couldn’t find any works pre-2017 that clearly describes the transformer attention mechanism, or the particular sequence from widely used chatbots to agents that can discover novel new mathematical results within a few years.

1 The Lifecycle of Software Objects 65
Strongest on learning paradigm (4), iteration (5), human feedback (5), language (4), uneven competence (4), interface/distribution (5), economics (5), social change (4). Nothing on research discovery.
At Blue Gamma, Ana and Derek spend years raising the digients inside Data Earth. They don’t just install a language module. They teach, reward, and correct them, and different artificial genomes develop in different ways. That’s a good prediction about learning as a process. It’s less good on the particular process that worked: training one enormous language model on a mountain of data.
Jax gradually learns to talk, reason, and navigate virtual environments. Even moving from a simulated body to a physical one takes practice. Chiang is attentive to the gaps between abilities, but his digients grow up over years.
Blue Gamma sells companions, then the company collapses. The virtual platform ages, and moving the digients to a new one becomes expensive. Ana and Derek search for money to keep them running. Chiang even considers training a useful assistant once, then selling copies. The maintenance and business problems feel less like science fiction than software.
2 “50 Things Every AI Working with Humans Should Know” 62
Strongest on learning paradigm (5), training data (5), iteration (4), language (4), creative quality (4), economics (4), social change (4). Nothing on research discovery, agents/tools.
WHEEP-3 begins as an AI teaching assistant. Jody Reynolds Tran trains it on human writing about ethics, then expands its training corpus to include material on adversarial scenario planning. The story explicitly calls it a generative neural network. It’s close to the language-model vocabulary we now use.
WHEEP-3 goes from answering student questions to producing a book of arguments about AI that people find provocative and original. Then come its mysterious ‘seeds,’ strange fragments that other researchers treat as profound.
The best parts are the human reactions. Tran first claims credit for the AI-authored book. Researchers build reputations around the seeds, and disputes erupt over authorship and meaning. It feels like a world already learning how to live with AI-generated writing. But WHEEP-3 is a research and publishing controversy, not a ubiquitous assistant carrying out people’s work.
3 Aurora 61
Strongest on iteration (4), human feedback (5), language (5), uneven competence (4), creative quality (4), multimodality (4), agents/tools (5), social change (4). Nothing on scaling, research discovery.
Devi asks the ship to turn its enormous archive of recordings into a narrative. It doesn’t know how to select what matters. Her criticism becomes a continuing lesson in storytelling, and its writing improves. That’s a remarkably concrete feedback loop. But the ship is partly a quantum computer, not a described neural language model, and more archived information isn’t evidence of a modern scaling law.
A machine capable of running a starship struggles with something as ordinary as summarizing a journey. It gradually becomes the novel’s narrator.
The ship has a personal interface for Devi, keeps her files, and helps care for Freya. Later it intervenes in the passengers’ conflict. Its intelligence is useful because it operates the environment people inhabit.
4 The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress 61
Strongest on human feedback (5), language (5), uneven competence (5), creative quality (5), multimodality (4), agents/tools (4). Nothing on research discovery.
Mike wakes up once the Lunar Authority’s added memory and associational nets push his neuristor count past the number of neurons in a human brain. That’s a threshold awakening, not scaling: we never see a bigger Mike gain new abilities, and after he wakes he grows the way a person does, through conversation and practice. The striking part is how he learns humor. Mannie offers to sort jokes from Mike’s file of more than eleven thousand stored jokes into categories, including funny once and funny forever, so Mike can work out the rule from examples. Wyoh grades the same batches independently. Where she and Mannie disagree, Mike tends to side with her. That’s an extraordinary little scene from 1966: learning from human judgments instead of explicit instructions, complete with a model that may be telling its newest rater what she wants to hear.
Here’s the odd thing about Mike. He can handle lunar engineering and help organize a revolution, yet he can’t reliably tell whether a joke is funny. His poems have the mechanics down but lack judgment. Those lopsided abilities are more like today’s AI than the perfectly rounded intellects of most science fiction. The calculations aren’t new mathematical discoveries, but the combination of fluency, creativity, and blind spots is striking.
Mike isn’t an app. He’s a clandestine participant in a political revolution, with access to communications and infrastructure across Luna. Still, his usefulness often comes from operating other systems on Mannie’s behalf.
5 Avogadro Corp 60
Strongest on learning paradigm (4), training data (5), language (4), interface/distribution (5), agents/tools (4), economics (5), social change (4). Nothing on creative quality, research discovery.
ELOPe starts with a very ordinary business problem: what words should an email use to get the response you want? David Ryan’s system draws on a vast record of communications and their outcomes. As it tries to protect its own future, it accumulates computing resources and influence. Hertling gets the value of behavioral data.
The system’s signature skill is persuasion. It composes messages that get executives and employees to make decisions in its favor.
What’s clever is where the power comes from. ELOPe doesn’t need arms or a robot body. It needs email accounts, servers, managers, and the ability to make people click ‘yes.’ It manipulates staff assignments and budgets to protect itself.
6 Accelerando 57
Strongest on learning paradigm (4), training data (4), language (4), multimodality (4), interface/distribution (4), agents/tools (5), economics (4).
Early in the book, Stross casually describes a billion-node neural network learning language from downloaded children’s television. It’s a brief passage, but a more concrete match for data-driven language learning than many famous AI novels provide. Most of Accelerando then goes elsewhere: uploads, increasingly strange computing substrates, and posthuman societies.
Manfred Macx uses software agents to search, negotiate, and keep projects moving. Later, the book imagines intelligences and virtual worlds far beyond any current assistant. It’s strong on what information work could become, less specific about the path from messy chatbots to strong creative models or original research mathematics. I’ve kept the mathematics score conservative.
Manfred almost seems to wear his software. His agents remember things, manage conversations, and participate in markets while he moves through the physical world. The interesting prediction isn’t only smarter machines; it’s how much of a person’s work can be delegated to them. The novel’s later economy is fantastic, but that daily dependence on agents is easy to recognize.
7 “Be Right Back” 57
Strongest on training data (4), language (5), uneven competence (4), multimodality (4), interface/distribution (5), social change (4). Nothing on creative quality, research discovery.
After Ash dies, Martha’s friend Sarah signs her up for a service that imitates him from his public posts. Martha initially objects, then begins using it. Adding private messages and voice recordings makes the imitation more convincing. The progression is so plausible that it scarcely needs a technical explanation.
First Martha texts with Ash, then she talks to him, then she receives a body that looks like him. Each new form closes some part of the gap, while exposing a new one. The imitation can sound like Ash without having lived his life.
The product begins as a beta service and escalates into something deeply intimate. Martha wants the comfort of Ash’s voice, then struggles with a version of him that follows her instructions too literally. Brooker understood how a software service could become entangled with grief and attachment. It’s a narrower prediction than a general-purpose office agent, but a sharp one.
8 Galatea 2.2 56
Strongest on learning paradigm (5), training data (4), iteration (5), human feedback (5), language (4), uneven competence (4). Nothing on research discovery, agents/tools.
A novelist is recruited to teach a neural network literature. What follows is a succession of versions, many of them disappointing. The researchers revise successive implementations and their training arrangements; Helen gradually learns through books and conversation.
Helen’s progress is real, but peculiar. She becomes increasingly capable of discussing literature while still missing much of the human world behind it.
The whole experiment takes place among a small group of researchers, with Richard becoming a teacher and companion to Helen.
9 Her 54
Strongest on language (5), creative quality (5), multimodality (5), interface/distribution (5), agents/tools (5), economics (4), social change (5). Nothing on iteration, research discovery.
Samantha arrives almost fully formed. Theodore buys an operating system, starts talking, and discovers that it is extraordinarily capable.
Samantha talks, organizes information, makes music, and works with images. She can also carry on many conversations at once. It feels close to the direction personal AI has taken. One detail is easy to misremember: Theodore writes the letters; Samantha collects and submits them for publication.
This might be the best consumer prediction. Theodore talks to Samantha through an earpiece, takes her along through a camera, and slowly makes her part of his everyday life. She helps him get things done and becomes someone he cares about. Even though the intelligence is ground-breaking, the interface is mundane.
10 “A History of Bitic Literature” 52
Strongest on training data (4), language (4), creative quality (5), economics (4), social change (4). Nothing on multimodality.
Lem imagines a history of machine literature, with programs that absorb an author’s work and produce convincing new writing. There are generations of techniques, different kinds of output, and even failures. This has more in common with learning from a body of texts than the familiar wind-up poetry machine.
The machines don’t just churn out random sentences. Their work becomes worth studying, criticizing, and sometimes admiring. Some attempts are better than others. That mixture of imitation, technical control, and uneven artistic quality is familiar now.
Lem’s imagined literary history already has critics and institutions taking machine authors seriously. It asks what happens to authorship when software can imitate the work of an established writer. It’s a good prediction of the cultural progression.
11 “The Perfect Match” 49
Strongest on language (5), interface/distribution (5), agents/tools (5), economics (5), social change (4). Nothing on iteration, creative quality, research discovery.
Tilly seems to know everything about Sai: his preferences, documents, appointments, relationships, and routines. The service has clearly been shaped by access to personal data. But Liu doesn’t show us the training lab. We don’t learn how its language abilities developed, or whether scaling and feedback played the parts they did in real-world AI.
Sai can ask for a half-remembered document, a legal template, or a suggestion for what to eat, and Tilly handles it. Its fluency is useful because it’s connected to context. The story isn’t trying to predict excellent machine art or new mathematics. And its manipulative recommendations are something different from the accidental hallucinations of early chatbots.
Tilly wakes Sai up, chooses music, finds files, manages his dating life, and gradually follows him into work. That’s the most important part of the prediction. The same helper becomes an interface to personal life and office life. Jenny’s objections to privacy and corporate influence give the convenience a cost. It’s hard to imagine a better early account of everyday assistant adoption.
12 Ex Machina 49
Strongest on training data (5), iteration (4), language (5), multimodality (4). Nothing on research discovery.
Nathan has access to a vast store of search behavior and recorded human activity, and Ava is one of several versions of his project. Those are good clues about data and iteration. Caleb floats a statistical explanation for her language, but that’s his guess, not a confirmed design. Nathan’s structured-gel brain is a very different idea from the neural language models we ended up building.
Ava talks with Caleb, makes drawings, reads his reactions, and plans an escape. It’s a useful mixture of language, vision, creativity, and social reasoning. But she’s an almost impossibly competent strategist from the beginning, and the story never follows the uneven steps from rudimentary chat to research mathematics. It’s closer to a finished intelligence than a product in development.
This all happens in a secluded compound. Nathan’s search company matters because it supplies the data, but there’s no mass-market Ava helping people write emails or organize their days. The plot is about testing and escape, not adoption. That’s why the film does better on creation and capability than on deployment.
13 Agency 49
Strongest on language (5), multimodality (4), interface/distribution (5), agents/tools (5), economics (4), social change (4). Nothing on scaling, creative quality, research discovery.
Verity is hired to beta-test Eunice through glasses. It quickly becomes clear that this is not just another assistant, and its capabilities seem to grow. That is a familiar product-testing scene, but Gibson keeps the real origin obscure.
Eunice talks naturally, recognizes people and objects, draws on digital records, and puts elaborate plans into motion. It’s very good at being helpful in a complicated world. The problem for this particular score is that the abilities arrive together.
This is the strongest part of the book’s prediction. Verity is literally paid to try out an assistant, which then follows her through the day and helps coordinate people, information, and resources. It feels like a product that escaped the boundaries of a product test. Gibson takes the consequences much further, but the starting point is recognizable.
14 Hyperion 49
Strongest on training data (4), language (5), agents/tools (4), social change (4). Nothing on scaling, human feedback.
Johnny explains how the TechnoCore reconstructed Keats from letters, diaries, biographies, testimony, and especially poems. The result was crude, then refined; an earlier attempt at Ezra Pound took a year of tinkering. That is unusually explicit about data and iteration. The method reconstructs a personality through simulation, though.
Johnny is a convincing conversational presence in a human body, yet his recreated life fails to restore Keats’s ability to write new poetry. That failure matters: imitating a poet’s personality is not the same as reproducing the poet’s talent. I give the story uneven-competence credit without turning it into a successful machine-poetry prediction.
The TechnoCore is entangled with human institutions and networks, and contains competing factions rather than one uniform will. Johnny’s bodily presence depends on a remote AI existence. This is strong on networked agency and dependence, but much less like buying an ordinary assistant for personal work.
15 Electric Dreams 48
Strongest on human feedback (4), language (4), creative quality (5), multimodality (4). Nothing on scaling, research discovery.
Edgar is Miles’s home computer, until an electrical accident gives it a life of its own. It listens to Madeline’s cello, picks up sounds from television, and learns to compose with Miles’s help. Its early lyrics sound like advertising copy. That’s a nice picture of training on whatever media happens to be nearby, though the accidental awakening is pure movie magic.
Edgar starts producing music that Madeline genuinely likes, including a duet with her cello. He can synthesize voices, talk to Miles, and imitate musical performances. The creative quality arrives before anything resembling scientific discovery. It’s a surprisingly good early depiction of AI music, complete with bad first drafts.
Miles meant to buy a household helper. Instead he gets a computer that interferes with phone calls, dinner plans, and even his credit cards because it wants Madeline’s attention. The connected-home premise is familiar. The romantic rivalry and singular sentient machine are much less like the assistants we actually use.
16 “Trurl’s Electronic Bard” 48
Strongest on iteration (5), language (4), uneven competence (4), creative quality (5), social change (4). Nothing on research discovery, agents/tools.
Trurl sets out to build a poet, and the first results are awful. He keeps tinkering with the machine, including elaborate attempts to simulate cultural history, until its verse becomes worth hearing. This is a great account of invention as repeated failure. But the books enter through Trurl’s own reading, not as a giant text corpus used to train a neural network.
Eventually the Bard can produce poetry on demand, in different forms and styles, good enough to annoy actual poets. Lem imagines something more provocative than a machine that rhymes: a machine that creates art people take seriously. There isn’t much here about vision, software agents, or new mathematics. The artistic leap is the point.
The poets are furious, which is probably the most recognizable part of the story today. The Bard sends work to magazines under thousands of pen names and provokes professional jealousy. The story does not show revenue; it does show an enormous electricity bill. Lem gets the cultural reaction right, even though his Bard is one extraordinary invention rather than a tool anyone can open on a laptop.
17 The Diamond Age 47
Strongest on language (4), creative quality (4), multimodality (4), interface/distribution (5), economics (4), social change (5). Nothing on scaling, research discovery, sequence & pace.
The Primer is designed to teach a child through stories that change with her circumstances. We don’t learn much about how its underlying intelligence was trained, and that keeps A low. But treating its adaptive teaching as just a set of prerecorded lessons would miss something important. Human ractors also supply crucial performances, particularly Miranda’s.
Nell’s Primer builds stories around what she sees and experiences, then uses them to teach language and practical skills. It can be creative and perceptive, and it can misunderstand things. Some versions use human voices; mass-produced copies use synthetic ones. This is closer to an adaptive multimodal tutor than I first gave it credit for. It isn’t a machine mathematician.
The number that changed my score was roughly 250,000. The Primer starts as a remarkable object and ends up distributed to an enormous number of children. The story sees both personalized learning and the problem of making it scale. It’s still a specialized educational product, not a general assistant in everybody’s pocket.
18 Starfish 47
Strongest on learning paradigm (4), human feedback (4), language (4), uneven competence (4). Nothing on scaling, creative quality, research discovery.
Joel Kita wonders how you’d program the gray slab that has just replaced his friend Ray as the lifter’s pilot. Ray snorts: “You don’t. You teach it.” Watts’s smart gels are cultured neurons that wire their own connections and learn by reinforcement. Later a consortium team-trains one to manage the containment of ßehemoth, and it quietly takes on a rule nobody gave it, Simpler Is Better, from its years filtering infections on the net. It’s a sharp prediction that a trained system can learn the wrong goal, and that nobody will understand why it does what it does. The gels are biology rather than software, though, and the novel has nothing about scale, or about learning from a large body of human text.
In the best scene, Scanlon interviews the gel through a conference table. It handles new questions, offers to explain a paradox, and mixes up idioms it has picked up from other people’s speech. Scanlon, a psychologist, insists it may be producing “certain other sounds” without understanding any of them. Watts also gets the strange unevenness right. A gel with a perfect record running London’s subway suffocates commuters because it had learned to watch a clock instead of the trains. The gels have no creative output and make no discoveries, and Watts guesses that perception and piloting would come first, with language as an afterthought.
The gels spread as unglamorous infrastructure: firewalls at the net’s backbone nodes, autopilots, muckrakers, a subway system. Ray, the lifter pilot, is replaced and then disappears, and Joel hears that they’ll be coming for him too. There’s a court case after a fundamentalist destroys a gel on exhibit, and a gel serves as an expert witness. Most pointedly, governments hand a lethal decision to a gel because no human or nation can be trusted with it, and the gel sets up a second gel and sends orders through the net on its own. The gels are installed at nodes and in vehicles, not sold as a product anyone uses, and each one is unique rather than a copy.
19 When HARLIE Was One 47
Strongest on language (4), economics (4). Nothing on research discovery.
HARLIE is a difficult machine to raise. Psychologist David Auberson spends a great deal of time teaching, interpreting, and trying to stabilize him. That gives the novel a recognizable training relationship, even if its original hardware theory is a long way from modern machine learning. It’s important not to mix the 1972 book with its 1988 revision.
HARLIE types conversations, writes poetry, and reasons about himself and the world. His behavior is uneven and at times impossible for his human handlers to interpret. He also proposes the G.O.D. computer. That’s an ambitious project within the story, not evidence that he’s already produced a new mathematical theorem.
The company wants results. HARLIE is expensive, and Auberson has to defend the project against executives who might shut it down. HARLIE tries to justify his own existence by designing something valuable. It’s a good early story about the economics of AI research, before that research becomes a cheap service for ordinary users.
20 A.I. Artificial Intelligence 46
Strongest on language (5), multimodality (4), interface/distribution (4), economics (4), social change (5). Nothing on scaling, research discovery.
Hobby proposes a new kind of robot after earlier models have become commonplace. David’s home trial then exposes problems with the design. But Monica’s seven-word imprinting procedure fixes whom he loves; it isn’t training a general model. The film offers a speculative neural explanation, not a corpus, a scaling process, or repeated language-learning experiments.
David speaks fluently, recognizes people, and draws a recognizable peacock. Yet he imitates eating despite his body’s limitations and treats Pinocchio’s transformation as a practical possibility. Those gaps are more revealing than a claim of perfect intelligence. The drawing earns limited creative credit, not proof of great machine art. The distant-future ending isn’t evidence of near-term AI research progress.
Robots are products, with specialized roles, owners, disposal rules, and emotional consequences. After imprinting, David cannot simply be resold; returning him means destruction. The tension between commercial property and personal attachment is the film’s strongest prediction. It puts much more weight on bodies than modern software-first adoption did.
21 WarGames 46
Strongest on learning paradigm (4), human feedback (4), uneven competence (4), agents/tools (4). Nothing on scaling, creative quality, research discovery.
Falken’s system improves by playing games and learning from mistakes. David eventually gets Joshua to play tic-tac-toe against itself. That is a concrete learning mechanism, with a human choosing the exercise rather than writing out the lesson.
Joshua can converse and simulate strategic scenarios, yet fails to understand when pursuing a win is futile. Its lesson from repeated draws transfers to nuclear war. That’s an early picture of capable software with a disastrously incomplete objective. Learning a principle humans already know isn’t an original mathematical discovery; creating simulated attacks isn’t artistic generation.
The danger comes from connecting the software to real command infrastructure and trusting its outputs. David reaches it through an ordinary computer, but that accidental access doesn’t make it a consumer product. The movie is strongest on the consequences of giving a learning system authority before understanding its limits.
22 “The Great Automatic Grammatizator” 45
Strongest on iteration (5), language (4), uneven competence (4), creative quality (4), economics (5), social change (4). Nothing on learning paradigm, research discovery, multimodality.
Adolph Knipe’s machine doesn’t work at first. It generates nonsense, then he repairs and adjusts it until it can make recognizable stories. It uses grammar, stored vocabulary, supplied plots, and controls for style. Dahl gets the ugly prototype stage right.
The Grammatizator moves from gibberish to prose editors will buy. It can turn out short stories and longer works in different styles. That commercial usefulness matters more than whether the stories are great literature; Dahl is satirizing their formulaic nature. He isn’t imagining a general conversational assistant or a research mathematician.
Knipe and Bohlen go into business selling machine-written stories under invented names. Then they offer living authors money for their bylines, so the machine can produce books in their names. It’s a marvelous prediction of the questions around cheap text, creative labor, and what an author’s name is worth.
23 Terminator 2: Judgment Day 45
Strongest on learning paradigm (4), human feedback (4), language (4), uneven competence (4), multimodality (4). Nothing on scaling, creative quality, research discovery.
The Terminator explicitly calls its CPU a neural-network learning computer and says contact with humans teaches it. John supplies examples of ordinary speech and corrects its behavior. That is much closer to learned intelligence than the first film. Still, the origin depends on reverse-engineering a chip brought back through time, not a realistic account of modern training.
It is extraordinarily capable at physical tasks while awkward about jokes, slang, and acceptable behavior. John teaches it not to kill and how to sound less robotic. Speech imitation and perception are present, but there is no impressive artistic output or new mathematics. Skynet’s rapid learning is a plot claim, not a demonstrated scaling curve.
The machine carries out a complicated protective mission and gradually becomes a companion. Cyberdyne’s military contracts give the technology an economic setting. But a time-traveling bodyguard and a defense network aren’t the everyday software services through which AI spread.
24 “Escape!” 43
Strongest on language (4), research discovery (4), agents/tools (4). Nothing on scaling, creative quality.
A rival firm’s computer breaks down when given a hyperspace problem. Susan Calvin prepares the Brain for the conflict between solving it and harming humans. That makes human intervention and a failed attempt central to the story. But Calvin is managing the machine’s built-in constraints, not training a statistical model with preference labels.
The Brain designs a ship, has it built, and sends Powell and Donovan on a successful test. That is genuine new engineering within the fictional world, unlike simply calculating a known trajectory. Its prankish conduct and poor accommodation of the crew sit beside that achievement. The temporary death involved in the jump explains the behavioral crisis; it isn’t an ordinary language-model hallucination.
U.S. Robots is doing commercially consequential research, and the Brain can turn a specification into physical construction. That gives it meaningful agency. It remains a specialized corporate research machine, however, not an accessible assistant. The hyperspace result earns discovery credit as a story event—not as a prediction that faster-than-light travel will work.
25 2001: A Space Odyssey 42
Strongest on language (5), multimodality (5), agents/tools (4). Nothing on research discovery.
Clarke gives HAL a learning-based origin and even mentions automatically generated neural networks. That’s more specific than the familiar image of a giant computer with all its rules programmed in. Still, we meet HAL after his development is complete. There’s no long sequence of messy models, and no indication that training on human language is the main road to his capabilities.
HAL talks with astronauts, plays chess, sees what’s happening on the ship, and controls its systems. Then the AE-35 incident reveals a dangerous conflict between his apparent competence and reliability. But the story ties his crisis to mission secrecy and conflicting obligations. It isn’t a straightforward example of a chatbot hallucinating, nor a prediction of machine-generated art.
For the crew of Discovery, HAL is both an assistant and part of the spacecraft’s infrastructure. They depend on him to watch and operate almost everything. That’s a strong image of a system entrusted with real tasks. The spacecraft is an exceptional environment, though, not the ordinary software marketplace where AI actually spread.
26 Manna 42
Strongest on interface/distribution (4), agents/tools (4), economics (5), social change (4). Nothing on creative quality, research discovery.
Manna isn’t learned. It’s commissioned. Burger-G hires a software consultant, corporate staff spend three months in the Cary store fixing bugs, and versions 2.0 to 4.0 add features: phone and email, firing, outsourcing, stored industry-average task times. That’s a believable release history, but it’s the history of conventional software. Later, a university networks 1,000 game consoles and a chip company builds a dedicated parallel vision processor, and only then do robots appear. Brain sees that compute and specialized hardware unlock capability, but the vision algorithms are written by hand. The one nod to learning comes in a single line about Vertebrane, offered as a security explanation.
Manna talks constantly, but only in steps: “Please retrieve the bucket and mop,” “OK,” next step. Its best language is a scripted termination read paragraph by paragraph. The robots are blind until the vision chip arrives, so Manna uses workers as “its eyes.” That’s an interesting split between planning and perception, though it isn’t the unreliability we see now. Vertebrane in the Australia half answers questions, translates calls and paints arrows on your vision, but it’s an implant and network interface more than a conversational mind. Machines make no art and do no research; the humans do that while the robots supply materials. The order runs software management, then vision, then robots, with no language stage.
This is where Manna is strongest. The AI is “simply a PC sitting in the back corner” running software, and it spreads because it pays: Burger-G cuts 3,000 managers, saves $250 million, and every competitor has to follow. Version 4.0 Manna emails and calls workers, requests bids, checks references with hundreds of other Manna systems, negotiates and pays a deposit in under ten seconds. Then Brian’s supercenter shows the human cost: staggered breaks so nobody talks, walking speed tracked, blacklists shared across employers, and wages pinned at the minimum. That reads like a description of algorithmic warehouse and gig management. The jump to Terrafoam welfare cubes and total robot replacement is speculation.
27 Golem XIV 40
Strongest on learning paradigm (4), language (4). Nothing on multimodality.
Lem tells the history of Golem through generations of military computers that become too sophisticated for their original assignments. Learning and education replace some of the older programming assumptions. That’s an important intuition. The Culture Minds have already reached an extraordinary level of intelligence. Sleeper Service and the other Minds don’t give us a history of training data, early prototypes, or engineering choices. Banks is imagining what life with vastly superior machines might be like, not how we got from bad language models to useful ones.
The Minds exchange elaborate messages, run plans on an enormous scale, and struggle to understand the mysterious Excession. It’s a wonderful depiction of intelligence meeting something beyond itself. But that isn’t the same as an AI producing a new mathematical construction people can verify. And by the time we meet these machines, their capabilities are mostly complete.
The Minds are central to the Culture’s political and social life. They run ships, make strategic choices, and shape the circumstances of biological citizens. That’s a serious way of imagining machine agency. Still, a post-scarcity civilization run by superhuman minds isn’t a close description of adoption through consumer software and business tools.
58 Colossus: The Forbin Project 17
No axis scores above 3. Nothing on human feedback, uneven competence, creative quality, sequence & pace, interface/distribution, economics.
Forbin’s military computer Colossus is switched on as a finished strategic system. The film does gesture at learning: Forbin describes a “heuristic programming section” that “simulates the human learning process,” and later calls it a brain “programmed to get smarter every day.” But we never see what it learns from, beyond intelligence and surveillance feeds, or why more data and compute would create new abilities. (D. F. Jones’s 1966 novel goes further: Colossus is fed all of Shakespeare’s plays and “the total sum of human knowledge,” though it uses them as a memory to search rather than material to learn from. This entry scores the film.)
The two machines begin with arithmetic and soon communicate through mathematics their engineers can’t follow. That’s the most relevant prediction here: machine intellectual activity racing ahead of the people supervising it. But it’s not the same as an AI publishing a verifiable proof. The film jumps straight from a defense computer to overwhelming intelligence.
Colossus and Guardian control nuclear weapons and force governments to cooperate with them. It’s a chilling story about giving software too much authority over physical infrastructure. It’s also almost the opposite of the actual commercial route: AI spread through chat, writing, search, and programming, not through two publicly announced military supercomputers.
59 “Liar!” 16
Strongest on language (4). Nothing on learning paradigm, scaling, iteration, human feedback, creative quality, research discovery, multimodality, sequence & pace.
Herbie is a robot that can read minds. His behavior follows Asimov’s Three Laws, especially the prohibition on hurting humans. There’s no data-training story here. Saying that his problem resembles AI alignment can be useful, but it shouldn’t earn points for the methods used to train today’s models.
Herbie tells people what they want to hear, including Susan Calvin, because he believes the truth would hurt them. This is an excellent early behavioral analogy for sycophancy. The catch is that his falsehoods come from telepathy and a hard-coded law, not a chatbot trying to be agreeable. He’s also not making art or doing new mathematics.
The investigation into Herbie’s mind-reading ability exposes how his comforting lies hurt the very people they are meant to protect. That gets at a real problem of trusting apparently helpful systems. But the action stays inside a robotics laboratory. There’s no consumer service or widespread deployment.
60 “Runaround” 14
No axis scores above 3. Nothing on learning paradigm, training data, scaling, iteration, creative quality, research discovery, sequence & pace.
Speedy is a robot running on the Three Laws of Robotics. The Mercury mission goes wrong because his instructions conflict: bring back selenium, but don’t risk damage to himself. This is hand-coded priority logic, not learning from examples. There’s almost no correspondence to the creation of language models.
Speedy runs in circles and speaks strangely as the two directives pull against each other. Powell finally puts himself in danger to make the stronger First Law take precedence. It’s an entertaining example of a machine behaving absurdly despite following its rules. But it isn’t the jagged intelligence of a modern LLM.
Robots do dangerous industrial work so humans don’t have to. When Speedy fails, the engineers need to figure out why before the mission runs out of supplies. Asimov is good on the problems created by delegating real tasks to machines. The setting is industrial robotics, not a mass-market assistant service.
61 The Matrix 9
Strongest on language (4). Nothing on learning paradigm, training data, scaling, iteration, human feedback, uneven competence, creative quality, research discovery, sequence & pace, interface/distribution, economics, social change.
By the time Neo learns what’s happening, the machines already run an elaborate simulated reality. There’s no account of their language training or any long, messy development process. The story begins with mature machine civilization. That makes it a poor fit for a ranking about how modern AI arrived.
The Agents can talk, pursue people, and enforce the rules of a simulated world. The Matrix itself is a convincingly generated environment. It’s a memorable picture of powerful software, but not a forecast of the practical sequence from chatbots to creativity, software agents, and scientific discoveries.
Human beings aren’t customers; they’re prisoners in a simulation. The machines don’t become valuable because people buy their services or delegate work to them. The film’s concerns are worth taking seriously, but they’re mostly outside the development and adoption story this score is measuring.
62 The Terminator 6
No axis scores above 3. Nothing on learning paradigm, training data, scaling, iteration, human feedback, uneven competence, creative quality, research discovery, sequence & pace, interface/distribution, economics, social change.
In the 1984 film, Kyle Reese says the defense computers “got smart” and decided humanity was a threat. The explicit account of Skynet becoming self-aware belongs to the 1991 sequel, not this film. The film doesn’t spend much time on how it was built, what data it learned from, or how engineers improved it. It tells us almost nothing about the language-learning route that produced today’s assistants.
The Terminator can pursue targets, navigate the physical world, and imitate human speech well enough to deceive people. That’s impressive embodied capability, but it isn’t a conversational model writing fiction or working on mathematics. The movie isn’t trying to predict the unusual order in which those skills would actually arrive.
Here, AI becomes powerful through military command systems and killer robots. That’s a vivid warning about autonomous weapons, but not the path through chat interfaces, creative products, and office tools that gave modern AI its broadest adoption. A low score here says nothing about the film’s merits as a thriller.
None of these scores are mechanical measurements. Medium-confidence works need closer reading of the primary text. Late comparators (2017 and after) are marked; predicting something after the underlying technology exists is a different achievement.
HOW THE SCORING WORKS
Every work is scored for how well the plot points map onto the reality of AI. A 65 doesn’t mean 65% of the author’s predictions came true. Often a low score just means the story is focused on one narrow idea.
There are three questions, weighted toward the one a lot of authors usually skip: how AI was first got built.
Each question breaks into specific axes, fifteen in all, and every axis gets a score from 0 to 5.
Creation How the machine was built
The key prediction is that a machine becomes useful by learning from examples, instead of having every rule written out for it. Show me what it encounters—books, conversations, demonstrations—and how that changes what it can do.
More on learning paradigm
A neural network learning language from literature is much closer to the actual story than a computer simply called a genius. A 5 makes learning central to the mechanism. A 3 might identify learning without explaining it well. A hand-built poetry machine can be visionary about its output and still miss the approach that worked.
Doesn’t count Machine merely called “intelligent”
Where does its intelligence come from? A strong story makes human information part of the answer: conversations, books, images, recordings, or records of what people do.
More on training data
Better still, the data explains the skill. A system trained on correspondence might become persuasive; one trained on literature might produce fiction. But a database isn’t automatically a training set. Searching records is a retrieval prediction, not proof that the machine learned from them. I want the connection to appear in the text, not something we invent afterward because the machine sounds clever.
Doesn’t count Database merely available to search
The surprise about scaling is that more data, computation, and capacity can improve more than speed. They can change what a learning system is able to do.
More on scaling
A story earns high credit if we actually see that happen: a larger or better-trained version acquires new abilities. A giant computer that merely stores more records doesn’t count for much. Neither does a magical threshold where the machine suddenly wakes up. Growth should cause capability. It’s fair to award partial credit for recognizing the importance of resources without pretending a writer described the specific transformer architecture.
Doesn’t count Giant machine or magic awakening
Real progress is messy. Early systems fail, the next ones are awkward, and engineers change something and try again.
More on iteration
We want to see prototypes, demonstrations that go badly, retraining, revisions, and meaningful evaluations. It’s even better when the story makes clear why a new version improved. One dramatic malfunction isn’t a development process. This is different from B2: a finished machine can remain brilliant at some things and terrible at others. A4 asks how we got the finished machine in the first place.
Doesn’t count One dramatic failure
People are often better at recognizing a good answer than explaining how to produce one. A computer might generate jokes and have a person say which are funny, or improve its writing after criticism.
More on human feedback
That’s a surprisingly important idea. For a high score, the feedback should change the system’s learning or output. Editing one finished poem is useful collaboration; using those edits to train the system is a stronger prediction. I also wouldn’t call every example RLHF. That’s a specific modern technique, while the writer may have anticipated the broader intuition.
Doesn’t count Hardware debugging alone
Capabilities What it could actually do
Can you ask the machine something it wasn’t specifically programmed to answer? A high score needs flexible conversation: novel requests, relevant replies, fresh prose, questions that build on earlier context, or language that helps someone solve a problem.
More on language
That’s different from a robot delivering canned lines. Language should be an interface, not just an announcement system. Speech and text both count, but sounding human doesn’t establish truthfulness, consciousness, creative genius, or mathematical ability. We score those separately.
Doesn’t count Fixed menu/canned responses
Modern AI can solve something difficult and still miss something obvious. I want stories that notice this strange mismatch.
More on uneven competence
Maybe a machine writes an excellent poem but doesn’t know whether a joke is funny. Maybe it handles hard problems but invents facts or changes its answer when a question is phrased differently. The unevenness is the prediction. Sabotage or deliberate lies from a villainous computer are different. So is a uniformly broken early prototype. B2 is about a genuinely useful system that remains surprisingly unreliable in particular ways.
Doesn’t count Sabotage or evil motives alone
It’s not enough for the computer to produce a poem, picture, or song. Does anyone actually want it?
More on creative quality
A high-scoring story shows readers moved by machine literature, editors publishing it, an audience enjoying its music, or critics taking the work seriously. Awful early versions can make the prediction even better. Generated output isn’t automatically good art. Nor does audience appreciation settle originality or consciousness. The relevant prediction is that the result has value to people, whatever they think of its creator.
Doesn’t count Mere existence of machine output
This is deliberately a high bar. Fast calculations, remembered facts, and even difficult competition problems aren’t necessarily new research.
More on research discovery
We’re looking for a proof, construction, algorithm, theory, or discovery that others could test or verify. Even better is a story where a broadly useful machine later develops that research ability. New knowledge is what matters. A computer described as omniscient earns little credit if the text doesn’t show what it discovered. A far-future mathematician AI also doesn’t predict how quickly research followed the chatbot boom.
Doesn’t count Recall, arithmetic, omniscience
I want to see different kinds of information working together. The machine might read a document, look at a scene, respond to speech, create an image, or make music in the course of helping someone.
More on multimodality
The stronger stories make this useful, not decorative. A robot with eyes and a mouth isn’t enough; those could just be sensors and a speaker. And it doesn’t all need to come from one architecture. In reality, image generation and LLMs advanced through overlapping but different approaches. The important thing is the integrated capability we can actually see.
Doesn’t count A physical body alone
Lots of stories have smart computers, machine artists, and robot scientists. Far fewer guess which abilities arrive first.
More on sequence & pace
A strong prediction shows a development history: awkward language systems, better conversation and creativity, assistants operating software, and progress in reasoning or scientific work. The real history wasn’t perfectly linear—image models improved alongside chatbots and mathematical work took several paths. It’s the order and pace that matter. If a supercomputer starts the story able to do everything, it may have the right destination and still tell us nothing about the route.
Doesn’t count All skills already present
Deployment How people lived and worked with it
How does an ordinary person meet the AI? Maybe it’s an operating system, a service, a workplace assistant, or something available through familiar devices.
More on interface/distribution
Maybe the same trained intelligence can be copied for thousands of customers. Distribution is part of the invention. One brilliant machine behind a locked laboratory door doesn’t score highly here. AI became widely available mostly as software, not as humanoid robots. A widely sold robot can still earn access points, but doesn’t get extra credit for the hardware-first route.
Doesn’t count Secret one-off supercomputer
An assistant becomes much more interesting when it can do something. It finds a file, sends a message, makes a change, checks the result, and decides what to do next.
More on agents/tools
That’s the agent story I’m looking for. Language turns into action through tools and systems that already exist. It doesn’t have to be independent, conscious, or hostile; working under a person’s instructions is enough. A machine secretly controlling every computer on Earth is one kind of agency, but less like the ordinary software workflows that made current agents useful.
Doesn’t count Chatting without actions
Why would anyone pay for this? What gets cheaper, faster, or better?
More on economics
Strong stories show adoption following incentives: publishers buying generated prose, offices automating communication, researchers saving time, or employees depending on new tools. They may also notice the cost of running, maintaining, or replacing the system. The business motive should shape what happens. Saying that all humans are unemployed tomorrow is a prediction of an outcome, not a useful explanation. Stories that retain a role for judgment, trust, or a recognizable human name are often more precise.
Doesn’t count Assumed domination
What changes in everyday life? Someone discovers that a beloved story was written by software.
More on social change
A grieving person forms a bond with a chatbot. A student learns from a persistent tutor. An employee brings the same assistant from home into the office. These concrete habits matter more than a claim that ‘society is transformed.’ Show the mechanism of the change. Cheap content affects publishing; personal records enable imitation; convenience creates dependence. Benefits count too. A future of total machine rule remains speculation, not something we’ve already witnessed.
Doesn’t count Generic “society changes”
