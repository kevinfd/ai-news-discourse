# This brain change can make foxes act like pet dogs

Scientific American · Mary Randolph · 2026-11-06

In the 1950s Russian researchers embarked on an ambitious experiment: they wanted to see if foxes could be selectively bred to become domesticated, doglike animals, complete with wagging tails and affectionate natures. Now, close to 70 years into this long-running breeding project, scientists have pinpointed a specific type of brain cell linked with changes in the foxes’ behavior.
Specifically, the scientists found that a receptor protein in the ventromedial hypothalamus may form the basis for the foxes’ social behavior. This brain area is known to be involved in fear and defense responses and aggression.
The research “gets to the heart of brain programming” in animals, says Cheryl Rosenfeld, a neuroscientist at the University of Missouri, who was not involved in the study.

“If this can happen in foxes, it could also mean that, by altering the hormone profile or the receptor profile in us and even other species, it could change our patterns of behavior and last a lifetime,” Rosenfeld says.
Eirene Markenscoff-Papadimitriou is the study’s senior author and a molecular biologist at Cornell University. She calls herself an “accidental fox scientist.” Markenscoff-Papadimitriou primarily studies neuroscience in mice, but in 2023 she started looking into the Domesticated Silver Fox Experiment. The project, started in 1959 by a group of Russian researchers, selectively bred the tamest silver foxes.
After just a few generations, the foxes began to look and act more domesticated, with floppy ears, curly tails and an eagerness for human attention and love. The experiment is still going and is a model of the power of selective breeding. Markenscoff-Papadimitriou’s team wanted to know what was happening to the foxes’ brains.
The team compared the brains of three categories of farm-bred foxes—tame, aggressive and wild—with a special focus on the ventromedial hypothalamus, a brain region that’s been linked to aggressive behaviors in mice. In previous studies, scientists had found that when a receptor protein called estrogen receptor alpha was activated, the mice were more aggressive. In the new study, published on November 2 in Current Biology, Markenscoff-Papadimitriou and her team found that the tame foxes had notably fewer of the estrogen receptor alpha neurons than the aggressive or conventional foxes.
Markenscoff-Papadimitriou says this experiment made her a “convert” to researching other animals beyond mice.
“This showed me that we should be ambitious and follow our curiosity and that the tools that have been developed for just one species can be applied to others,” she says.
Both Markenscoff-Papadimitriou and Rosenfeld say they’re curious if the same neuron determines social behavior in other animals, such as dogs and wolves. Now that the researchers know how the tame foxes’ brains are different from their more aggressive peers, they’re asking why and how that happens.
“How are the genetic differences of these different foxes leading to differences in the cell type composition of this brain region throughout development?” Markenscoff-Papadimitriou says. “It’s a puzzle to solve, but now we know what we need to solve.”
