# Bringing The Factory To The Worker Could Solve The Skills Gap

Forbes · Alexander Clausbruch, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-09

Alexander Clausbruch, Radix NA CEO, enabling customers from Vision to Value with operational excellence and strong, sustainable results.
Today’s global headlines frequently focus on AI eliminating jobs. But the opposite is often true in heavy industry: companies simply cannot find enough workers to fill the roles.
In manufacturing and energy sectors, younger workers can be reluctant to take roles in remote or offshore environments. Meanwhile, more experienced operators are retiring in droves, creating a widening skills gap.
The numbers make for stark reading. In advanced economies, 2.4 energy workers are close to retiring for every worker under 25, according to the International Energy Agency. This means, between now and 2035, two out of every three new energy-sector hires will be needed just to replace retiring workers.
Manufacturing is similarly up against it. According to the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, job openings for industrial mechanics and maintenance workers are projected to grow by 14% by 2035, with approximately 51,900 openings annually over the next coming decade.
These figures clearly contradict the prevailing narrative that AI is replacing heavy industry jobs. The more nuanced story is that technology can help address labor shortages by making scarce human expertise more productive. We already see this in the growth of centralized operations centers, where experts can oversee multiple facilities remotely while working closer to where they actually want to live.
Technologies like digital twins, AI and connected industrial systems mean engineers and specialists no longer need to be standing beside assets or machinery to make sense of them. The global mining giant Rio Tinto offers a glimpse of what this could look like at scale. Around 90% of its haul truck fleet across the Pilbara is now autonomous, while its operations center in Perth allows trucks, trains, plants and drills to be operated from 1,500 kilometers (over 900 miles) away.
Roles that once depended on having workers physically present at remote mine sites are increasingly shifting toward remote operations specialists and autonomous fleet controllers. We are seeing the same trend at Radix. Leafcutter, our industrial data collection technology, reached a milestone with an oil and gas client, moving 1 billion data points per day from more than 4 million process variables at under two seconds of latency.
Crucially, connectivity such as this means real-time operational data can be made available beyond the plant. Data scientists and external technical specialists can access information that would traditionally have been available primarily to operators and engineers working on site.
This is also the foundation on which industrial AI depends. The value of collecting vast quantities of real-time data is not simply that people can see it remotely. AI systems can analyze that data continuously, helping identify patterns, flag emerging problems and give engineers the information they need to intervene earlier.
None of this removes the need for people on industrial sites. Plants, factories and energy facilities will continue to require operators, engineers and maintenance workers on the ground. But industrial AI, digital twins and connected operations can change the relationship between people and physical assets. AI can process an increasingly complex flow of operational data, while human expertise can be deployed where judgement and intervention are most valuable.
So, in response to today’s AI doomsayers: yes, the factory of the future will still need people in abundance. The difference is that not all of them will need to be on the factory floor.
