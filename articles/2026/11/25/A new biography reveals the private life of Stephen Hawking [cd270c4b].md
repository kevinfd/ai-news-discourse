# A new biography reveals the private life of Stephen Hawking

Scientific American · Brianne Kane, Fonda Mwangi, Alex Sugiura, Rachel Feltman · 2026-11-25

Rachel Feltman: For Scientific American’s Science Quickly, I’m Rachel Feltman.
If I asked you to name the most famous scientists of the 20th century—or even of all time—I bet Stephen Hawking would end up somewhere on your list. But how much do you really know about this iconic physicist?
In his new book Hawking: His Life and Work, award-winning science writer and biographer Graham Farmelo draws on years of research to explore Hawking’s complicated personal story. Scientific American’s books and culture lead Bri Kane recently sat down with Graham to chat. Here’s their conversation.

Bri Kane: The sources on this is really incredible. I mean, the level of detail of knowing what he had for breakfast some days, and what his mother went home and, and wrote in her journal about him that day. It’s pretty extraordinary. What was he like as a child? Because the first few hundred pages of this book, he’s a child. Like, he’s under 18. He’s in school. He’s playing with his friends. And I’d never really heard about that part of his life before. So I wanted to ask you about your research and your sources into his childhood.
Graham Farmelo: Two things. First of all, the very early life of Stephen Hawking, that is illuminated in my book thanks to the wonderful work of his father in keeping a journal for 60 years, right, which started in the 1920s. One of the things he longed for was a son; he wrote diligently, sometimes almost every day, about Stephen Hawking. That and the writings of his mother give an extraordinarily rich feel for the growth of Stephen Hawking and, I would say, help illuminate why Hawking became the person he is.
Well, his school days, I can’t tell you the good fortune I had there because I managed to meet with, I think it was about half a dozen of his school chums, and I actually became very good friends with his best friend at school, John McClenahan. Lovely man. I think he was very lucky to meet Stephen. I was very lucky to meet John. John died last Christmas Eve, and I was the last person to have lunch with him, right? And John read, reread, checked and facilitated a lot of the detail, right, of that amazing duo that was Hawking and McClenahan because they were seen as the kind of Lennon-McCartney of the Meccano model, and Stephen was the person that came out with the big dramatic ideas like. But John was the person who made it happen, and you needed them both, you know.
So again, all I read before was that John and Stephen worked together. But with him, he was sending me dozens of e-mails of picturing the devices they made. So anyway, it was a real experience to get to know. And I’m sad to say some of those people have died since that research has been done. So future Hawking biographers are not going to have, I’m sad to say, access to those people.
Kane: I did not realize he wasn’t a very diligent student. Like, young and in elementary school and, and what, you know, we Americans would refer to as, like, high school and early college years, he was not particularly interested in academics. It was boring. He was, you know, a bit of a gifted child syndrome. It was a little too easy for him, and he wasn’t really engaged, which is interesting because he was so popular. Like, he had friends. He was on the rowing team. He was busy tinkering with his friends and building helicopters and radios. It’s interesting to think about how that led to the popularized figure of Stephen Hawking that we have in our minds.
Farmelo: Well, it could very easily have gone wrong. Remember, if you just move the focus along to when he was at college—he was at [the University of] Oxford—that, on the quiet, he’d registered for the Oxford Union, which trains prime ministers. And at the age of 17, he was debating in that society opposite a future prime minister at one point, right? But what he didn’t want to do was spend all the time being a swot. He knew he was good; he came very close not to getting the top grade because he was spending so much of his time partying and coxing.
But one thing that I cut from the book, and I perhaps shouldn’t have done, but Derek Powney, who sadly died a couple of years ago, I showed him a telegram that he sent Stephen Hawking, and it said something like this. This is pretty accurate. He said, “Congratulations. A first-class honors degree on one term’s work.” [Laughs] Now, I think that’s a little bit of an exaggeration, but do you see what he’s saying? That basically he only got his finger out, as they say in English English, right, in the last term because he was having such fun. And that could have been disastrous.
Kane: Yeah, so he was quite the mischievous child, and he was very popular and, as you said, was always going to parties, hosting parties. He had a very rich social life.
And he kept a secret during this time. He was considered maybe frail. He was known to his friends as maybe a bit clumsy. But it was also during this time that he was getting his first diagnosis of motor neurone disease.
Farmelo: That was just after he graduated from Oxford. He got into [the University of] Cambridge, and after one term, he got that diagnosis. Basically the upshot was that he would probably be dead within the next two or three years, at most five. Absolutely devastating for him, right? Although he didn’t show it particularly clearly, but also his parents and his sisters ...
Kane: Yes, I mean, you had access to his father’s diaries at this time, and his father was very concerned, and this was very devastating news that Stephen might not have a future or what that future would look like. There’s a sentiment in the book at one point where Frank, Stephen’s father, is looking at the medical journals that he has access to at the time and looking into ...
Farmelo: And hiding them from his wife ...
Kane: Yes, and he hides them from his wife, and him and his wife do not tell Stephen right away. Stephen overhears a conversation.
Farmelo: Nobody told him. That’s exactly right. And even then, Stephen was in—it’s a pattern throughout his life—he was in denial. His mother would say, “He looks unperturbed.” And his father, you should read his diaries, right? One of them—I don’t think this is in the book ’cause my publishers didn’t like this, although I think they’ve changed their mind now—it begins, “Damn, damn, damn, damn.” And his father was saying, that he would walk into a room and burst into tears. It was a really terrible thing for his father, and his father worked his socks off to try and find if there was any hope, and it looked like there were none. Because that was a very primitive field in medicine. And basically there was nothing to be done.
Kane: Yeah, I mean, Stephen Hawking comes from two very loving parents who were very interested and very invested in his future. And then he gets this devastating diagnosis fairly early in his life, and it really throws all of their plans off track. And then as he grows—his disease is degenerative; it’s not exactly getting better with time, as they expected—and then he meets Jane [Wilde; later Jane Hawking], and him and Jane fall deeply in love. And this surprises his parents, it seemed. I mean, they had very real concerns about this relationship and if she really understood what her future with Stephen Hawking would be. But I have to say, one of the things I didn’t fully understand about Stephen Hawking’s personal life is the scandal around his marriages and his relationships with Jane. So can you just explain for our listeners, who maybe are not invested in the British tabloid press, what is exactly the relationship between Stephen, Jane, Jonathan [Hellyer Jones] and then, later on, Elaine [Mason]?
Farmelo: First thing is that Stephen did have a girlfriend, right before Jane, and she ran away. She just couldn’t handle it. He’s never mentioned this to anybody else but his mother, and he, one of his sisters has seen this, right? He met Jane, and this was an extraordinary young woman because when she found out that he had this thing, she was shocked, but she just was not gonna let that stand between them.
And as I say in the book, this is actually crucial in his life. Although the marriage lasted decades, the key thing was it gave him a reason why he had to earn some money. Wasn’t just a matter of passing his Ph.D.; he had to support, certainly, their life together. He was a bright graduate student, and then the great gravitational physicist Roger Penrose comes up with this discovery of how a sufficiently heavy star can actually collapse to form a singularity, which basically was the basis of modern black hole theory, right?
Now, that was a major research discovery, right? Stephen, very bright, that supercharged his research interest. So do you see he had this wonderful rebirth, so to speak, right? He’d met a great love who he was desperate to marry. His research was going well, right? But Stephen’s parents on the side were saying, with a lot of admiration for Jane, but “Does she really know what is happening?” Stephen and Jane looked away basically, said, “We can handle this.” You know, it’s admirable in some ways but terrifying for Stephen’s parents.
For the first few years, Stephen’s work was progressing well. They got accommodation courtesy of one of the Cambridge colleges, and everything appeared to be going well. But his condition was going to get worse, and it did. And one of the big strains on the relationship was that Stephen was a very difficult patient. Most people could put theirselves into Jane’s shoes and see that, you know, his selfishness as she saw it, in, you know, pursuing his academic career and not being a compliant patient—husband, right? It really got to her. It became a really heavy burden for her. I’m oversimplifying, but, in Stephen’s words, they grew apart
Kane: I mean, I would imagine the change of relationship between newlywed and new wife into a caregiver would dramatically change their personal relationship, I’ll say. And, and this is seen through the invitation of Jonathan into their lives.
Farmelo: Yeah. Well, Jane was under enormous stress—she said this in her own book—really concerned for her children. He fathered three children, which was a wonderful thing for him and for her. But still this, of course, made her responsible for so many things in that house. She was becoming very lonely and really [at] her wit’s end about how to deal with this. And she met somebody whose wife had recently died, and that was Jonathan. And they became close, eventually intimate, right? And, far as she could see, Stephen didn’t mind this.
Kane: Yes, I mean, he’s quoted at one point as, “As long as they are discreet.”
Farmelo: Well, that’s what, that’s her report. Jane’s perfectly entitled to say that, and I take that on. But Stephen has never said this. He told friends that he was very unhappy about this. She says she saw no sign of this. I wasn’t there, so I simply have to present things as they were, right? Incidentally, I’ve met Jonathan. I think he’s the most lovely man. Now, I don’t think you need to be a complete macho person to say that having your wife’s “friend,” in inverted commas, staying in your house, right, and taking this role was not exactly Stephen’s comfort zone. I’m being very, very discreet here, alright? And this is the point where Stephen’s eyes started looking elsewhere, and that’s when he fell for one of his nurses, Elaine.
And that was a horrible period of—you can imagine, right? Because he and Jane end up divorcing, and after a period, he married Elaine. That was terrible for him because he didn’t want all this stuff going public, and it did. And the newspapers were reporting on it. Jane wrote a book about it, which was very graphic. So this stuff is pretty, pretty tough to read. But I took the view, as far as I possibly could, of presenting all sides of the story.
Kane: Yes, I mean, it was years of personal dramas being reported in the tabloids and, and a lot of very inappropriate questions being asked of people about the personal relationships between them. But at this time, there’s also very serious accusations coming from Stephen’s children, as well as coming from other nurses, that Elaine was abusive in some capacity. Is it your understanding that Elaine was abusive?
Farmelo: That statement has never been validated within a legal environment. The police investigated this twice.
Kane: Yes, and Stephen also never has said publicly that there was any maltreatment in that relationship, but his children and the other nurses were concerned and, and voiced this concern to him on multiple occasions.
Farmelo: It is a difficult thing to report on, right? Because all the different parties will read my account and say, “Well, that’s not what I saw.” But my job was to present, as far as I can, the different sides so that readers can decide. But I’ll tell you one thing: I never saw a single line where he criticized either of his wives. But he did do one thing. He said he was furious about the way that Elaine was treated by the press, right? That’s the one thing he said, right?
Kane: Yeah, I mean, he was not interested in being a celebrity that had their personal lives discussed openly in the British tabloids. He wanted to be a famous scientist. He wanted to talk about the science.
Can you summarize Stephen Hawking’s physics contributions? I mean, how can you even say it? He’s contributed so much to the field. It’s hard to really summarize his influence on it. But can you please give me a summary today?
Farmelo: I’ve spoken to dozens of world-leading scientists, right? people who are Stephen’s peers, right at the top of the Nobel- [and] Breakthrough Prize–winning, group of physicists, right? And it’s completely clear that they regard him as a truly great scientist, right? This notion that he’s only famous because he was disabled is a nonsense as far as they’re concerned.
Kane: Absurd.
Farmelo: He did a lot of stuff. The main highlight that many people know about, but it’s worth stressing how great a contribution this was and how much it’s esteemed by his colleagues, and that is the nature of black holes.
When he went into this field, there were plenty of people that didn’t believe in them. That’s really important to bear in mind. He wasn’t analyzing something he could look at and do experiments on. He was looking at something that emerged from Einstein’s equations of gravity. So that was a courageous thing to do. He wasn’t alone in doing it, but he invested, most of the time in the 1970s, to focus his prodigious energies into understanding these black holes.
A black hole is a domain of space and time where—there is a boundary to it. Anything can fall into it, but once inside, nothing can get out, even light. For that reason, it’s a black hole, and it’s gravity that keeps even light within its boundaries. Now, what are the laws governing the behavior of that, right? Now, Stephen was one of the great pioneers of discovering those laws, and his tour de force, which he did in late 1973—and I want to point out here, at that time, his good friend Martin Rees thought he was dying, right? He was going into Stephen’s office thinking, “I don’t think Stephen’s gonna last another year.”
And Stephen, then, was doing one of the great calculations of 20th-century physics. What is that calculation? He was looking at the behavior of quanta, of tiny subatomic particles on the outside that boundary of a black hole in what we call curved spacetime. And he showed that if you introduce quantum theory—that’s the theory of the very, very small that we have to use when we look at atoms or molecules—if you apply that to black hole theory, then, in a absolute tour de force calculation, it demonstrates that black holes must be radiating. They are not completely black.
Let me just say to you that when I was shown by a real expert how to do that calculation, the expert said four times to me, “If I were doing that calculation, I would’ve given up four times,” alright? It is absolutely prodigious. He did that calculation, as far as I can tell, in about five or six weeks.
Early 1974 it broke, and when he announced it, the chairman of the seminar [where] he gave it said, “Well, this is obviously nonsense.” And it took another, I think, six, seven months before the overwhelming majority of his peer group actually accepted what he’d done. So this wasn’t just doing a quick calculation that other people can do, you know, in their coffee break. This was a major project, right? And that still, right, still is the subject of researchers today. They are still looking, trying to understand the behavior of black holes in detail with Stephen Hawking’s contribution at the heart of it. He did lots of other things, but that is the thing he will always be remembered for, for sure.
Kane: I wanted to brag about Scientific American a little bit for a moment because, as you say, in the early ’70s, he’s working on his, I will say, most famous work as a physicist, and in 1977 he writes a little article for a magazine you might have heard of, Scientific American.
Farmelo: Hold on, that’s wrong. It was a good article, right? In a very prominent magazine. [Laughs.] No, seriously, and it was a good article.
Kane: Yes. I mean, you say that this article really changed the trajectory of his field because not only was he brilliant, but he could articulate himself well to the masses. You didn’t have to have a Ph.D. in physics to understand Stephen Hawking talking to you about physics, which is an extraordinary skill.
Farmelo: It is. Fred Hoyle, the great cosmologist, he was a predecessor of Stephen’s, one of Stephen’s idols when he was young. And he did a bit of popularization on the side. So Stephen had that as a precedent. So he decided to just try his hand. And as you rightly say, he did, wrote a very nice article for Scientific American. It was picked up by encyclopedias and other people started to pick this up. So he started to just dip his toes, so to speak, in celebrity stuff in the late ’70s.
But that led to something very important, which is his incredible book, A Brief History of Time, which followed actually giving some lectures at Harvard University. When he saw the reception that his talk gave, he thought, “I’m gonna try and write a popular book.”
And it took him a long time to do. It’s sold, we’re told, more than 25 million copies, about just the sheer wonder of living in this universe where you have these deep equations that you are investigating and making deductions from—understanding the nature of space and time, the nature of gravity, right? And he really was able to communicate that very well, both as a writer and indeed as a speaker—because, by that time, he had an artificial voice, by 1988.
Kane: Okay, I have to close this out today ’cause I could stay here and talk to you about this book for the rest of the day. But I wanted to ask you: Writing a biography about Stephen Hawking is an extraordinary task to take on as an author. He was a brilliant physicist. He is arguably one of the most popular authors of popular science writing. I mean, as you mentioned, A Brief History of Time was a yearlong New York Times bestseller, and that’s extraordinary feat just by itself. So at the end of the day, Graham, do you think he’d like your book?
Farmelo: I hope he would. [Laughs.] I’d be very disappointed after six years researching if he didn’t. But I tell you one thing, I was always mindful, when writing it, to be fair to him. I want to make sure it’s grounded in the best factual information I have and in the widest possible input of views, right? And Stephen’s friends, they’ve been nice enough to say that I do capture that sense of this astonishing vitality and determination and stubbornness. And I hope I brought that across, and I hope you for one enjoyed it.
Kane: I definitely enjoyed it. Thank you so much, Graham.
Farmelo: Thank you so much.
