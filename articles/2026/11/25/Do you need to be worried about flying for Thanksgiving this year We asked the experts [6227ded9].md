# Do you need to be worried about flying for Thanksgiving this year? We asked the experts

Scientific American · Mary Randolph · 2026-11-25

The busiest travel days of the year are here, less than two weeks after a historic ground stop, numerous tech and airline outages and swirling questions over what could possibly go wrong now. Ahead of Thanksgiving, we asked experts the question at the top of our family group chats: Will it be safe to fly?
The short answer is yes, says Arnold Barnett, a statistician at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, who studies aviation. So far this year, roughly 800 million passengers have flown on commercial planes run by U.S. airlines, he says—and each one landed safely.
That includes the recent ground stop on November 13, which the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) said was caused by a problem with equipment during repair works at a remote facility that led to widespread communications problems. No air traffic control centers fully lost contact, Barnett points out, and all planes that were in the air during the ground stop landed safely.

“Our air traffic controllers and safety professionals are working around the clock to keep Americans moving during what is expected to be the busiest Thanksgiving travel week in 16 years,” said FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford in a statement posted online.
According to data from the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB), which tracks air incidents, 2026 has seen fewer aviation accidents through October than any previous year at that point—1,216 compared with 1,267 in 2025 and more than 13,00 in each previous year, except 2020, which had fewer accidents in the same time frame. Of the accidents recorded through October this year, 244 accidents were fatal and 972 were nonfatal, compared with 240 and 1,027, respectively, in 2025.
Indeed, January 2025 saw the first fatal U.S. commercial plane crash in 16 years, when an American Airlines flight collided with a military helicopter over Washington, D.C. The NTSB, which advises the FAA, currently has dozens of open recommendations to increase safety following such aviation incidents.
David Woods, a U.S. safety systems researcher, says that he trusts commercial planes to carry his grandchildren safely this holiday season—but that this doesn’t mean our aviation systems don’t warrant a critical look moving forward. Woods says he is concerned about so-called reactive safety, or the practice of prioritizing technological innovation and then only adding safety measures once accidents have already happened. The country’s aviation infrastructure is aging, and as it’s updated, officials appear to be prioritizing technological advances such as artificial intelligence over safety needs, he says.
“We’re worried on two fronts,” Woods says. “One is that we’re starting to backslide a little on the safety advances, and two is that the changing technology and scale of systems is creating new risks that we’re not seeing and anticipating.”
Najmedin Meshkati, an engineering professor at the University of Southern California, who served on an expert panel for the FAA following a fatal crash of a Boeing 737 MAX plane in Indonesia in 2018 and another in Ethiopia in 2019, points out that many aviation accidents happen on the runway—not in the sky. He says that issues with technology and human factors, such as understaffed air traffic control centers, can impact safety. Hundreds of FAA workers were laid off in “Department of Government Efficiency” (U.S. DOGE Service)–related cuts in 2025. The Department of Transportation has tried to bolster hiring for air traffic controllers since then; in response to a request for comment from Scientific American, the department said that no air traffic controllers were cut by DOGE.
Still, “the recent accidents, close calls and equipment failures are not isolated events,” Meshkati says. “They are symptoms of how people, organizations and technology interact, and fail to interact, in an overstretched system.”
