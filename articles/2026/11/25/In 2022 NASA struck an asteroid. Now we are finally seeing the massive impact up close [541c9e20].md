# In 2022 NASA struck an asteroid. Now we are finally seeing the massive impact up close

Scientific American · Claire Cameron · 2026-11-25

In 2022 NASA smashed a spacecraft into an asteroid called Dimorphos to see what would happen—and now, some four years later, another space probe is finally revealing the effects of the impact up close.
On Wednesday the European Space Agency (ESA) released several new images of Dimorphos taken by Hera, a mission that launched in 2024 to study the asteroid in the impact’s aftermath. These observations offer new insights into how, in 2022, the Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) indelibly changed Dimorphos, a moonlet satellite of the larger asteroid Didymos. The impact altered Dimorphos’s orbit around Didymos and the entire binary system’s orbit around the sun, demonstrating, for the first time, that such a strategy could perhaps keep Earth safe from any asteroids found on a collision course with our planet.
The ESA images reveal that the DART impact left a massive crater on Dimorphos, which is itself a mere 151 meters in diameter. “The face struck by DART has been flattened into an impact crater the size of a sports stadium, which extends across the asteroid’s entire width,” said Hera principal investigator Patrick Michel in a statement. Until now, it was unclear if the DART mission had left a small crater or if it had reshaped the asteroid entirely, Michel said at a press conference on Wednesday.

“It’s really strange because you have a big crater, and the rest seems not really perturbed, so we are perturbed actually,” Michel said. “Scientists are perturbed,” added Ian Carnelli, Hera mission lead.
The images also show that after DART struck Dimorphos, the little asteroid has been “tumbling wildly” in its orbit around Didymos, said Michael Küppers, ESA Hera mission scientist, in the same statement. In fact, Dimorphos appears to be the most chaotically rotating object in the solar system, Michel said at the Wednesday press event.
Hera launched to space in October 2024. Accompanied by two small CubeSats, the mission is designed to assess how DART altered Dimorphos and its structure, as well as its composition and gravity. These data will help refine planetary defense models. The Hera mission has also found hundreds of boulders in the binary system that the team previously did not know about, adding targets for future observation.
During Wednesday’s press conference, officials discussed how, in Earth’s past, catastrophic asteroid impacts had led to planetary-scale mass extinctions, most famously eradicating the nonavian dinosaurs at the end of the Cretaceous period.
“We just heard the story that happened 66 million years ago,” Carnelli said. “Clearly, that day dinosaurs had a very bad day, but they didn’t have a space agency. So today we are a better species. We have wonderful space agencies, and we know what to do.”
