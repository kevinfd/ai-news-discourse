# FCC clears SpaceX to put up to 1 million AI satellites in orbit

Mashable · Olivia Tauber · 2026-11-25

FCC clears SpaceX to put up to 1 million AI satellites in orbit
An AI chatbot may be able to answer a question in seconds, but the chips doing the work need a steady supply of electricity and a way to get rid of all the heat they generate. SpaceX wants to put some of those chips on satellites and power them with sunlight. In outer space.
On Wednesday, Nov. 25, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) gave Elon Musk's SpaceX conditional approval to deploy up to 1 million satellites for its Starmind project, a network of orbital data centers that would process information for use back on Earth. According to the FCC, it's "the first authorization in U.S. history for a constellation of data centers in space."
When could SpaceX launch its AI satellites?
SpaceX says the factory it’s building in Bastrop, Texas, could start turning out satellites for launch as early as late 2027. The FCC's deadline for the first one is Nov. 25, 2033. By then, SpaceX has to have a Starmind satellite in its assigned orbit and running.

Two years later, the FCC expects 100,000 satellites to be operating. That's nearly nine times as many satellites as SpaceX's Starlink network has in orbit today. The next deadlines are 500,000 by Nov. 2038 and the full million by Nov. 2040. Under the terms of the order, missing those deadlines would void the authorization.
SpaceX is counting on its Starship rocket to carry the heavy satellites into orbit in large numbers. Amazon Leo, which is building a rival to Starlink, called the proposal a "lofty ambition rather than a real plan" in a March petition. Amazon argued it could take SpaceX centuries to put a million satellites in orbit. The FCC rejected Amazon's petition, along with separate challenges from Viasat and the American Astronomical Society.
How would a data center work in space?

SpaceX wants the satellites themselves to do the computing. Each one would carry AI chips, solar panels to power them, and radiators to release the heat the chips generate. In some orbits, the panels could collect sunlight nearly around the clock. The satellites would use lasers to pass data between one another and send results back to Earth through Starlink.
In August (pre-Jensen Huang drama), Nvidia said SpaceX plans to adapt its Vera Rubin computing system for the first generation of satellites, "bringing the first CPU built for AI agents to one of the world’s most ambitious AI deployments." (CPU stands for central processing unit.)

One of the questions for any data center in space is how well its chips will hold up over time. Google is testing that with Project Suncatcher, a prototype satellite that launched on a SpaceX rocket in October. The chip has already run an AI model in orbit and sent back a haiku about being in space. ("Blue marble below, Data streams in silent grace, Starlight feeds my core.") Google is now testing how the hardware handles radiation and temperature swings.
What could it mean for the night sky?
The same sunlight that would power the satellites could also make them visible from the ground.
The American Astronomical Society said its preliminary simulations suggest that, if SpaceX deploys the full million, tens of thousands could be visible to the naked eye around the world. Reflected sunlight could also leave bright streaks in telescope images and make faint objects harder to see. How much the sky actually changes would depend on details like the satellites' size, materials, and orbits.
The FCC said SpaceX's commitment to work with the National Science Foundation was enough to address the optical astronomy concerns raised in the proceeding. But Starlink has already left its mark on telescope images. At California's Zwicky Transient Facility, for example, researchers found Starlink streaks in 18 percent of images taken at twilight in Aug. 2021, up from less than half a percent in late 2019. A separate study published this year found that several newer Starlink designs were typically dimmer than the originals but still brighter than the goal SpaceX agreed to work toward.
There's also the question of what happens when the satellites are done working. Under current FCC rules, satellites at those altitudes have to come back through Earth's atmosphere at the end of their missions. SpaceX asked for the option to move some retired satellites into other orbits, including around the sun. The FCC put that request on hold and says SpaceX has to submit a final plan for limiting space debris before deploying any Starmind satellites that would operate above 600 kilometers (or about 373 miles).
If Starmind reaches the scale SpaceX has in mind, getting satellites out of orbit safely will be as much a part of running the network as getting them up there. And the launch schedule alone is a tall order. Even if SpaceX gets half a million satellites running by Nov. 2038, it would have just two years to get another half million into orbit and operational.
