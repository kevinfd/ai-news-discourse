# How To Scale AI Without Sacrificing Human Agency

Forbes · Yuri Misnik, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-25

Yuri Misnik, Chief Technology Officer at inDrive.
The arrival of agentic AI has forced a long‑overdue conversation. As autonomous systems begin to reason, plan and act on behalf of users, what does it mean to keep a human in the loop when the loop itself is being redesigned?
For those of us building consumer technology, this is a pressing question. The decisions we make about where AI should advise, automate and step back will shape the relationship between billions of users and the digital services they rely on daily. I believe the most durable competitive advantage for companies deploying AI in the years ahead will hinge on the decisions they make today about how to strategically preserve—and in many cases expand—human agency.
My core principle is this: Automate the repeatable, create insights, and assist humans in making decisions on the irreversible. If a wrong decision can be cheaply corrected, automation is the right tool. If it could destroy trust, livelihood or safety, human judgment should stay in control. Our ultimate goal shouldn’t be systems that decide for people, but systems that help people decide more efficiently.
Technology Should Inform, Not Dictate
The most powerful applications of AI remove friction, so people can make decisions more effectively. Consider dynamic pricing in ride‑hailing—traditional surge pricing shows riders a multiplier with no insight into context, and no chance to respond. An alternative uses the same contextual model—supply, demand, distance, traffic and time of day, but it surfaces it as a suggestion, not a verdict. The rider sees a recommended price but can propose their own, taking into account personal factors, which the driver can then accept or counter.
The same logic applies elsewhere. A banking app can use AI to analyze a customer’s profile, surfacing relevant products and helping educate customers before they commit. Hiring platforms can leverage AI to screen candidates and highlight strong matches, but leave judgements about culture fit to industry professionals. The role of AI should be to analyze large datasets and help narrow the field, while leaving the final call to humans.
Let AI Handle The Routine—And Stop There
The second responsible use of AI is to absorb repetitive, error-prone work, freeing humans for higher-value, judgment-driven tasks.
This makes it an excellent tool for customer support, for example, with AI doing a great job at handling routine first-line queries and cutting hold times. In our own operations at inDrive, roughly 40% of first-line customer support conversations are now fully automated, freeing agents for more complex cases, such as safety, where human judgment is irreplaceable.
Similarly, HSBC has leveraged its generative AI assistant to support around 3 million client interactions annually, helping teams handle higher volumes while improving client experience. Further afield in Central Asia, TBC Uzbekistan has taken the same approach, using AI to automate repetitive tasks like payment reminder calls and launching an AI assistant that helps customers navigate banking options faster.
Scale Safety Without Surrendering Accountability
Customer support is closely related to trust, and AI’s ability to operate at a scale and speed humans can’t match renders it a powerful tool to help strengthen it.
In ride‑hailing, for example, we now use AI to assist with driver verification at scale. inDrive processes 67% of all car photographs and 98% of drivers’ profile avatars with AI to ensure compliance. We also use natural‑language models, trained on millions of messages, to screen more than two‑thirds of in‑app chats for inappropriate behavior to protect riders and drivers from harmful conversations.
But here too, I believe automation should act as a filter. When a situation becomes ambiguous, the judgment call should always go to a human. Permanent account bans, dispute resolution, appeals and decisions about how we operate in new regulatory environments should remain with humans, because the context is richer than what the data can capture, and the cost of getting it wrong is borne by someone whose livelihood is at stake.
The Discipline Of Restraint
When thinking about the “human-in-the-loop” dilemma, I focus on the uniquely human capabilities and how we can best support them with AI. Some companies think that “human in the loop” is simply a phase they’ll eventually automate away, but I believe this is wrong.
Recent 2026 industry surveys, including from Gartner, McKinsey and IDC, put enterprise AI-agent adoption near 79%, yet only around 11% of deployments have reached production. Perhaps explaining the gap, McKinsey’s 2026 research on responsible AI found that security and governance concerns, not regulation or technical limits, are the leading reason nearly two-thirds of companies hold agentic AI back. A separate 2026 analysis of enterprise AI readiness found that just 37% of organizations trust AI enough to let it act autonomously, even though 48% already trust its accuracy. The models are ready long before most organizations are prepared to fully trust them.
Human in the loop, properly understood, is the architecture of trust, and building that architecture at the scale of millions of daily decisions, each carrying different stakes, is a genuine engineering problem. What will separate enduring platforms is the discipline to deploy AI where it genuinely augments people, and to hold back where it would diminish them. Making the right call about this is the toughest part.
