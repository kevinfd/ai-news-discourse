# Solving Enterprise AI’s Signature Problem

Forbes · Siri Swahari, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-25

Siri Swahari is VP of US Global Technology Operations at CGI, author of ThinkIn4D, and founder of WEC, Women Executives Circle.
Every AI tool license request I review comes with a manager’s endorsement. Before access is approved, a name is attached to the request.
That endorsement establishes sponsorship. It also raises a question many organizations have yet to design for: Who stands behind the work the tool will produce?
Approving access, validating a result and authorizing an action are different decisions. When AI drafts a client proposal, summarizes a compliance report or recommends a contract price, an organization needs to know who can approve the result and what that person needs to verify it.
Someone has to stand behind the output. That is the signature problem.
Enterprise AI adoption requires more than demonstrating that a model can perform a task. Consequential work needs an accountable owner, credible evidence and a clear route for handling exceptions. Those requirements help explain why an impressive demonstration can remain far from a dependable business capability.
The Unsigned Economy
Consumer AI made experimentation easy. Type a prompt, inspect the result and try again. There was little distance between curiosity and a useful experience.
For many everyday uses, that is enough. An image concept, a dinner idea or a first draft can be discarded with little consequence. I think of this as the unsigned economy: work that can remain provisional without requiring someone to attest to its reliability.
The distinction is about consequence. Consumer uses can carry serious risks, and enterprises also have plenty of low-stakes work. The signature becomes important wherever an AI result creates a commitment, affects another person or triggers an action that is difficult to undo.
Consumer adoption still offers enterprises two useful lessons. Every unnecessary login, approval layer and clunky interface taxes usage. Visible value matters just as much: People need to experience how a tool helps them. A mandate provides little motivation when the workflow remains cumbersome.
Those lessons can encourage trial and engagement. Moving into consequential work adds another design requirement: The people accountable for a result must have a workable way to evaluate it. That adoption barrier deserves the same attention as cost, integration and business value.
When The Output Is An Action
An AI-generated proposal offers a checkpoint before someone sends it. An agent authorized to send messages, pay invoices or provision access can move directly from output to consequence.
With generation, someone signs the output. With agents, someone also signs the authority.
That means deciding which actions the system may take, within what limits, when it must pause and who can intervene. Accountability extends beyond an individual approval to the permissions, monitoring and recovery arrangements governing the system’s behavior.
In June 2025, Gartner forecast that more than 40% of agentic AI projects would be canceled by the end of 2027 because of rising costs, uncertain business value or insufficient risk controls.
The forecast encompasses several implementation problems. Unclear authority is one vulnerability leaders can address before deployment. If an agent takes an action outside its intended scope, the organization should already know who can stop it, assess the consequences and authorize recovery.
Consider an agent that processes supplier payments. Its operating authority might cover verified invoices within an approved limit, while changes to bank details or unusual transactions require human review. That review must happen before payment. A record of who approved the tool months earlier provides little help at that decision point.
Designing For Signature
Accountability becomes useful when it is built into the work. Four design choices deserve attention.
1. Naming The Accountable Owner Before Deployment
For each AI-assisted workflow, identify the business owner, the people authorized to approve consequential decisions and the team responsible for operating the system. Define how they handle exceptions and resolve disagreements.
The employee entering a prompt may lack the authority or expertise to accept the resulting business risk. Assign responsibility at the level where people can exercise it, and give them a clear escalation route. A name on a workflow has limited value without the authority to act.
2. Making The Data Behind The Result Traceable
Anyone approving an AI-generated result needs access to the evidence supporting it. That includes the sources used, their currency and any material gaps or conflicting information.
For example, a reviewer assessing a contract price needs to know whether the recommendation used current rates and approved terms. A fluent explanation from the model is insufficient evidence. Data lineage and validation make the review possible; they also help teams investigate when something goes wrong.
3. Matching Autonomy To Consequence
Give AI greater freedom where errors are inexpensive and recoverable. Introduce stronger controls when decisions affect people, commit funds or create obligations that are difficult to reverse.
For agents, translate that principle into specific permissions, transaction limits and conditions that trigger a pause. Define which actions require advance approval and how authority can be withdrawn. Review those boundaries as the workflow and its risks change.
4. Giving The Signer A Workable Decision
Adding an approval box to a crowded workflow invites rubber-stamping. Put the checkpoint where a decision becomes consequential, and give the reviewer enough information and time to assess it.
A meaningful signature requires access to evidence, authority to challenge the result and a clear path to stop or escalate the action. People also need permission to withhold approval when the evidence is incomplete. Measure whether the review catches meaningful problems and supports sound decisions, alongside how quickly it is completed.
Making Accountability Work
The signature problem is a practical test of enterprise readiness. Can the person accountable for the result explain the basis for accepting it? Can they identify the limits of the system’s authority? Can they intervene when those limits are reached?
Low friction and visible value remain essential to adoption. Clear ownership and usable controls make it possible to carry that value into consequential work. Designing them together gives employees a basis for trusting the system and leaders a basis for expanding its use.
Before you deploy the agent, decide who signs for the action.
Build for the signature.
