# The U.S. Army is blowing up five tons of toxic lead azide in Iowa

Scientific American · Claire Cameron · 2026-11-25

The U.S. Army detonated five tons of lead azide, used in munition manufacturing, at a site in Iowa on Wednesday. Any toxic material released by the blast could extend for miles, affecting parts of Iowa and neighboring Missouri, and would pose a significant danger to people's health, experts say.
Officials said prior to the blast that they anticipated “zero release” of toxins because of mitigation efforts. Two controlled explosions occurred at 10 A.M. EST. A New York Times reporter on the scene said he saw a plume of smoke rise into the sky and drift south. It is unclear whether any toxic material escaped at this time, but early reports suggest the material was largely contained at the site. Independent scientists are also trying to verify whether any lead exposure occurred.
An Environmental Protection Agency site tracking lead concentration in the air around the blast zone had so far failed to detect the element as of noon on Wednesday. Iowa’s governor-elect Rob Sand said in a social media post that, by all indications, “Iowans are safe” but that the “lead-up to the detonation should never have been so chaotic and unclear,” adding that “Iowans ... are understandably alarmed and scared.”

“Lead can cause irreversible neurological effects and has been linked to increased miscarriages,” says Patrick McNamara, an engineering professor at Marquette University. “To put into perspective, lead pills were used as abortion pills years ago.”
Residents in the surrounding area had been urged to evacuate prior to the explosion. Any remaining members of the public were told to block and secure their windows and doors to prevent any potential released lead from entering their homes.
The Army has estimated that exposure to any toxic material could last about one hour after the explosions. State officials recommended that any residents in the potential blast zone to stay inside with their windows and doors closed for at least 24 hours. McNamara recommends anyone nearby should do whatever they can to avoid bringing lead indoors, such as by taking off outdoor shoes and clothes before entering their homes. “Lead does not go away; it only moves around,” he says.
The explosion was necessary, the Army said, because the 68 drums of lead azide had degraded in storage, forming unstable crystals that could explode spontaneously at room temperature. It was so unstable that the material could not be moved, touched or left in place. In a blog post for Science, chemist Derek Lowe pointed out that crystallization is a “foreseeable problem” and suggested that the lead azide wasn’t stored properly in the first place. “The larger picture is that someone, or a series of someones over time, has grievously screwed up,” he wrote.
Any lead dust released by the explosion would pose a danger to public health, especially for children and pregnant women. “Lead is a neurotoxin, which is particularly nasty because it acts as an impostor. Your body mistakes it for what it needs, like calcium, and it jams all sorts of nerve signals,” explained epidemiologist Katelyn Jetelina in a recent newsletter post about the explosion. “There is no safe level because scientists have never found a threshold below which lead stops doing harm.”
The Army built a mitigation zone around the lead azide in the hope of containing any such dust, including tanks of water and foam blocks, as well as containment screens. Still, the Army released maps showing the potential path of any dust plume should any of the material have escaped.
EPA head Lee Zeldin said on Tuesday, ahead of the explosion, that the agency would monitor the area’s water, air and soil for signs of contamination in the aftermath of the blast.
“I would be as concerned about lead settling onto soil on someone’s property as I would it getting into waterways and crops,” McNamara says. “A lot of lead exposure in urban areas comes from soil, paint chips and old materials. If lead is on soil on someone’s property, it presents a direct route of exposure for children. All folks in this area should consider testing their blood lead levels over time.”
