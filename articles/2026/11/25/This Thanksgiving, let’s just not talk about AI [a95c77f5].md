# This Thanksgiving, let’s just not talk about AI

Fast Company · Mark Sullivan · 2026-11-25

Maybe we just don’t talk about AI this Thanksgiving.
Of course, we need some common conversation topics to throw around the dining room with friends and relatives we haven’t seen in a while. And though AI seems to be showing up everywhere now, we still have a lot of other stuff to talk about—sports, food, movies, and the like. Or there’s always that thing where you go around the table and everybody says something they’re thankful for. Unless somebody actually says “Claude” (which is fine).
Mostly there are good reasons to avoid the subject of AI at your gathering this year.
- Too political. Talking politics in mixed company at the Thanksgiving table can have unpredictable, sometimes explosive results. AI has become very political, mainly because of AI data centers. A surprising number of midterm voters cited an aversion to new AI data centers as the main rationale for their swing toward Democrats. Studies have shown that data centers do indeed contribute to rising energy costs, not to mention the noise pollution they produce. In fact, data centers are already so prevalent that there’s a decent chance one of your Thanksgiving guests lives near one. (You may recognize them by the way they keep saying, “Huh? What? Huh?”) Sore subject.
- Too technical. The last major tech wave, mobile computing, was pretty easy to conceptualize. They turned cellphones into internet devices with big screens and made computer programs called apps to run on them. AI is different. Very few laypeople can explain how a neural network is trained and how it operates. Even PhDs don’t know exactly how AI models develop new skills, some of which have come as a complete surprise. That creates a problem for Thanksgiving conversation. Somebody at the table who listens to a lot of podcasts may seize the moment to nerd-splain AI to everybody. Or worse, they may go on and on about how LLMs are just stochastic parrots—word-guessing machines that will never be capable of anything more. Pedantry, jargon, and boredom will waft through the air around the table. With luck, the speaker will take the blank stares looking back at him as a signal to put a turkey leg in his mouth.
- Too painful. Somebody at the table might own a bunch of Nvidia or SpaceX stock and get depressed. Or somebody might remind everybody that their retirement accounts are already bound up in risky AI-dependent stocks like SpaceX. Someone may gaze up from their stuffing with a faraway look and recall those simpler times when Jensen Huang was a one-woman man and everybody believed that all the AI companies buying up Nvidia GPUs would actually be able to pay for them, with nary a revenue stream to match. Heady days! They are over. Nvidia has lost 20% of its value this year because maybe bankrolling your profit-free customers the cash to buy your product isn’t the most sustainable business plan. So just don’t bring it up; heavy drinking may ensue.
- Too personal. Don’t ask people what they use AI for. It can lead to some bad places. Just last year, a 22-year-old sitting across the table from me began talking about all the personal stuff she discussed with ChatGPT. I quickly tuned her out, of course, but there was something in there about an antifungal cream, a GLP-1 “crisis,” and a neurodivergent ex-boyfriend.
- Too anxiety-inducing. Somebody in the house Thursday may have that soon-to-be-unemployed vibe about them, and it’s quite possible that an AI has already learned to do their job. Challenger, Gray & Christmas found that AI was the top-stated reason for U.S. job cuts through October. Soon that person will be spending their days in the uncanny valley, doing video interviews with wooden-faced AI avatars, only to be passed over (and over) by a candidate-screening algorithm that was offended by their Zoom background.
On a more serious, and more anxious, note, whether people realize it or not, our relationship with AI is pretty different from what it was at this time last year. The models aren’t perfect, or even trustworthy in some applications, but they’ve clearly become more intelligent.
We hear about teams of AI agents working tirelessly on big problems, coding agents building entire apps, and models solving some of the world’s hardest math problems. We may be headed for a world where we coexist with coequal, nonbiological intelligences that are generally as smart as humans. Discussing where this is all headed can make for an uncomfortable conversation, and not one suitable for a holiday gathering. I’d leave it be this Thanksgiving. Turkey legs and existential angst just aren’t a good mix.
The trouble is, AI has seeped into everything, raising the constant question: How is AI affecting this? So you must listen closely for signs of conversational drift toward the AI thing and have some safe, go-to topics ready to throw in. Like these:
- Remember the World Cup this summer? Wasn’t that nice? For the first time in a while, I felt a tinge of real national pride as I watched the crowds of foreign football fans having a ball alongside Americans in our cities and stadiums. Americans got rave reviews for their hospitality—everybody from Uber drivers to hotel staff to strangers who helped our international guests get around. My favorites were those videos of Europeans discovering the simple joy of chicken tenders, pizza, and french fries slathered in ranch dressing.
- Returning to the moon. In April, NASA’s Artemis II mission sent a crew of astronauts on a loop around the moon, taking them 252,756 miles from Earth—farther than any humans had ever traveled. They spotted a crater on the lunar surface and named it after the crew commander’s late wife, Carroll.
- The return of the jaguar. A jaguar nicknamed “Cinco” is roaming southern Arizona’s mountains, only the fifth jaguar seen there since monitoring began 15 years ago. Researchers say it’s the latest sign that the endangered species may be gaining a foothold in the region. They also say that Cinco has a distinctive mark on its face that resembles Groucho Marx’s mustache.
- Beating cancer. For the first time, 70% of Americans diagnosed with cancer survive at least five years, the American Cancer Society says. In the mid-1970s, only half did.
- Fewer overdose deaths. U.S. overdose deaths fell about 14% in 2025, the third straight annual drop and the longest decline in decades, according to provisional data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.
And finally, Costco’s hot dog and soda combo still costs $1.50, the same as it did in 1985. You can thank me later. Happy Thanksgiving.
