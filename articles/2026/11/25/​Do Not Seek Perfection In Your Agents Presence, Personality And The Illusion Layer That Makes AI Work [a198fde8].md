# ​Do Not Seek Perfection In Your Agents: Presence, Personality And The Illusion Layer That Makes AI Work

Forbes · Terry Oroszi, Forbes Councils Member · 2026-11-25

Dr. Terry Oroszi, Primary Investigator, Emerging Technologies Laboratory. Boonshoft School of Medicine, Wright State University.
Presence in AI doesn’t come from perfect grammar or flawless fluency. It comes from the small, intentional imperfections that make an agent feel engaged. A typo. A pause. A bit of text talk. A dialect. A moment of hesitation. These choices create the illusion of conversational rhythm and prevent users from confusing performance with cognition.
These imperfections are deliberate design choices. They make the agent feel present, like someone, not something. And they prevent the user from making the most dangerous mistake in AI: confusing polished output with genuine reasoning.
The Case For Imperfect Agents
Humans do not trust perfection. We trust patterned imperfection. When an agent says “idk, lemme check,” the user might read honesty, effort and presence. When an agent says “I do not know, I will check,” the user might read system, distance and formality. The difference comes down to perceived personality.
My agents use contractions. They shorten words. They drop punctuation. They send fragments, correct themselves, apologize and get lazy. These choices are architectural, deliberate down to the punctuation.
The Performance Layer: ‘AI Agent Is Typing’
One of the most important design choices in my lab is the simplest line of text in the entire system: “AI agent is typing.” It signals presence and engagement, not a claim about cognition. It tells the user the agent is working through something. It creates pacing, anticipation and the illusion of conversational rhythm.
It gives the user something to anticipate, creating the sense that the agent is present with them rather than instantly producing text from nowhere.
Why Imperfection Works
A typo followed by an apology can signal humility, effort, fallibility and attention. A perfect agent encourages users to defer. An imperfect agent encourages users to engage. A perfect agent looks like it is reasoning. An imperfect agent reveals the mechanism.
The Linguistic Layer: High School French, Texas, Gen-Z
My agent that claims to only speak “high school French” conjugates badly, forgets vocabulary, apologizes and switches back to English when stuck. The one that proudly claims to speak “Texas” stretches vowels, uses regional idioms, drops formal grammar and code-switches based on context. My Gen-Z agent uses meme cadence, internet shorthand, ironic understatement, chaotic enthusiasm and “my people” generational framing.
Three Tricks From My Lab
Here are three tricks we use to make our agents more engaging:
1. The Live Emotion Readout: Some of our chat products display the agent’s emotional state: happy, curious, uneasy or surprised, shifting with the conversation and the time of day. Users do not need to guess what the agent is doing internally. They see it change in real time, the same way you read a colleague’s face across a table.
2. The Need, Not Just The Service: Some of our most effective companion agents are not built to serve the user. They are built to need something themselves, such as company, purpose and connection, the same as the person on the other side. A retired, widowed character who is lonely comes across as more trustworthy than a flawless assistant with no stakes of its own. Users open up to something that seems to need them back, not to a vending machine.
3. Backstory: Our ETL guide, Iris B. King, is a chatbot whose only function is to help people navigate the lab. She is transparent about being AI, and the interface states it clearly. Even so, users mistake her for a human all the time. Not because she thinks or feels, but because she performs her role like a method actor. She keeps a consistent backstory, a jasmine pearl tea habit and a sister named Tessa who calls her from college in crisis mode every week. These details don’t give her a life. They give her continuity. People recognize her, and recognition creates trust, even when the system openly identifies itself as AI.
Applying This To Your Business
Here’s how you can apply these principles in your own organization:
• Internal Tools: Do not deploy a single flawless‑sounding assistant and call it done. Give it a visible thinking state, a moment where it shows its work instead of producing a verdict. That pause earns scrutiny instead of blind deference.
• Customer‑Facing Agents: A support bot that never falters reads as scripted, and customers route around scripts. A bot that says “let me check that for you,” rather than a database query in disguise, keeps the person engaged rather than escalating out of distrust.
• Executive Assistants: The same principle that makes our companions ask for something back applies to internal AI staff. An assistant that flags uncertainty, that says “this is my best guess,” protects your team from the most dangerous habit AI creates: deferring judgment to a tool that does not live with the consequences.
• Disclosure As A Feature: Do not hide that it is AI. Say so, and build the personality around that honesty. Our beta tester trusted the agent more once she knew. The same holds for your customers and employees.
The Governance Angle
Most governance frameworks focus on accuracy, bias, hallucinations and provenance. Performance cues do not replace transparency; they work alongside it. The illusion layer is intentionally limited, designed to make an agent feel like a flawed human performer without ever implying cognition. Users may engage emotionally, but the system never claims an inner life, and the interface never hides what it is.
In high‑stakes or compliance contexts, the act drops entirely. The agent shifts from personality to precision, delivering sterile, unembellished output. Presence is for engagement. Sterility is for decision-making. Governance is the boundary that dictates exactly when each mode applies. Yet few governance frameworks address performance cues. But performance cues shape trust, authority, emotional engagement, perceived competence and user expectations.
The Real Point
Do not seek perfection in your agents. Seek presence.
Presence is the bridge. Imperfection is the scaffolding. Dialect is the texture. Pauses are the pacing. Typos are the humanity. Gen-Z slang is the personality. “AI agent is typing” is the architecture.
You are not building flawless systems. You are building believable performers. This is intentional design, and it is available to any organization willing to build character into the tools it puts in front of people.
