# AWS CFO John Felton explains the thinking behind Amazon’s $220 billion AI spending strategy

Business Insider · Eugene Kim, Eugene Kim (ekim@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-25

Amazon Web Services CFO John Felton Amazon AWS CFO John Felton says the company commits early to land, and power, but buys servers later. Felton says AWS plans for multiple demand scenarios because "there is no perfect forecast." Felton says AWS must move fast in AI: "Speed is a choice." Amazon expects record capital expenditure of roughly $220 billion this year, much of it for AWS and AI infrastructure. In an email interview with Business Insider, AWS CFO John Felton said the company commits early to long-lead assets such as land, power, and permits, while delaying server purchases and other equipment until customer commitments offer a clearer demand signal. "We try to make the right decision at the last responsible moment," he said. He also explained why AWS believes the risk of moving too slowly in AI outweighs the risk of spending aggressively. "Speed is a choice," he said. "With AI, I think the answer is to go fast." Felton, who spent roughly 20 years in Amazon's retail business across finance and operations roles before moving to AWS, discussed how the company weighs AI capacity and returns, the lessons of its Covid-era logistics expansion, and his career progression. The following Q&A has been lightly edited for clarity. What principles guide you when Amazon should build ahead of demand and when it should hold back? We build to the demand we can see from customers. Their commitments tell us how much to build. Land, power and permitting take years, so we commit to those early. Everything we can buy later, we buy later, because waiting gives us more information and keeps our choices open. We try to make the right decision at the last responsible moment, using as much data as we can. If the demand is not there, we do not place the order. What have you learned from past expansion cycles about making capital-allocation decisions with limited information? We have learned that there is no perfect forecast, so you plan for several possible outcomes, and keep adjusting as the world changes. In the pandemic, customers and sellers needed us, so we built quickly. That showed us where we could do better, and we redesigned the network into regions, which cut our cost-to-serve and allowed us to speed up, getting packages to customers faster. In AWS, long-term assets, like land and power, are fungible and useful for many years, so we are comfortable committing to them early. We buy the rest once customers have committed. Then we watch our backlog and adjust. How would you describe the scale of Amazon's current infrastructure investment cycle? What makes this period different from earlier expansions? This is a very large investment, because the opportunity is very large. AI is moving faster than any technology we have seen, and it is still early. The signals we see, with customers committing and demand running ahead of what we can install, tell us the investment is justified. We are also seeing the revenue growth, the margins, and the paybacks. When we started AWS, nobody knew whether customers would use it. Now customers commit for years ahead, and our demand comes from millions of them, so we build with a clear view of what is coming. We're always looking at what customers want and need now, and what we think they'll want and need in the future — that's what guided our fulfillment center build-out and the expansion of our last-mile transportation network. We're always going to err on the side of the customer. What are your biggest assumptions and tradeoffs that shape AWS's capex plans? We make a lot of assumptions, but we are not trying to make trade-offs. Our goal is to do the right thing for the long term for customers, without having to choose between them. We assume customers keep growing with us, and their multi-year commitments are what we plan around. We also assume the assets we build keep earning for a long time, so the returns come over years. The risk of going slow is higher in AI than in earlier technology shifts, so we move fast. Our investment follows demand, and the goal is to match it. Beyond customer demand, how do factors such as power availability, permitting, community engagement, and the broader policy environment shape where and when AWS can add data center capacity? There is never just one constraint, and the constraints keep moving. Power, permitting, chips, memory, networking and optics are all part of it, and as soon as you solve one, the next one appears. Our role is to look around corners for what comes next and work to get in front of it. Community engagement is part of that planning from the start. We work with local and state officials and with residents, and we work hard to make sure every community where we build is better off because we are there. How have recent election outcomes changed your assumptions about spending? We work with whoever is in office, and aim to build a productive relationship with them so that we can advocate on behalf of our employees, our customers, and our business. Our approach has stayed the same since Amazon launched, whichever party has been in power. We focus on customers, we invent and we think long term. That is not going to change. Our plans follow what customers need. What indicators do you use to judge whether AWS's AI investments will generate substantial returns? The first thing we look at is whether customers are committing, because that tells us the demand is real. As of Q3, 2026, our backlog is $578 billion and growing triple digits, from customers of every size and every industry. Then we look at whether the investment is paying back and whether growth and margins are holding up, and AWS has accelerated for six straight quarters, most recently growing 39.3%. When all of those are moving the right way, we feel good about the return. What opportunities do you think investors most often miss when they evaluate the economics of AWS's AI infrastructure? The biggest opportunity is that AI and the rest of AWS grow together. Customers already run their applications and data on AWS, so they bring their AI here too. As their AI grows, they use more compute and storage, so the investment we make for AI also builds demand across the whole business. That is the flywheel, and it is why we like the economics. You have spent much of your Amazon career in operations rather than in a traditional CFO track. What do you think you see in a capital plan that a finance executive without that operating background might miss? I started in finance and led our operations finance team before I moved into running the operations business, so I have seen plans from both sides. At Amazon, finance leaders are meant to help run the business, and that means asking how we run it better and how we add value. My time in operations taught me how critical it is to have great people, great technology, and great processes, and that it works when you have all three. That helps me work with the teams who build our capacity. Before AWS, you helped regionalize Amazon's US delivery network. What lessons from that work apply to building AI infrastructure? Moving to a regionalized network showed us how much placement matters. When you put inventory closer to the customer, delivery gets faster and the network runs more efficiently, and we are seeing the benefits today. The same idea applies to infrastructure. Where capacity sits shapes how well it serves customers, so we plan where we build with the customer in mind. Looking back at Amazon's Covid-era warehouse plans, what did that experience teach you about the danger of overexpanding? We built fast during the pandemic because customers needed us, and we doubled a network that took 25 years to build. We grew into it and made the network more efficient, and moving to regions was a big part of that. That experience taught us to build against demand we can see. In AI, customers commit for years, and we buy servers only a few months before they go live. How was the transition to AWS CFO? I spent two decades across finance and operations, and two and a half years ago I was given the opportunity to come back into finance at AWS. It was a very easy yes. AWS is a transformative and super interesting part of Amazon, and I wanted the chance to dig in and understand the business. We knew AI was going to transform every part of our business, and I wanted to be part of that. Have you had a mentor at Amazon? What is the most important lesson you learned from that person? I have learned from a lot of people at Amazon. One of the most important lessons is that speed is a choice. There are times to go slow and times to go fast, and with AI, I think the answer is to go fast, because most of these decisions are ones we can come back from. Going fast means everyone on my team uses AI every day. I leave the specifics to them, because they know their jobs so much better than I do. They have embraced it and made it their own, for example, our finance organization cut the time to process a regulatory compliance document from 160 hours to 32, and that time now goes to higher-value decisions, and every week I see new launches and ideas I would never have thought of.
