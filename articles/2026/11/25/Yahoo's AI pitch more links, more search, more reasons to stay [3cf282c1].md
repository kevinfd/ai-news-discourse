# Yahoo's AI pitch: more links, more search, more reasons to stay

Business Insider · Peter Kafka, Peter Kafka (pkafka@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-25

Jim Lazone; BI Yahoo lost the search wars to Google decades ago. Now it has a new threat from AI. The bear case for Yahoo is that people who use it for sports and finance migrate to AI. CEO Jim Lanzone thinks he can use AI to improve his properties for his users and attract new ones. If you remember when Yahoo was an internet giant, you are not a young person. Yahoo was the original search king, but it lost that title to Google some 20 years ago, and then fell into steady decline. Jim Lanzone's job is not to turn Yahoo into a Google-killer — or a ChatGPT or Claude-killer, for that matter. Instead the CEO wants to take Yahoo's still-large user base, get them to keep using products like Yahoo mail, finance and sports, and give them reasons to spend even more time on his properties. When Lazone took over Yahoo in 2021, I figured he'd be running a private equity playbook for Apollo, the private equity firm that bought Yahoo and AOL in a fire sale from Verizon : Gut the workforce, sell off whatever he could, milk the remainder for cash. And he's done some of that, capped by selling off AOL to dot-com harvester Bending Spoons a year ago. But Lanzone insists that Yahoo has lots of growth left — even in the face of AI competition that is causing problems for all kinds of internet companies. On the newest episode of my Channels podcast, I talked to him about Yahoo's own entry into AI; why he thought Yahoo could be turned around when almost all internet second chances never pan out; and what he makes of today's streaming wars, given that he used to run digital for the company formerly known as CBS. You can hear that whole conversation here ; what follows is an edited excerpt of our chat. Peter Kafka: Let's talk about Scout , your entry into AI. What is it — a chatbot, or a search engine? Jim Lanzone: Specifically not a chatbot. It's an AI answer or search engine. We're using lightweight models against our own data. We're not a pass-through for a large language model. We have 30 years of search history, publisher relationships, vertical information, eventually personal information about you. We have a great knowledge graph and dataset to deliver answers. For the first time in 17 years, we own our own destiny in search again. We'd been outsourcing to Microsoft that whole time. Is the idea that ChatGPT has created this great search experience that people are flocking to, and you need to give Yahoo users a reason to stay? Or is it that a better search product can bring in new users? Both. When you deliver what people are looking for, frequency and retention grow. If you don't, it goes away. Search is changing fast because of AI, and we need that product or we would lose users. But if you do it well, they'll search with us more often. It's been a long time since people chose Yahoo as a standalone search engine relative to Google. They're the verb. That ship sailed a long time ago. But the next time people are on our property and we have a search box there, they'll be more likely to use it if the last time they got a really good experience. A lot of people say, "I hate AI. I don't want it shoved into everything." What do you say to that? That's because other properties have done it clumsily — shoving AI answers on top of 10 blue links, randomly pushing everything else down the page. It's not always what you're looking for. They also don't link out to publishers. One reason that matters is that it's the right thing to do for publishers, since that's where you're getting the answers. But it's also important for users: They need to see the sources, dive deeper, and verify the answer. If you do it right, AI search can be really helpful. But just doing it clumsily isn't. OpenAI and Anthropic don't have a Yahoo Finance or Yahoo Sports. Maybe they never will. But they have enormous resources. What stops them from doing that? Google launched Google Finance, and Yahoo Finance is still doing really well. The only way to defend yourself is to compete, innovate, and modernize. That fight is never-ending. It's always a war. You're doing licensing deals with publishers to use their content in AI. What is meaningfully different from the deals OpenAI has signed? In the case of the AI companies, it was after the fact. They'd already taken the content. Then it's: "Maybe we'll pay you. Is it worth what we're giving you? Who knows? Do you have any leverage? No." Theirs is a pure license. Ours is based on usage. But the higher-order bit is that publishers can't survive only on these licensing agreements. They have to get traffic. Otherwise, they're not going to exist, and we're not going to be able to get answers going forward. So this isn't just altruism: If AI companies don't send traffic to websites, the websites go away and there's no new content. Right. And the thing is, you're not asking for much, because it is better for users. And it's essential to monetization, because you can't have paid links without unpaid links. That's always been the social contract with publishers: We take a snippet, and then we link to you. Now it's answers.
