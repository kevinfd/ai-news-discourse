# We just created a million Jensen Huangs

Business Insider · Aki Ito, Aki Ito (aito@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-25

Nadja Wohlleben/Getty Images; Tyler Le/BI The pressure from Donald Trump was decisive, but even without it, I wonder if the resurrection of Jensen Huang was kind of inevitable. Since Nvidia announced its CEO's resignation on November 12, the company's stock had been in freefall, erasing about $1 trillion from the market. Investors clearly didn't believe in a Huang-less Nvidia. All it took was one announcement of his return , and ta-da — the company's stock soared 30%, almost returning to its pre-ouster level. The presence of a single man, according to the market, was worth a trillion dollars to the future of the world's second most valuable company. Huang clearly wanted it to be this way. Over the three decades he spent building Nvidia, he ran the business with an astonishing level of control. He had 60 people directly reporting to him, instead of trusting a handful of executives to run their own divisions. He had his 40,000 employees write out their weekly priorities, and he read and responded to many of those emails himself. "It's really important that I understand what everybody's doing," he once explained. This is exactly the kind of micromanaging that management textbooks tell you not to do. They say to build a company so it can outlast you, but Huang openly talked about not believing in succession planning. "Somebody asked me: 'Jensen, if you don't use conventional management techniques and organizational techniques, what's going to happen when you leave the company?'" he recounted earlier this year. "Well, when I die on the job — someday — I told them they'll just have to reshape the company for the next CEO." A common piece of advice for workers is to make themselves indispensable to their employers. Huang did that, and then some. He made Nvidia so dependent on him it couldn't possibly thrive without him. Some people might call this being a total control freak. Silicon Valley celebrates it as " founder mode ." Paul Graham, the cofounder of Y Combinator, popularized the term in 2024, crediting it to a talk given by the Airbnb founder and CEO Brian Chesky. Chesky had followed the traditional advice of delegating to deputies and ended with "disastrous" results (Graham disparagingly calls this "manager mode"). So he flipped to founder mode, inserting himself in the nitty-gritty details of the company the way Steve Jobs once did at Apple. Chesky had to grope his way toward this management philosophy through trial and error, but by all accounts this was the way Huang's always been. Huang himself has embraced the label. There's a reason founder mode has become a rallying cry for tech executives in recent years. CEOs always wanted to run their companies this way, but they just couldn't. White-collar work required the coordination of too many people for the CEO to have their eyes on everything, so they needed an elaborate chain of command. They didn't have the bandwidth to meddle in everything. CEOs — especially founder CEOs — are going to become even more untouchable than they already were. Now, AI is changing that, by making it possible for companies to operate with fewer managers. Part of that is because these tools allow companies to operate with fewer employees , which means they need fewer people to supervise them. It's also because AI is taking on some of the work that managers traditionally did, like coordinating and monitoring the work of their employees and answering their questions. That opens up the bandwidth of managers to oversee more workers, and reduces the levels of management an organization needs. AI can do the same for CEOs. If Huang once took the time to read a hundred of those employee emails every morning to check for early warning signs, he can now have agents read every single one of them for him. That brings the CEO closer to the employees doing the day-to-day work, making it possible for them to be a lot more steeped in the details. Over the last year, I've spoken to so many startup founders who are ecstatic about this. Many of their reorg memos say they want to empower rank-and-file employees to make their own decisions, but I'm skeptical. In the past, when companies flattened their organizations, it largely had the opposite effect: It consolidated power at the top. "CEOs flatten to achieve more control," one researcher concluded. I think all of this means that in the future, we're going to have a lot more CEOs like Jensen Huang, who have their tentacles so deep in the business that you can't cleanly remove them without crippling the entire company. And that means CEOs — especially founder CEOs — are going to become more untouchable than they already were. When so much depends on that single person, how are boards possibly going to fire them for even the gravest of misconduct ? Huang proved that you can have affairs with a suspected Chinese spy and allegedly with an employee, mislead your deputies and your board about those affairs, and have horrifying news stories come out about how you threatened to pee on an employee's brain — and still get to run your company. It's the kind of ironclad job security that workers in today's economy can only dream of. No wonder so many founders petitioned to bring Huang back, and cheered when he got reinstated. "The mistake has been corrected," Elon Musk wrote. "Jensen!" posted Sam Altman, who himself clawed back his job at OpenAI three years ago. Travis Kalanick, who was less fortunate when he was ousted from Uber in 2017 for a series of scandals — but who's back at it with a new startup that's raised $1.7 billion — also posted one word upon Huang's return: "Thanksgiving."
