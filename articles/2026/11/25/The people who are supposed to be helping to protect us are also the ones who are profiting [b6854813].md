# "The people who are supposed to be helping to protect us are also the ones who are profiting."

Axios · Maria Curi · 2026-11-25

Maryland Gov. Wes Moore (D), a potential 2028 presidential contender, asserted in an interview with "The Axios Show" that Washington is leaving Americans vulnerable to AI because of billionaire greed and clout. He said voters hate the technology because they fear losing control. Moore, chair of the National Governors Association, told Mike Allen this week that after an " AI earthquake " helped propel Democrats to huge midterm gains, Congress must treat oversight of the technology as an "emergency" for the first 100 hours of Democratic control. Why it matters: Moore, 48, focused on AI early, with an executive order in 2024 . He has used his NGA leadership roles to work across the aisle on AI protections, arguing that Marylanders can't afford to wait for Washington and shouldn't have to wait for a disaster . Moore, Maryland's first Black governor , is a former Army paratrooper who deployed to combat in Afghanistan with the 82nd Airborne Division, and was a Rhodes Scholar and White House fellow. What they're saying: Moore, speaking at the Maryland State House in Annapolis, makes it clear in this episode of "The Axios Show" that AI will be a huge focus of the 2028 presidential race after becoming a flashpoint in November's midterms. Among the governor's key points: On the pace of regulation: "We are watching a fast-moving technology that is being protected at a snail's pace." On why people hate AI: "Genesis 1 talks about how God gave humanity dominion over all land, air and sea. And that dominion was given to humanity, not to robots." On whether regulating AI lets China win: "This is not a question of U.S. versus China. This is a question of man versus machine." On a bubble: "We are in a bubble because you cannot have that much influence by such a small group of companies." On Nvidia and Trump: "It seems like it's rules for me, but not for thee. ... We are talking about not just millionaires, but billionaires who are sitting in a quiet room by themselves making this decision." Zoom in: Moore in September laid out his framework for AI policy, aimed at regulating frontier AI developers, shielding workers from surveillance and protecting children. His priorities include: Ensuring regulations keep pace with rapidly advancing technology. Holding companies responsible for releasing dangerous AI models. Building on Maryland's efforts to protect people's name, image and likeness from AI misuse, including in political advertising. Earlier this year, Moore signed a law targeting deceptive AI in campaign ads, including images, audio and video. The big picture: Governors across the political spectrum, from California's Gavin Newsom and New York's Kathy Hochul (both Democrats) to Utah's Spencer Cox and Florida's Ron DeSantis (both Republicans), are positioning themselves as filling the regulatory vacuum left by Washington. Moore is linking AI policy to a broader Democratic message about corruption and the influence of money in politics. "The people who are supposed to be helping to protect us are also the ones who are profiting," the governor told us. "It's difficult to hear when Congress is talking about what they're going to do to protect us when we know that they are trading stocks." Watch the episode ...
