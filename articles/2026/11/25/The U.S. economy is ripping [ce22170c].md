# The U.S. economy is ripping

Axios · Neil Irwin · 2026-11-25

Wall Street has been reeling amid fears about the future of the AI boom. But in the here and now, the U.S. economy is experiencing an outright boom. The big picture: Growth was even stronger this summer than it appeared, and the robust expansion is continuing this fall, according to a range of data released Wednesday morning. That is pushing Americans' incomes up and the pace of layoffs down. The strong data helps explain why the Federal Reserve is prepared to raise interest rates two weeks from now even in the face of financial market turbulence. Driving the news: The U.S. economy grew at a 4.3% annual rate in the July-through-September quarter, according to the second revision to GDP data — well above the 3.4% originally reported. The strong results have continued into Q4. The Commerce Department separately said that personal income rose 0.7% in October, well ahead of analysts' expectations. Overall orders for durable goods came in below forecasts for October, but core capital goods orders — a forward-looking measure of investment spending — climbed 1.2%, also well ahead of projections. The Labor Department said that only 189,000 people filed new claims for jobless benefits last week, matching the lowest of 2026 and among the lowest readings on record (claims go back to 1967). Zoom out: The Q3 GDP reading was powered by business investment, especially for software, showing that the AI buildout remains a powerful driver of overall economic growth. It wasn't driven by volatile components of GDP like inventory and trade swings; real final sales to private domestic purchasers surged at a whopping 5.8% pace, and gross domestic income — conceptually similar to GDP — rose at a 4.4% rate. Translation: This isn't some fluky reading, but rather a sign of underlying robustness. Rapid growth looks set to continue in Q4; the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow tool estimates current-quarter growth at 2.9%. Zoom in: Nonresidential fixed investment — the GDP subcategory that covers capital spending — contributed 2.2 percentage points to overall growth in Q3. That was the highest since the bounce back following the 2020 pandemic. Excluding that episode, it was the highest since 2011. Yes, but: Personal consumption expenditures rose a moderate 0.3% in October, below analysts' forecasts (+0.4%) and September readings (+0.6%). That implies some softness in consumer spending, which, along with AI investment, has been powering the economy forward. The silver lining, however, is that strong income growth paired with modest consumption growth is good news for American household balance sheets: The personal savings rate rose to 4.1%, from 3.6% in September. Data: Bureau of Economic Analysis; Chart: Neil Irwin/Axios The strong growth readings affirm the Fed's all-but-certain plans to raise interest rates at a meeting concluding Dec. 9. Inflation data also released in the report shows why. By the numbers: The Personal Consumption Expenditures Price Index, which the Fed targets, was up 3.8% over the last 12 months, or 3.3% excluding volatile food and energy. In month-on-month terms, both total and core PCE inflation came in at 0.3% for October, implying no meaningful deceleration is underway. The core number annualizes to 3.5% inflation. State of play: While those readings were right in line with forecasts, they underscore that inflation remains far above the Fed's 2% target — as it has been for nearly six consecutive years. The combination of rapid growth and elevated inflation looks more like a classic overheating economy — one in which the central bank needs to tighten policy to bring inflation into line. Between the lines: The market turbulence of the last few weeks — the S&P 500 was down 2.8% as of 11:45am ET — points to risks that the AI investment boom may deflate in the quarters ahead. But the Fed is unlikely to react to what might happen when growth numbers are coming in so hot and it has been missing its inflation target for so long. What they're saying: "The economy isn't merely hanging in there," Jim Baird, chief investment officer at Plante Moran Financial Advisors, wrote in a note. "It continues to demonstrate considerable resilience, with consumers spending, businesses investing, and few signs that restrictive monetary policy is creating significant cracks in the broader expansion," he added. "Boil it all down, and [Wednesday's] report reinforces an increasingly familiar tension: the economy is performing quite well, but its resilience also gives the Fed an even stronger case for further rate hikes."
