# Enterprise AI is waiting for its Transformer moment

Fast Company · Enrique Dans · 2026-11-08

As a professor of innovation, I have a slide in my deck I use every time I start a session on artificial intelligence: a fragment of the first page from the “Attention Is All You Need” paper, from 2017. I use just a fragment because I don’t want the students to read it. . . . I usually refrain from using a lot of academic papers when I teach MBAs and executives; they tend to be boring, and this one is no exception and is not particularly easy to read. However, it is historic. Written by eight Google researchers, it is the paper that defines the Transformer architecture, which among other things, became the architecture behind GPT and much of the subsequent generative AI wave. The rest, as I said, is history.
The Transformer was not only a successful model architecture; it was also a huge architectural discontinuity. The 2017 breakthrough was able to completely reorganize computation around self-attention, eliminate recurrence, and make parallel training much easier. And in doing so, it did not simply enlarge what was already there; it completely changed the architecture.
In 2017, Google understood the technology, but underestimated the category it could create. It did not ignore the Transformer; it incorporated the architecture into products such as Translate and Search, but it failed to turn its own breakthrough into the product that defined the next era. OpenAI, founded two years before the paper appeared, recognized its broader potential and built GPT, and ultimately ChatGPT, on top of it. Meanwhile, all eight authors eventually left Google, seven to become founders and one to join OpenAI. For present purposes, “Transformer moment” means an organizing principle whose eventual importance greatly exceeds its initial application.
Making an artificial worker more capable vs. making an organization intelligent
I have been insisting on this for a long while now: An individual, human or artificial, can complete tasks, but a company, on top of that, must preserve state, coordinate specialized capabilities, enforce authority, remember outcomes and adapt across time. In that sense, transformers provided the architectural foundation for extraordinary advances in model-level and task-level capabilities. But from there, there is still a significant gap before you reach enterprise performance.
In McKinsey’s 2026 survey, 80% of respondents reported improved personal productivity, but only 37% reported a positive contribution to enterprise EBIT [earnings before interest and tax], with no significant differences from the previous year. This reveals the distance between individual augmentation and organizational results. McKinsey also notes that the high performers are typically those companies that redesign workflows, rather than simply inserting AI into existing ones.
In practice, the industry’s current starting point seems to be the agent. Anthropic explicitly begins with the “augmented LLM,” a model supplied with retrieval, tools and memory, while Microsoft presents sequential, concurrent, group-chat, handoff and other multiagent patterns. These are useful techniques, but nearly all begin with an artificial worker and ask how to connect it to other workers. Is coordinating agents the same thing as designing an intelligent organization? To me, at least, it looks completely different.
