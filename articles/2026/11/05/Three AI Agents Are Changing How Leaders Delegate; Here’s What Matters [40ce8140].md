# Three AI Agents Are Changing How Leaders Delegate; Here’s What Matters

Forbes · Denis Sinelnikov · 2026-11-05

Denis Sinelnikov is the CEO of Media Components and Curis Digital, an award-winning, full-service digital marketing agency.
Writing a client email takes time. Remembering that the client still needs an answer takes attention. For business leaders, that second burden can be harder to manage.
Running a digital marketing agency means moving constantly between client communication, team questions, prospecting and research. AI has helped me write and process information faster. But faster output still leaves someone responsible for deciding what happens next and making sure it happens.
That is what interests me about the latest generation of AI agents. They can carry work across multiple steps, use connected applications and maintain context between conversations. Over recent weeks, I have been testing how they might fit into my company’s operations.
The question I keep returning to is a management question: How clearly can I define a responsibility and how will I know whether it has been handled well?
From Requests To Responsibilities
A chatbot interaction typically begins with a request and ends with a response. An agent can pursue an outcome through a series of actions, within the access and authority it receives.
Think of the difference between opening an inbox and seeing client requests that have not been answered and scheduling follow-ups versus informing the account manager about decisions that need to be made. The second assignment needs background information and your opinion.
Gartner, Inc. predicts that by the end of 2026, up to 40% of enterprise applications will include task-specific AI agents, up from less than 5% in 2025.
For leaders, the challenge of adoption is a pragmatic one: which responsibilities are defined enough to delegate.
Three Products, Three Ways To Think About Delegation
OpenAI’s Dots, Meta’s Muse and xAI’s Grok Bot offer useful examples of this emerging category. Their capabilities overlap, but I see three different emphases that help frame where each might fit.
1. OpenAI Dots: Continuity Across Work
Dots work on ChatGPT and connected apps, with its own cloud computer and context building up over time. They can also talk on Slack and Microsoft Teams.
The management appeal is continuity: less time rebuilding context whenever a task crosses a conversation or application. That could be valuable when work depends on understanding a project’s history, preferences and outstanding decisions.
Boundaries are still needed for continuity. Leaders need to decide what information the agent can access and what actions will be subject to review.
2. Meta Muse: Delegating Everyday Tasks
Meta’s Muse emphasizes personal assistance across connected services. Meta describes a dedicated cloud environment and a separate Sentinel agent that monitors outgoing actions and requests permission when needed.
I see this as a concierge model: a way to explore delegation through everyday coordination and administrative tasks.
For business use, convenience is only part of the evaluation. The relevant question is whether its access controls and approval process fit the responsibility being assigned.
3. Grok Bot: Organizing Work Around Roles
Grok Bot supports named Bots with defined jobs, persistent context and the ability to coordinate with one another.
That suggests a role-based approach: one Bot supporting prospect research, another preparing briefs and another monitoring a recurring workflow.
A meaningful operational detail is that a user’s Bots share a cloud computer, including its files, browser sessions and app logins. Separate names and assignments should not be mistaken for separate access boundaries.
These are useful ways to interpret the products, rather than rigid categories. The right fit depends on the work, its integrations and the controls available.
Where I’m Testing Delegation
My tests fall into three areas: communication, growth and preparation.
Communication includes sorting email, supporting WhatsApp follow-ups and identifying calendar conflicts. Each of these activities is small in itself but the monitoring of them is a constant cumulative burden.
Growth is about finding potential clients, learning who the decision makers are and creating relevant conversation starters. I’m also looking at how agents can find industry conversations worth participating in.
Preparation is about gathering intelligence before calls and watching competitors.
These experiments raise a more demanding question than whether an agent can produce something useful: Does its output reduce the attention required to complete the responsibility?
Give The Agent A Clear Assignment
And so to start off with, before you connect a lot of tools, I suggest you define five things.
1. The Deliverable: Specify what completed work looks like. “Help with client communication” is too broad. A daily list of unanswered client requests, with proposed replies and supporting context, is reviewable.
2. The Information It Can Use: Identify the inboxes, folders or records relevant to the assignment. Expand access when the work demonstrates a need.
3. The Actions It Can Take: Reading, drafting, sending and changing records are different levels of authority. Make those distinctions explicit.
4. The Escalation Conditions: Define when the agent should stop and ask. Conflicting instructions, uncertain information and commitments involving pricing or deadlines are useful starting points.
5. The Success Measure: Track the business outcome alongside the review burden. For an inbox pilot, that could include missed requests, incorrect prioritization and time spent reviewing drafts.
This turns a broad promise of productivity into an experiment a leader can evaluate.
Accountability Remains A Leadership Responsibility
Gartner, Inc. predicts that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027 because of rising costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls. That forecast reinforces the importance of choosing a defined business problem and measuring whether the solution works.
I see substantial potential in agents that support communication, prospecting and research. Realizing that potential will require leaders to become more precise about how they delegate. The first milestone is a responsibility completed to a clear standard, with a manageable review burden. That is the evidence a leader needs before expanding an agent’s authority.
