# Data centers were on the ballot, and voters sent unmistakable message

Fox Business · Bradford Betz · 2026-11-05

Data centers were on the ballot, and voters sent unmistakable message
Communities across the country voted to restrict data center development
Voters across at least 10 states delivered a sweeping rebuke to data center expansion in Tuesday's midterm elections, backing restrictions and rejecting development proposals amid growing concerns about electricity costs, land use and the rapid buildout of artificial intelligence infrastructure.
Voters backed restrictions or rejected pro-development measures in all but one of 41 local ballot measures across 10 states, according to an analysis by Ballotpedia and results compiled by CNBC.
The Washington Post separately reported that voters in at least 38 jurisdictions passed measures limiting or blocking data centers. Many passed by wide margins, underscoring the intensity of opposition in communities where the projects were directly on the ballot.
The results pose a growing challenge for an industry pouring billions of dollars into AI infrastructure, as communities increasingly weigh the economic benefits of new development against concerns about higher utility bills, water consumption, noise and local control.

Opposition has already disrupted projects beyond the ballot box. More than 145 data center projects were delayed or canceled nationwide through early August, according to S&P Global figures cited by The Washington Post, which reported that public opposition accounted for the vast majority of those setbacks.
Ohio, a major hub for data center development, delivered some of the most decisive results.
In the City of Defiance, voters approved a charter amendment restricting data center construction by a 64.73% to 35.27% margin, according to unofficial county election results.
Voters in Pataskala approved a data center-related charter amendment by a 73.62% to 26.38% margin, while voters in Granville backed a similar amendment, 82.45% to 17.55%.

In Ashville, voters rejected a data center referendum by an even wider margin, with 81.61% voting against the measure and 18.39% voting in favor.
The backlash extended into statewide political campaigns, where data center development emerged as an issue alongside broader concerns about affordability and economic growth.
In Texas, Democrat Gina Hinojosa defeated Republican Gov. Greg Abbott after campaigning on a proposed moratorium on new data center projects and tougher protections for the environment and electricity ratepayers.
Ohio elected Democrat Amy Acton as governor. She supports a moratorium and stricter regulations, while Georgia Gov.-elect Keisha Lance Bottoms made greater accountability for data center developers a central part of her campaign.

Polling ahead of the election suggested that opposition to new data centers extended across party lines.
A Washington Post-ABC News-Ipsos poll conducted before the election found that seven in 10 Americans, including a majority of Republicans, opposed new data center construction in their communities.
The Post also cited an election-day poll showing that 56% of voters considered artificial intelligence and data centers a major or crucial factor in their vote, though it ranked last among five issues surveyed, behind concerns including government corruption, the war in Iran and the cost of living.
Industry representatives, meanwhile, argue that data center investment can provide substantial economic benefits to the communities hosting the facilities.
"Affordability, jobs, and the economy are priorities for Americans across the country," said Dan Diorio, executive vice president of state policy and government affairs at the Data Center Coalition. He said data centers can generate jobs, local tax revenue and investment.

"The data center industry is committed to advancing America’s digital infrastructure, supporting energy reliability and affordability, and promoting long-term, responsible economic development," Diorio said.
As public scrutiny intensifies, some major technology companies are taking steps to address concerns about the costs of expanding AI infrastructure.
OpenAI said Thursday that it would pay the full cost of powering data centers it owns and operates, solicit community input, publish construction plans and avoid using nondisclosure agreements with public officials. The company also released a policy blueprint encouraging lawmakers to adopt similar standards.
"What we’re doing with this blueprint is really laying out the best lessons that we have learned and committing to applying them as they go forward," OpenAI Chief Global Affairs Officer Chris Lehane told reporters, according to CNBC.
The announcement followed Tuesday's election results, although the blueprint had been in development since August and built on earlier company pledges to protect ratepayers and address community concerns.
OpenAI is seeking to expand rapidly to meet demand for AI services and has targeted hundreds of billions of dollars in computing infrastructure spending by 2030.
