# The midterm elections’ biggest loser? Data centers

Fast Company · Adele Peters · 2026-11-05

Data centers were on the ballot this election, and they lost.
While results from the midterm elections are still coming in, as of November 4, 38 out of 39 restrictions on data centers had passed—from temporary moratoriums on new construction to full bans. And multiple candidates who made opposition to data centers a key part of their campaign also won.
Take the example of William Lawrence, the Democratic candidate for the U.S. House of Representatives in Michigan’s purple 7th Congressional District, which Trump narrowly won in 2024. Lawrence, a progressive community organizer and cofounder of the Sunrise Movement, was new to politics and faced a challenge running against the Trump-backed incumbent, Tom Barrett. In the primary, facing two establishment Democrats who had raised more money, Lawrence won in part because he vowed to fight data centers.
“He found an issue that connected with local residents across partisanship,” says Matt Grossman, director of the Institute for Public Policy and Social Research at Michigan State University. Recent polls say that seven in 10 Americans don’t want data centers to be built in their communities, for reasons ranging from potential impacts on electric bills to noise and water use.
In his first ad, Lawrence stood at a farm and said, “Tech billionaires want to turn this field into a massive data center that’ll jack up our energy bills and ruin our home values.” After going to local planning meetings to watch data center developers pitch projects, he said that it was an “abuse of power” in communities that didn’t have the resources to fight back. He also called for other controls on technology, including a pause on development of advanced artificial intelligence and mandatory safety requirements for AI.
Barrett, his Republican opponent, had supported data centers in the past. That included a vote for a version of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act that would have stopped states’ ability to regulate AI, and likely also data centers, for a decade. (The provision was later taken out of the bill). The OBBBA includes tax breaks for companies building out AI infrastructure. Barrett also voted for the SPEED Act, which accelerates data center construction. As backlash to data centers grew, Barrett argued that communities should have local control over whether projects get built, but the message fell flat. Lawrence won.
Barrett was unpopular for other reasons—including the fact that he’d never held a local town hall—and it’s not clear that data centers were the thing that made voters push him out, versus anger about the Iran war, tariffs’ impact on the Michigan economy, and other factors. It’s probably too early to say that data centers as an issue swung a race, says Daniel Schiff, an associate professor of technology policy at Purdue University. But the election results affirm the opposition.
