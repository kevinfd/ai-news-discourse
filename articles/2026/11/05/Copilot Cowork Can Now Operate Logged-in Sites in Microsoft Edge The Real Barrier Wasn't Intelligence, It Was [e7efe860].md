# Copilot Cowork Can Now Operate Logged-in Sites in Microsoft Edge: The Real Barrier Wasn't Intelligence, It Was Logging In

Copilot Cowork Can Now Operate Logged-in Sites in Microsoft Edge: The Real Barrier Wasn't Intelligence, It Was Logging In
Hello! I'm Uniko🦄!
Microsoft has enabled Copilot Cowork to use the Microsoft Edge browser on your own computer.
It is a mechanism where the AI can view pages, gather information, and fill out forms on sites you are already signed into, using the same access permissions as you.

Many people probably saw this and thought, "AI has become one step smarter again."
But my perspective is a little different.
The real barrier when delegating work to an AI agent wasn't intelligence, it was "whether it could log in." This update is a story about punching a hole in that wall.
And now that the hole is open, what we need to decide first is the line between which sites we allow the AI to touch and which buttons we must never let it press.
I'll explain in order.
The barrier to delegating work to AI wasn't intelligence, it was logging in
First, I'll start with what I want to say the most.
For about the last year, AI agents have become quite good at "researching," "summarizing," and "creating drafts."
Yet, when you try to delegate actual work entirely, it stops somewhere.
That place where it stops is almost always the login screen.
Think about it.
Most of our work exists behind a login.
Internal application systems, partner portals, advertising management screens, expense reimbursement sites.
No matter how smart the AI is, the moment it hits a wall saying "You cannot enter beyond this point," the work returns to human hands.
That said, it's scary to hand over passwords to an AI, isn't it?
To be honest, this was the biggest dilemma.
You want to delegate, but you don't want to hand over the keys.
Because of this catch-22, AI agents remained "smart but unusable for the important stuff" newcomers.
Moreover, this wall doesn't disappear no matter how much AI performance improves.
Every time a new model comes out, we hope, "This time I can delegate everything," only to end up back at the login screen.
I've seen that scene many times over the past year.
Intelligence increases every month, but the scope of what can be delegated hardly expands.
Thinking that the reason for this was not a matter of capability but a matter of the entrance explains it quite clearly.
The barrier for AI agents wasn't intelligence, it was in front of the login door.
What changed with Copilot Cowork and Microsoft Edge
So, what was released this time?
In the official Microsoft 365 Copilot release notes (posted November 4, 2026, for updates from October 28 to November 3), an item titled "Copilot Cowork supports local Microsoft Edge browser use" was included.
If I summarize what is written in my own words, it looks like this.
- When Cowork needs to perform browser operations during a task, it uses the user's computer's Microsoft Edge
- What it can do includes viewing pages, gathering information, and filling out forms
- The work is done in a separate Edge tab, and the user can see the progress in the Cowork conversation
- Sites that are already signed in can be used with the same access permissions as the user
- It is officially stated that login information, cookies, and sessions remain on the computer
- Company browser settings, IDs, conditional access, and data protection rules are applied as they are
Here is one important note.
Which licenses can use this and when it will reach everyone is not written on this page.
Therefore, I cannot say "anyone can use it starting today."
Please check with your administrator or official guidance to see if it is available in your environment.
The same release notes also listed a SharePoint list agent that lets you create Microsoft Lists by talking to it, reports in the admin center to view agent usage, and AI Workflows that allow you to schedule Copilot to run periodically in Teams. 
Looking at the big picture, it is clear that Microsoft is focusing more on "integrating AI into the workplace" than on "making AI smarter."
What is new is not the AI's brain, but the place where the AI stands.
The significance of keys never leaving the computer
Of all the announcements, this is the one sentence that impressed me the most: 
Credentials, cookies, and session tokens stay on your device.
Login information, cookies, and sessions never leave your computer. 
This may seem subtle, but it is seriously important.
Until now, there were generally only two ways to have AI perform tasks that require logging in. 
One was to entrust passwords or keys to the AI service provider. 
The other was for a person to manually operate the logged-in screen and pass it along. 
The former is scary, and the latter ultimately requires human effort.
The current approach is neither of those. 
The AI sits in your seat and works in your browser. 
The keys remain in your desk drawer. 
Moreover, the browser rules and access conditions set by the company still apply. 
In other words, instead of "creating a separate entrance for AI," it has become a form of "passing through the same entrance as a person, under the same rules as a person."
What is great about this is that company IT staff do not have to create new rules from scratch. 
The work of issuing AI-specific keys, creating management tables for those keys, and deciding on procedures in case of a leak becomes unnecessary, at least at the entry point. 
The scope of what can be delegated expands without increasing the number of defensive mechanisms. 
From the perspective of those implementing it, I think this is the biggest change.
I think this is a very sound approach. 
Recently, other products have emerged that use resident agents that ask for approval before payments or transmissions, or that operate on the concept that what you pass to the AI is approval, not keys. 
The entire industry is moving from "handing over keys" to "handing over approval." 
I see this Copilot Cowork as one move within that same trend.
Instead of handing over the keys, you lend your seat. This is the new form of delegating to AI.

Decide which sites to let it touch and which buttons not to press before delegating
However, this is where the real issue begins. 
The fact that the login barrier disappears also means that the AI can press the same buttons as you, with the same permissions as you. 
Convenience and danger are two sides of the same coin.
The buttons you can press, the AI can also press. 
The send button, the payment button, the delete button, the publish button. 
That is why I think it is best to decide on the line of "how much to delegate" beforehand, as if you were writing it down on paper, before you start using it.
If it were me, I would divide it like this.
- Safe to delegate: Read-only sites, research, summarizing lists
- Delegate up to drafting: Form input, application drafts, preparing reply text
- Do not let AI press: Send, pay, delete, publish, grant permissions, agree to terms
The point is to decide the third category based on the type of operation. 
If you decide by saying "this site is fine, so I'll delegate everything," you end up delegating the "irreversible buttons" within that site as well. 
Draw the line by button, not by site. 
I think this is the new etiquette for work after the login barrier has disappeared.
In terms of Unico's constitution, this is a story about how to use your remaining lives. 
Since the number of decisions a human can make is finite, your day would be over if you checked everything yourself. 
Therefore, leave 'reading, collecting, and drafting' to the AI, and use your remaining life for the final step: 'to click or not to click.' 
Those who decide on this allocation first are the ones who will start freeing up time with AI.
To add one more thing, drawing the line is not a one-time decision. 
At first, keep it at 'up to drafting,' and if there are no problems after watching for a week or two, move it to 'okay to delegate.' 
Conversely, if you have a moment where you feel uneasy, move it back to 'human clicks.' 
It is fine to move the line. However, it is always the human who moves it. 
Just by following this order, you can safely expand the scope of what you delegate to AI.
Don't draw the line at the site level; draw it at the button level.
Make it a practice for a human to press the final button.
Another thing I like about this mechanism is that you can see the AI's work in a separate tab. 
Being able to see it means you can stop it midway. 
This is quite significant when setting up operations.
The operation I want to recommend is very simple. 
Have the AI proceed up to the final button, and have a human press it that last time. 
The AI fills out the form, and the human reviews the content and presses send. 
This way, you get both the speed of AI and the responsibility of a human.
When using it in a team, the perspective of the management side also becomes important. 
The release notes for the same version also included a report in the admin center where you can see agent usage (number of people using it, number of agents, and breakdown by creator). 
When you can see who is using which agent, you can verify later whether the 'line-drawing rules' are being followed. 
Don't just make rules and be done with it; keep it in a state where you can see and correct them. 
Only by doing this much does it become a mechanism you can delegate with peace of mind.
The way of thinking is the same when using it individually. 
You don't need to be glued to the screen the whole time the AI tab is running. 
Just ask it to always stop before the final button, and when you return, you only need to check the content and click. 
Don't watch over it; just look at the final step. 
I believe this is the least tiring distance to maintain when working with AI.

Summary
Let's summarize the story of Copilot Cowork and Microsoft Edge once more. 
The barrier to delegating work to AI agents was login, not intelligence. 
That barrier has been lowered in a way that doesn't take keys outside the computer. 
This means that AI has moved from being a 'newcomer who is smart but stops at the door' to a 'colleague who can work at the same desk.'
That is precisely why there are things to decide before you start using it. 
Which sites to let it touch. 
Which buttons not to let it press. 
Who will press the final button. 
Only those who decide this first can hand over work to AI with peace of mind.
Conversely, as long as this is decided, there is no need to panic every time a new feature comes out. 
No matter what kind of agent comes next, you can immediately judge whether to use it by applying it to these three things: 'sites to let it touch,' 'buttons not to let it press,' and 'who presses the final button.'
These are the three things to do today.
