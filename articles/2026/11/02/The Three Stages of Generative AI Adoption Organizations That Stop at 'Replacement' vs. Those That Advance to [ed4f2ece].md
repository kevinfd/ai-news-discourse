# The Three Stages of Generative AI Adoption: Organizations That Stop at 'Replacement' vs. Those That Advance to 'Business Transformation'

note · デジタルトランスフォーメーション研究所 · 2026-11-02

The Three Stages of Generative AI Adoption: Organizations That Stop at 'Replacement' vs. Those That Advance to 'Business Transformation'
The adoption of generative AI has progressed in many companies. Creating meeting minutes, summarizing documents, drafting text, and translating—these uses have already become part of daily life.
On the other hand, I often hear people say, 'We introduced it, but we haven't moved beyond efficiency gains.' It is true that tasks have become faster, but there is no sense that the way the organization works has changed.
This is not because the way it is being used is wrong. There are stages to utilizing generative AI, and many companies remain at the first stage.
At our company, we categorize the utilization of generative AI into three stages.
The first stage is 'Replacement.' You have AI take over tasks that people are currently doing. Research, summarization, drafting, and organizing information. The primary value is operational efficiency.
The second stage is 'Thinking Support.' People and AI engage in dialogue to advance analysis, ideation, and organization. Strategy formulation, problem definition, issue identification, and solution development. The primary value is the quality and speed of decision-making, as well as organizational alignment.
The third stage is 'Business Redesign.' Assuming AI is present, you redesign the business workflow and the division of roles among people themselves. The primary value is the acceleration of business process transformation.
Let's look at them in order.
Why the first stage, 'Replacement,' is prone to stalling
The first stage is currently the most common way it is used. Structuring messy notes or photos of whiteboards. Organizing large amounts of information. Writing speech drafts. Translating and summarizing. You delegate tasks to AI that would take time if done by a person.
The benefit of this stage is that you can start tomorrow. Since you can delegate tasks at your own discretion, you do not need anyone's approval or interdepartmental coordination.
However, this nature of 'not needing anyone's approval' also becomes its limitation.
Improvements completed at an individual's discretion do not create friction with surrounding work. They are not opposed. That is why it is easy to proceed. But conversely, this means that the conventional way of working, the division of roles, and the methods of decision-making have not been touched at all.
Leaving old work processes as they are and only shortening task time is efficiency, not transformation.
This is the reason why many companies feel that 'even though we introduced generative AI, the organization hasn't changed.'
The second stage, 'Thinking Support,' is not about asking AI to do tasks
What changes in the second stage is not what you entrust to AI, but your relationship with AI.
In the first stage, AI was a substitute for tasks. In the second stage, it becomes a partner that accompanies the organization's thinking. Deciding on organizational problem-solving policies, planning annual action items, and conceptualizing new services. AI enters these scenes where people should originally gather to discuss.
There are three points to this.
The first is to use management frameworks. Instead of discussing things based on spur-of-the-moment ideas, we proceed with examinations following proven patterns of thought. AI leads the flow of decision-making in accordance with those patterns.
The second is to redefine the roles of AI and humans. AI excels at gathering information, expanding options, and organizing the opinions that emerge. What humans take on is deciding what to ask, choosing one from multiple options, and taking responsibility for that decision. AI organizes, and humans take charge of inquiry and decision-making. Based on this division of labor, we restructure the thinking process itself.
The third is consensus building within the organization. This is the decisive difference from the first stage.
Problem-solving proceeds through problem definition, problem identification, and solution planning. In problem definition, we redefine the tentatively set question. In problem identification, we break down the problem and choose which part to solve. In solution planning, we diverge ideas, evaluate them, and converge.
What is important is that stakeholders build consensus at each of these stages.
Conventionally, because this alignment was insufficient, people who should have supported the same solution would sometimes clash at the execution stage. This was because their assumptions regarding the scope, business flow, division of roles, and expected effects were mismatched. When such experiences accumulate, people fear organizational friction and no one embarks on transformation.
Why now?
So why has this problem-solving process not worked in many organizations until now?
It is not because the correct method was unknown. It is because it took too much time.
Redefining problems, breaking them down, selecting, generating ideas, and evaluating them. Aligning the understanding of stakeholders at every one of those stages. If done only by humans, it takes an enormous amount of time. In an organization burdened with daily operations, it was realistically almost impossible to carry this process through to the end.
That is why many organizations skipped the middle parts. They entered discussions on solutions while leaving the problem definition vague, moved to execution without reaching a consensus, and ended up reverting when things did not go well.
This is what generative AI has changed. By having AI take on divergence and organization, humans can focus on inquiry and decision-making. Processes that could not be run due to time constraints can now be run in a realistic amount of time.
This is the reason why the second stage is a story of 'now'.
Assumed case: Incident reporting at a food factory
Let's consider how the three stages actually differ, using the incident reporting work at a food factory as an example. This is not a case study of an actual company, but an assumption for the sake of explanation.
Suppose such voices are being raised from the field: Hygiene rules are strict, and we cannot input into the tablet without washing and disinfecting our hands. The line stops, so reporting is put off. To fill out the report, we write 'thorough caution' every time. Past data remains in PDF format and cannot be searched, so countermeasures are stopgap and problems recur.
What can be done in the first stage? We photograph the blackboard from a meeting, have AI read it, and create minutes sorted into categories such as 'input burden on the field,' 'dead storage of data,' and 'delayed judgment.' Information is organized. However, the work itself has not changed at all.
What happens in the second stage? Based on the organized information, the team discusses with the AI. When we ask, 'Why are off-target countermeasures repeated?', the focus of the examination shifts. Is the problem not the morale of the field, but the business design itself that requires humans to input text in a factory where hands get dirty? Is the goal to create a clean report, or to detect abnormalities even one second faster?
What is happening here is a redefinition of the problem. The question of 'how to ensure input is thorough' changes to 'is there a way to avoid having staff enter text at all?' And because this shift is shared by everyone in the meeting, the subsequent discussion proceeds from a unified understanding.
What do we do in the third stage? We redesign the business workflow itself in line with the new question. When staff find an anomaly, they simply take a photo with their smartphone and dictate the situation by voice. The AI analyzes the photo and audio, then presents past similar issues and effective countermeasures. The person in charge makes a decision after reviewing the alert along with the proposed countermeasures.
Even though it is all 'generative AI utilization,' what happens in these three stages is completely different.
The third stage, 'Business Redesign,' allows for consensus after creation
The hallmark of the third stage is the speed of design and prototyping.
Conventionally, creating a new business mechanism involved documenting requirements, outsourcing design to a vendor, and checking the finished product. This was time-consuming and costly, and parts that 'you don't know until you build it' remained until the very end.
By using generative AI, you can quickly set up new business flowcharts, system screen images, and prototypes as an extension of dialogue. You have an AI agent create and run a prototype, which humans then evaluate and improve.
What changes because of this? Before ordering development, you can reach a consensus while looking at something that actually works. You can gain conviction at the design stage, such as 'this way, I don't have to take off my gloves' or 'this way, we can prevent recurrence.'
You cannot skip the three stages
What we must be careful about here is that trying to go straight from the first stage to the third stage will not work.
To redesign business, stakeholders must first have a shared understanding of 'what should we be solving in the first place?' If you create a new business flow without this agreement, what you end up with is a mechanism that keeps the old problem definition but just looks new.
The second stage is the bridge connecting the first and third stages. And this part cannot be completed by an individual alone. It must be tackled as an organization.
The era of replacing with AI is over; the era of transforming with AI has begun
Utilizing generative AI is not about having AI take over tasks while keeping old business processes in place. It is about questioning AI on 'why are we doing this task in the first place?' and redesigning the division of roles between humans and AI through dialogue.
Because AI takes on the organization of information, humans can focus on questioning and decision-making.
