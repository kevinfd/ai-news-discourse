# Four Things To Settle Before You Release A Biology AI Model

Forbes · Guadalupe Hayes-Mota · 2026-11-20

Guadalupe Hayes-Mota| Director of Bioethics, Markkula Center for Applied Ethics Santa Clara University| MIT Senior Lecturer| EU, NIH Advisor
Suppose you run a 60-person biotech and your team has built an AI model that designs viral capsids, the protein shells that carry gene therapies into cells. It could help target therapies to specific tissues and overcome immune responses that exclude patients from treatment.
Customers want it. Academic labs want it. Your board wants to know why it isn’t on the market. Then your head of research raises an uncomfortable possibility: A tool that helps a therapeutic delivery virus evade immune defenses might also inform work on dangerous pathogens.
This is a dual-use capability, with beneficial and potentially harmful applications. So now the question is no longer simply whether the model works but whether your company can release it responsibly.
Washington Has Named Both Sides
America’s AI Action Plan says AI will unlock “nearly limitless potential in biology,” but warns that it could help malicious actors synthesize harmful pathogens. It calls for federally funded research institutions to use DNA synthesis providers that screen sequences and verify customers.
But screening a DNA order does not determine who should receive the design tool upstream.
The gap is becoming harder to ignore. The International AI Safety Report cited a survey of 375 biological AI tools in which only 3% had safeguards. As one approach to restricting access, in April, OpenAI released GPT-Rosalind through a trusted-access program with early partners including Amgen and Moderna.
A June executive order established voluntary federal evaluation of certain frontier models with advanced cybersecurity capabilities. Another July policy on high-risk life sciences research subsequently directed an interagency group to monitor developments at the biology-AI intersection. Neither establishes a comprehensive pre-release evaluation program for biological AI models.
Putting CARES To Work
As can be seen, the practical decision still lands on company leaders. Here is how I would approach it using CARES, the ethical AI framework I developed for healthcare.
This does not replace established safety practices, but organizes them around five ethical responsibilities that can help make accountability more explicit.
Clinical Accuracy: Who validates the benefit?
For a biological design tool, I would adapt this principle to validated benefit. Commission an independent laboratory to test a representative sample of designs and disclose aggregate results.
A benchmark is not proof that the technology works in practice. If the benefit fails independent testing, why accept the risk?
Accessibility And Equity: Who Receives Access?
Managed access is an emerging biosecurity practice, not an invention of CARES. NTI’s tiered managed-access framework adjusts requirements according to tool risk and user legitimacy.
CARES adds an equity test: Does verification assess legitimate purpose and safeguards, or simply reward wealthy institutions? Create pathways for qualified public-health and rare-disease researchers, including those in lower-income countries.
Responsibility And Oversight: Who Assesses Misuse, And Who Can Stop Deployment?
Red-teaming and independent review are established governance tools. I propose adapting clinical-trial data and safety monitoring boards to biological AI releases. Independent biosecurity, scientific and ethics experts should review pre-release tests and post-release warnings.
Establish written stopping rules and identify an executive authorized to suspend access immediately.
Ethics In Data Privacy: What Did The Model Learn From?
Training-data choices affect safety and trust. For example, developers of Evo 2, which was trained on roughly 9 trillion DNA base pairs, excluded viruses that infect eukaryotic hosts, including humans, even though doing so reduced the model’s performance on those viruses.
Document exclusions and their rationale. If patient-derived data were used, address consent, privacy and downstream expectations.
Social Accountability: Who Gets Told?
Publish what was tested, what risks emerged and what changed, without revealing dangerous operational details.
A July 2026 EMBO Reports article argues that temporary restrictions on biological AI capabilities require independent evaluation, defensive preparation and eventual public reporting. CARES makes that accountability an operational responsibility.
Governance A Small Company Can Afford
For a 60-person biotech, this oversight costs money and can slow commercialization. But inadequate safeguards can threaten partnerships, expose companies to liability and undermine investor confidence if misuse occurs. Regulatory expectations are also evolving.
I think the answer is proportional oversight, not a permanent bureaucracy. You can contract an independent laboratory for targeted validation, share an external safety review panel with other developers or retain qualified reviewers for periodic assessments. Build the cost into product development, as companies already do for quality assurance.
Responsible governance should be affordable enough to implement and credible enough to withstand scrutiny.
The People Who Never Signed Your Contract
CARES organizes these decisions. But I propose one additional requirement: a non-customer impact statement.
This is my proposed governance tool, not an established industry standard, but the idea draws on Magnifica Humanitas, Pope Leo XIV’s encyclical on AI. It describes the common good as “the social expression of the dignity recognized in every person.” The key is every person, not every paying customer.
For a capsid-design model, two populations are missing from the commercial agreement. One bears the risk: people potentially harmed by misuse. The other misses the benefit: patients and researchers excluded by cost, geography or institutional status.
I know the second group. Growing up with hemophilia in rural Mexico, my family traveled eight hours by bus to see a hematologist and sometimes found no medicine. An access policy limited to wealthy partners can reproduce that exclusion with newer technology.
So I propose requiring a one-page statement: Who outside our customer base could be harmed? Who could benefit but be excluded? What have we done for each? Have independent reviewers assess it before launch. No satisfactory answers, no release.
Before Your Next Board Meeting
The Biosecurity Modernization and Innovation Act of 2026 (S.3741), introduced by Senators Tom Cotton and Amy Klobuchar, would strengthen DNA synthesis screening requirements. But even if enacted, it would not make your model-release decision for you.
Put four deliverables on the agenda: independent validation, proportionate safety oversight with stopping rules, equitable managed access and a signed non-customer impact statement.
CARES makes the responsibilities visible. The impact statement widens the circle of people whose interests count. Then ask: Who in your company can say stop, and would anyone listen?
