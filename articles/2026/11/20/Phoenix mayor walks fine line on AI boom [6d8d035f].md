# Phoenix mayor walks fine line on AI boom

Axios · Amy Harder · 2026-11-20

Phoenix Mayor Kate Gallego wants more tech investment in her city — but on her terms, as data centers strain resources and frustrate residents. Why it matters: Phoenix has become a bellwether for the AI industry's growing demands on electricity and water in an increasingly hot, resource-constrained world. As the mayor, Gallego is navigating an outsized dilemma facing communities nationwide: How to embrace the AI boom while protecting residents and resources in an increasingly hot world. "I do want us to lead in technology, and I want us to be a tech-forward community," Gallego said at a recent event co-hosted by the University of Chicago's Institute of Politics and Energy Policy Institute, and moderated by Axios. But she added: "I want companies who care about our community and will give back to it, and not just a race to see who can get the deepest incentives and the cheapest" power. "We're open for business, but we're not for sale," she said. Zoom out: Arizona's Democratic sweep could give Gallego more allies in her efforts to strike the balance she wants. Democrats flipped both legislative chambers and Gov. Katie Hobbs won reelection, giving the state full Democratic control for the first time since 1966. Affordability dominated the election, with data center subsidies and community impacts part of that debate, Gallego said. Catch up fast: Phoenix ranks fourth among major U.S. data center markets by operational capacity. Now the debate is expanding from how much development the region can accommodate to what communities should demand in return. State of play: Gallego, the longest-serving current mayor of America's 10 largest cities, has spent recent years trying to balance that. She helped push to eliminate Arizona's generous data center sales tax exemptions. The state enacted a three-year pause on new applications this summer. She opposes a blanket construction moratorium, favoring more local control over where facilities are built, particularly near homes. Phoenix adopted tougher zoning rules last year requiring special permits for new data centers. Gallego says communities should have more bargaining power over data centers, including where they're built and what residents get in return. That could mean more permanent jobs, transparency about electricity and water use, and benefits for neighbors affected by development. By the numbers: Data centers have requested so much electricity in the Phoenix region that, if all the projects materialized, they could account for roughly two-thirds of the area's power demand, Gallego said. Many may never be built, but Gallego warned: "That's not a balanced economy, and that's not economically sustainable." Friction point: Phoenix's efforts to rein in data centers have sparked legal challenges, with developers originally seeking roughly $750 million in compensation over zoning restrictions adopted last year. The city has granted several exemptions while fighting other claims in court. "We have been open and good partners to the industry, and it's deeply disappointing that they don't want to work with us," Gallego told Axios. The other side: Developers argue the restrictions infringe on their property rights and diminish land values, entitling them to compensation under Arizona law, though industry allies have also acknowledged tensions . Reality check: It's unclear whether Gallego can have it both ways: attracting more tech investment while limiting its costs to residents, especially as developers challenge the city's regulatory authority. Gallego is also navigating the tradeoffs between electricity, water and heat in the desert. Newer cooling technologies can reduce water consumption at data centers but often require more electricity, shifting some of the water footprint to power plants. "We're seeing many of these data centers get power from natural gas plants in an area of our state that is very constrained around water," Gallego said. The intrigue: Data centers are adding another source of heat to a region already struggling with dangerously high temperatures. An Arizona State University study published in May found that data centers can raise nearby temperatures by as much as 4 degrees Fahrenheit. Gallego, who established the nation's first publicly funded municipal heat-response office in 2021, said she's "jealous of Midwestern mayors" who can reuse data centers' waste heat to warm buildings. What we're watching: Gallego worries Arizona might be betting too heavily on an industry whose long-term trajectory remains uncertain. She compared the risk of abandoned data centers to Phoenix's vacant Kmart stores, many of which have been difficult to redevelop. "What will I do in a community if we have these giant windowless boxes" that are no longer operating, she asked. "How will we redevelop or make them not become blight?"
