# Why ‘you don’t have to use AI’ is the new AI pitch

Fast Company · Pete Pachal · 2026-11-20

Adobe is courting AI skeptics and enthusiasts at the same time. Media faces a similar balancing act.
BY Pete Pachal
Pete Pachal is a journalist and the creator of Media Copilot, a newsletter and podcast that examines how AI is changing media, journalism, and the news.
Watching Adobe’s keynote presentation at the 24th annual Max conference in Miami this week, I couldn’t help but think about the tightrope the software giant is walking with AI, and how similar it is to what the media industry is going through. In both cases, there’s a desire to be AI-forward and skate to where the puck is going. At the same time, they need to deal with a hostile crowd—meaning the growing backlash against AI among many of their customers.
At Adobe’s last two annual Max events, AI was a huge emphasis. The company touted AI features, including ways to generate content directly in its Creative Cloud apps, such as Photoshop and Premiere. Adobe hyped up Firefly, which was then mostly associated with the company’s image models. In 2024, Adobe told the audience that “2024 will go down as the year that creativity changed forever.” In 2025, the headliners were a new Firefly image model and a lineup of third-party models from Google, OpenAI, Runway, and others, and “choice” mostly meant choosing which AI model to use. The lone nod to skeptics was a passing aside: “Of course, you can ignore it all as well.”
This year, the tone shifted. In fact, one of the first things incoming CEO Anil Chakravarthy emphasized during the opening keynote was that users didn’t have to use AI if they didn’t want to. “Some of you don’t want to have anything to do with AI,” he said, promising to give those users “the precision and control that you’re looking for, and the option to not use AI.”
Instead of a new Firefly model number, the keynote spotlighted upgrades with no AI in them at all, like a rebuilt color mode in Premiere and Dynamic Text in Illustrator. Firefly is still an area of focus, but it’s evolved into a catch-all creative studio, more like Figma than an AI model. (Disclosure: Adobe paid for my travel to the Max conference, though it had no input on this column.)
None of this is to say Adobe is walking away from AI. Far from it: During that same keynote, Chakravarthy announced Adobe would be providing a way for AI services to access Creative Cloud applications directly through the model context protocol (MCP) standard. That means AI users will be able to have Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini act as an agent, effectively “driving” Photoshop and other Creative Cloud apps to edit projects, create content, and export files directly on their computers. The integrations go into private beta testing in early 2027.
Adobe already provides an AI connector that works similarly, but the MCPs would take things to a new level of precision and control. Jesus Diaz has a great breakdown of exactly how they would work and what it means for creators. In addition, Adobe is building out its own agents. The new Creative Agent is essentially an Adobe chatbot: You describe what you want, and the agent handles the execution in the appropriate app. It’s now in beta inside Premiere, Illustrator, InDesign, After Effects, and Frame.io.
All this makes it clear Adobe isn’t slowing down on AI. It has, however, recognized that a great bifurcation is taking place, one that’s affecting all industries to some extent, but especially the media. On the one hand, you have your AI skeptics, the customers who are suspicious of AI generally and AI-generated or -assisted content specifically. And on the other, you have the AI enthusiasts, the ones who are using AI to enhance and speed up their work, including through tools from other companies.
