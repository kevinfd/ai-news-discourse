# The legal standard that could help rein in AI

Fast Company · The Conversation · 2026-11-20

Some people have begun using the legal system to rein in AI companies and the harms they are creating. Cases are mounting.
In October 2026, the parents of Bowen Wan sued OpenAI, charging that its ChatGPT agent may have instructed their daughter to stop taking her medication, leading to her death. In 2024, a mother sued the Character.AI company, claiming that its chatbot’s interactions with her son contributed to his suicide. The state of Kentucky later filed suit, claiming that Character.AI’s software exposed minors to self-harm and psychological manipulation.
Social media companies, powered by artificial intelligence, are also facing lawsuits about injuries they purportedly caused.
Regulating AI was a hot issue during the 2026 midterms, too. Several candidates who won their races have a regulation agenda.
I am a former litigator and a professor of business law, and all this action signals to me that people are looking to the law to protect them from AI harm.
But what is the legal liability of AI software? An answer may be found in a principle known as strict liability. Applied properly, strict liability could restrain the risky parts of the AI industry and still allow it to provide the tools the public wants.
Abnormally dangerous software
Tort law protects people from negligence, such as who is responsible if you slip and fall in a store. Strict liability is part of tort law, but it is used to hold a person or company responsible for harm even if they were not at fault, not negligent, or had no intent to create trouble.
