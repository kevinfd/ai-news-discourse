# Former Secret Service investigator sounds alarm over AI scams as Americans grow increasingly anxious

Fox News · Michael Sinkewicz · 2026-11-20

A former Secret Service cyberfraud investigator warned that Americans are increasingly on edge following a wave of major technology disruptions, while artificial intelligence is making scams targeting families and consumers more sophisticated.
Jason Brown, a former U.S. Secret Service cyberfraud investigator and iCounter's director of customer advisory and counter fraud lead, told Fox News Digital on Thursday that Americans have reason to be increasingly cautious about technology.
"Everybody's on edge," Brown said.
"There's been a lot of stuff that has happened in the past week in particular that people are concerned about," he continued, citing recent technology outages involving Microsoft, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and several financial institutions.

Brown acknowledged that some incidents involved infrastructure problems while others remained under investigation.
With the holiday season approaching, Brown warned that AI has made it easier for criminals to create convincing fraudulent messages, eliminating many of the warning signs consumers previously relied on.
"AI has just made the criminal a lot more efficient and a little bit more effective," Brown said.
Spelling mistakes, poor grammar and awkward phrasing — once common indicators of fraudulent messages — are becoming less reliable as criminals use AI to generate more convincing communications, he explained.

AI also allows criminals to impersonate familiar voices, potentially tricking victims into believing they are speaking with a family member in distress.
Brown described a scenario in which a scammer uses a short voice recording to impersonate someone's relative and demand thousands of dollars in gift cards.
"AI just makes them a lot more efficient and a lot less prone to errors," he said, warning that Americans must become more skeptical of suspicious messages.
"Unfortunately, we're at a point where everybody needs to have that level of distrust," he said.
As holiday shopping ramps up, Brown said scammers are likely to impersonate major delivery companies, including UPS, FedEx and the U.S. Postal Service.

He warned that fraudulent messages may claim packages cannot be delivered until additional postage is paid, with scammers demanding payment through cryptocurrency or gift cards.
"If it comes from crypto, especially if it comes from gift cards, that's a red flag for sure," Brown said.
Beyond the emotional toll, Brown warned that increasingly sophisticated scams can result in significant financial losses. He cited FBI reporting that he said identified more than 22,000 AI-related fraud cases in 2025, with losses exceeding $890 million.
Brown noted that businesses are also vulnerable to schemes in which criminals impersonate trusted vendors and redirect legitimate payments into fraudulent accounts.

The holiday season can create additional vulnerabilities as executives travel, employees take time off and temporary workers join company operations, he said.
Brown urged Americans to independently verify suspicious messages and take additional precautions when communicating with family members, including establishing a "safe word" to help confirm someone's identity during an unexpected call.
"Another thing that we always talk about with family members, set up a safe word ahead of time. Have that conversation now," he said, adding that even if a caller provides the correct word, people should still end the call and contact the person independently using a trusted number.
