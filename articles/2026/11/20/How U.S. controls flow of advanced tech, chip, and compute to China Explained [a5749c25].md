# How U.S. controls flow of advanced tech, chip, and compute to China | Explained

The Hindu · John Xavier · 2026-11-20

The story so far: The battle for the most sought after commodity that underpins development of artificial intelligence technology just grew fiercer after the U.S. Commerce Department, on November 16, 2026, issued an interim final rule (IFR) barring data centre operators outside of the U.S., equipped with advanced American AI chips, from providing cloud service to businesses in two dozen countries, including China, Iran and Russia. The new, yet to be published, rule also restricts American companies from freely shipping high-end AI chips to a set of five countries, including Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore, without the explicit approval of the U.S. government.
China has censured the move, calling it “yet another case of the U.S. overstretching the concept of national security, and abusing export control measures to interfere in normal trade and tech cooperation between China and other countries and go after China’s high-tech industry.”
The interim rule came just days ahead of the meeting between U.S. President Donald Trump and China’s President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen, China on the sidelines of the November 18-19 APEC Summit.
The U.S. government’s policy on making advanced technology inaccessible to China has been a talking point among lawmakers in Washington for well over a decade, but a legal tool to control the flow took shape only during the first Trump term when the administration passed the Export Control Reform Act in 2018.
It gave the U.S. a legal weapon to control the movement of advanced technology originating from the country. Its primary purpose was to curb export of top-tier chips that had dual-use — both commercial and military purposes — to countries like China, Russia and Iran.
