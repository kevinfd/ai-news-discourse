# Two probes are about to arrive at Mercury for an up-close look at this puzzling planet

Scientific American · Jonathan O'Callaghan · 2026-11-20

After eight years of a winding journey across the inner Solar System, a new spacecraft is about to arrive at Mercury—or rather, two spacecraft, one from Europe and one from Japan, that have traveled together for eight years over some six billion miles (10 billion kilometers). They will be our first visitors to Mercury in more than a decade as they pursue a mission to scour this strange world in greater detail than ever before. What they discover could help enable something unprecedented: a first-ever lander on Mercury.
The mission, called BepiColombo, is due to enter into orbit around Mercury on November 21 after an almighty trek. The two spacecraft launched entwined from Earth in October 2018 and have since completed nine planetary flybys—one of Earth, two of Venus and six of Mercury—to gradually slow their precession enough to be captured by Mercury’s orbit.
The last spacecraft to visit Mercury, and the only prior spacecraft to orbit it, was NASA’s MESSENGER mission, which circled the planet from 2011 to 2015. The only other spacecraft to venture there, one of the most difficult places to reach in the solar system, given its proximity to the sun, was NASA’s Mariner 10, which flew by Mercury three times in 1974 and 1975.

“For a long time, people didn’t think it was possible to get to Mercury orbit,” says Shoshana Weider, Mercury science lead at NASA Headquarters in Washington, D.C. BepiColombo is named for the man who worked out how to do it, Italian mathematician and engineer Giuseppe (Bepi) Colombo, who died in 1984.
The reasons for going are plentiful. Mercury is bizarre, at times more like an exoplanet than a true planet of our sun. It has a bafflingly large iron-rich core that extends about 85 percent of the way from the planet’s center to its surface; in contrast, Earth’s core extends just 55 percent of the way to our world’s surface. Mercury has an extreme surface that’s alternately bathed in solar radiation and then plunged into darkness, experiencing temperature swings of about 800 degrees Fahrenheit in daylight to –290 degrees F at night, as well as wisps of an atmosphere, called an exosphere, that scientists are eager to study.
Bizarrely, it also seems to have ice trapped in craters at its poles, something that should be impossible so close to the sun; Mercury circles our star at some 36 million miles away, about three times closer than Earth does. “The leading theory is that material came from either a volatile asteroid or comet impact,” says Carolyn Ernst, a planetary scientist at the Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory in Maryland. “That ties back to how Earth got its water.”
BepiColombo’s orbital insertion was relatively straightforward, a weak capture as the spacecraft edged toward the planet, although it was delayed by a year because of a thruster malfunction. The true drama, though, should come on December 9. That’s when, following a series of thruster burns that will lower and stabilize the spacecraft around Mercury, the two mission components—the Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) from the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter (Mio) from the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA)—will separate and say goodbye.
The separation will be one of the most tense and difficult moments in space exploration history. These twin spacecraft, orbiting another world, will pry themselves apart to enter different orbits around Mercury. Latches will unclasp, wires connecting the two spacecraft will be cut, and a spring will push them apart slower than walking speed. “It’s the most complex mission I have worked on by far,” says Ignacio Tanco, head of inner solar system mission operations at ESA, “and probably the most complex mission that ESA has operated ever. It’s extremely challenging.”
Presuming everything goes to plan, Mio will enter an orbit of some nine hours around Mercury, and MPO will move slightly closer to a two-hour orbit over three months. Both spacecraft will loop over the Mercury’s poles, enabling them to see most of the planet, which is two fifths the size of Earth in total, as it rotates underneath.
Although MESSENGER saw the majority of the surface during its mission, BepiColombo will give us renewed views across the planet (including any new craters that have formed in the past decade) and precise measurements of that surface, particularly of the south pole, which was not seen in detail by its predecessor. Europe’s MPO has a camera to take high-resolution images. “It will tell us whether what we saw in the north is also found in the south,” including by looking for signs of ice in the shadowed craters, says Sean Solomon of Columbia University, former lead for MESSENGER.
Mercury has a range of mysteries that MPO and Mio will investigate. It has signs of past volcanism on its surface stretching back billions of years, which might be linked to strange holes known as hollows, where parts of the surface of Mercury have collapsed. “We don’t understand what’s causing the volcanism,” says David Rothery, a planetary geoscientist at the Open University in England, who works on two of MPO’s instruments.
The planet also appears to be shrinking ever so slightly as the planet cools, forming vast escarpments, “a couple of kilometers high,” where chunks of its surface have been scraped over one another, Rothery says. “And some of these thrust faults are still moving, so Mercury’s tectonics haven’t finished.”
Having two spacecraft in orbit around Mercury enables some novel science. Both MPO and Mio will measure Mercury’s magnetic field simultaneously; Mercury is the only rocky planet in our solar system, besides Earth, that generates a global magnetic field from its core, albeit one about 100 times weaker than our own. Measuring it in two locations at once will show us how it responds to the charged particles from the sun—the solar wind—and perhaps tell us more about how it arises under Mercury’s surface.
Another tool on MPO called Strofio will measure the thin atmosphere around Mercury, called an exosphere, “which is basically little particles that follow a ballistic trajectory off the surface and then fall back down,” says Weider, who is program scientist for the NASA-funded instrument. It will capture some of these particles and reveal how they are kicked up into space.
The two spacecraft will begin science operations in April 2027 and should spend at least a couple of years studying Mercury before their orbits decay and they crash into the planet. Following this mission, scientists are hopeful that our next foray to Mercury will be a trip to its surface, once we have learned everything we can from space, similar to how our exploration of Mars has progressed. “After BepiColombo, we will have done all of the things that we can think of today from orbit,” Solomon says. “If we want to learn more about Mercury, we have to go to the surface.”
Such an undertaking would be immensely challenging, given that Mercury—which is roughly the size of Mars and has similar gravity—has no atmosphere to slow the descent of a spacecraft. Yet there are proposals on the table, including one from Ernst and her colleagues that was drawn up in 2020. “It is not impossible,” she says. BepiColombo, too, once had a lander earmarked for the mission, but it was scrapped for cost and complexity reasons. If a subsequent lander could include a means of launching back to Earth from the surface, we could get our hands on material from Mercury for the first time ever.
For now, it is BepiColombo’s turn, which should give us a nearly complete picture of this unusual world. “Mercury is a strange little oddball,” Weider says. It doesn’t fit with the rest of the solar system. Maybe, in the next few years, we’ll finally find out why.
“Every time we go to Mercury, we are surprised,” Solomon says. “I expect that to repeat.”
