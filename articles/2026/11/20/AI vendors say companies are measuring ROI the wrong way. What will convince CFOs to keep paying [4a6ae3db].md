# AI vendors say companies are measuring ROI the wrong way. What will convince CFOs to keep paying?

Fast Company · Victor Dey · 2026-11-20

Artificial intelligence companies want their customers to use more AI. Their customers want AI to help them spend less money. That creates a tricky dynamic for an industry whose revenue depends on selling more of a technology that’s supposed to make businesses more efficient. As CFOs prepare their 2027 budgets, the question is becoming harder to avoid: Is all this AI spending actually paying off?
The industry’s preferred measures of progress offer an incomplete answer. Salesforce reports that its technology has performed 7 billion agentic work units, which count activities such as invoking a tool or completing a reasoning chain. A single business outcome can require many such units. Meanwhile, ServiceNow told investors on October 28 that its internal AI use generates more than $1 billion in annualized value, double its estimate at the end of 2025.
Those figures help vendors make their case to investors but offer buyers less certainty about what they’re paying for. More automated activity can mean useful work—or repeated attempts to complete the same task.
At Infosys, that tension is already affecting revenue expectations. AI revenue rose 29% from the previous quarter and reached 10.5% of sales, yet the company cut its full-year growth outlook to 1%-2% amid pricing pressure and demands for greater productivity. When AI lets a client buy the same service for less, the savings strengthen the client’s case for investing in AI while reducing the supplier’s contract value. New AI business must make up for the revenue lost as existing work becomes cheaper.
In a survey of 260 finance executives, the cloud cost management company CloudZero found that two-thirds said their boards required proof of returns before approving further AI investment. Yet a separate survey from Protiviti, a management consultancy, found that only 35% of finance organizations considered themselves effective at measuring AI returns.
In conversations with Fast Company, enterprise AI leaders described how competing definitions of success are shaping pricing and contract renewals. Outcome-based pricing offers little protection when a vendor counts a problem as resolved but leaves the customer with more work. Higher consumption bills are difficult to justify when a cheaper model delivers the same result. To secure another year of spending, suppliers may need to show customers which expenses they can eliminate, even when that means reducing their own fees.
Snowflake tells some customers to spend less on AI
Snowflake’s CEO, Sridhar Ramaswamy, argues that rising AI consumption is worthwhile only when it leads to operational improvements and replaces existing costs. Snowflake reported $1.49 billion in product revenue for its second fiscal quarter of 2027, up 37% from a year earlier, and 126% net revenue retention.
