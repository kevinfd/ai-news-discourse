# How I use Meta Muse safely to stretch my AI budget – and what I never let it do

ZDNET · David Gewirtz · 2026-11-20

ZDNET’s key takeaways
- Meta Muse is useful, free, and hard to trust.
- I use its research to stretch my paid AI subscriptions.
- My accounts, passwords, and computer stay off-limits.
Here’s the thing about Meta Muse: it’s considerably better than you might expect for a free AI agent. It’s also sketchy as all heck. That’s why I use Muse only for public information research, without giving it access to my accounts, passwords, or computer.
Because it’s free, and because it’s actually really pretty good, I tend to use Muse fairly often. However, because it’s sketchy as heck, I take precautions. In this article, I’ll show you what is safe to use and how to protect yourself.

I’ll walk you through my best-practice recommendations, which also include restricting prompts so they contain nothing that needs to be kept private. Meta is Facebook’s parent company. Let’s face it, Facebook is not a paragon of identity protection and privacy. In fact, Facebook’s primary business is turning you into an asset production machine for it to mine and monetize. Make no mistake about it: Muse expands on that considerably.
The permissions I lock down and the personal data I keep from Muse
In September, I took a first hands-on look at Muse. There were five worrisome themes, all in terms of privacy and data usage:
- Full disk permission: First, it wanted me to grant it full disk access permission simply as part of the setup process. It didn’t wait until I gave it an assignment that needed it, nor did it try to limit its impact. It wanted all in, right away. I said no.
- Insistent on microphone access: It did the same with the microphone. There have long been rumors that Facebook listens covertly. While those rumors have been resoundingly debunked, Muse asked for microphone access on installation. When I told it no, it triggered a system-level request for access. I canceled that.
- Logins and passwords: It wanted to store my logins and passwords, effectively volunteering itself as a password manager. While other AIs try to limit their password access, Meta wanted all of them. Oh, hell, no.
- Model training on by default: Meta turned model training on by default. What this means is that it uses your data to train its models. If you don’t like that, you have to go in and find a setting and turn it off. I turned it off.
- Meta’s slimy track record: And then, of course, there’s Meta’s track record of privacy misbehavior, ranging from manipulating feeds for emotional contagion and concealing internal research to misrepresenting privacy controls, deceiving users, and sharing data, which led to a whopping $5 billion fine from the Federal Trade Commission.

CNET has a piece that concurs with my observations, calling Muse “a potential security nightmare.” It documented a number of cases where, despite permissions being turned off, researchers claim Muse harvested private data. I haven’t seen this behavior in my testing, but it is a big part of why I’m very careful with how I use it.
So while I use Muse regularly, I never give it any personal details. I don’t let it have any access to my computer or my computer’s microphone. I never give it an assignment that requires computer use or access to any of my accounts. I don’t set up any connectors, including to my Mac apps like Notepad or Reminders.
And I most definitely do not give it access to Chrome, let it record my screen, or give it access to my payment methods through my Stripe account or Shop Pay.
In addition to these obvious “what not to do” items, I also don’t use Muse interactively as a chatbot. I don’t converse with Muse. During chats, you are likely to reveal snippets of personal information. We do not want Meta to have any of that personal information. For any of the standard chatbot-type activities, I use a more trustworthy tool.

How Muse’s free research helps me stretch my paid AI plans
Put simply, Muse’s free agentic capabilities are pretty good. As an AI researcher, I need to use it just because it’s out there and it’s popular. I test these things as part of my job. But I would use it anyway because it has a fairly unique value proposition.
As a basic AI tool, it’s as good as anything we had in 2025. In terms of late 2026 technology, I’d put it on a par with ChatGPT Terra or Luna, or Claude Sonnet or Haiku. Muse is definitely not at the flagship model level like ChatGPT Sol or Claude Opus, but it’s not bad.
Muse’s value prop has to do with how much agentic work it’s willing to do for free. All the frontier model providers meter their agentic capabilities in one way or another. For applications using the models, the AI companies sell API access by tokens. For desktop work, you can buy subscriptions, typically at $20, $100, and $200 per month.
For those monthly subscription fees, you get a certain amount of usage: less on the lower tiers, more if you’re a bigger spender. I have the $20-per-month ChatGPT Plus plan, which gets me a small amount of programming and a moderate amount of agentic work a month.
I have the $100-per-month Claude Max plan, which usually gets me through a month of fairly heavy vibe coding, but I do use up my weekly allocation from time to time.
I am constantly aware of how I am consuming my allocated usage. I don’t really want to pay more than I already am. So I try to spend my usage allocation where it will have the most impact.

Muse gives me an agentic tool with free usage allocation. I can give Muse non-mission-critical assignments and not worry about whether or not I’ll run out of usage. If I do, I do. I’m not using it as part of my day-to-day projects.
That’s Muse’s value. You can give it work that requires agentic capabilities, but limit it to assignments that are not worth upgrading one of your other paid subscriptions for.
How I use Muse to research public information
I use Muse exclusively for agentic search and analysis. I give it assignments that require a chain of web searches for information-gathering purposes. Those searches generate data that needs analysis and intelligence as the chain is processed, which then results in a report or recommendation. It’s really quite good at that.
The way I’m doing it with agentic assignments is that I’m substantially less interactive with the tool. I give it a homework assignment. Essentially, I let it go off and do that homework for me. Here are some examples of what I’ve sent it off to research.
- Manor Lords sentiment in 2026: I wanted to know what people thought of this popular city-builder game. I wound up getting Transport Fever 3 instead because I’m a supply chain geek.
- PBS Passport subscription requirements: How to gain access to the streaming channel without a local cable TV subscription.
- Get rid of kitchen fruit flies: Tips to set fruit fly traps and stop them from breeding.
- Research Google Jules shutdown: Find out what happened with the Jules wind-down.
- Check Mechanical Turk service status: What the current story is with Amazon Mechanical Turk (it closed in September).
- Research Facebook trust violation incidents: Debunk or confirm the “Facebook listens through mics” claim.
- Explore PNG metadata: Understand the internal format of PNG files and how to apply it to a specific project I was working on.
All of these assignments (I’ve done a total of 46 so far) require multiple web searches and aggregation into a summary or detailed report. These are all ideal projects for an agentic researcher. Muse does the job well.
One issue is that even these “incognito” searches do give away information Facebook might use. For example, they could show Meta the kinds of games I like, the fact that I want to watch shows on PBS, that we had a fruit fly problem for a week, and more.

My interest profile is probably a matter of public record. Heck, if you’ve been reading my articles for any time at all, you know what I’m interested in. And you know that I’m a bit of a geek. So I don’t mind the risk that Facebook will pick up on this.
But I don’t want Meta to have access to my accounts, my passwords, my computer, or any files that are personal or client-related or contain any identifying information that could lead to serious privacy or identity theft issues.
My best-practice recommendation is to lock down all of Muse’s permissions and only feed it assignments that contain interest clues that, if released publicly, wouldn’t reveal anything that you would mind having revealed.
If you do that, you can have a free agentic researcher available to you for lower-priority work. Just keep in mind that this free ride might not last. Meta does have paid plans at the same pricing tiers as the other AI providers. That said, its free access is unusually generous. I’m sure there will come a time when Meta charges, at least for those of us who don’t give it enough data to feed its prodigious data-gathering appetite.
