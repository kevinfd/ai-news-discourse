# SpaceX has a CoreWeave problem that no one is talking about

Fast Company · Inc. · 2026-11-20

After a no-good, very bad week for AI stocks, SpaceX gave the AI faithful—and all the Elon Musk acolytes—something to celebrate when it reported earnings on October 20. Its results beat analyst expectations across the board in almost every way, with revenue leaping 161% to $14.6 billion, and operating income hitting $2.8 billion. Even its so-called Super Intelligence segment—its AI business—reported $431 million in operating income, a welcome relief from the red ink that is a chief worry about AI companies. The stock surged 8%, JPMorgan doubled its price target to $500 a share, and Truist Securities called the results “the first data point during earnings season to support the bull case for AI cloud providers.”
What’s not to like?
Since I’m a curmudgeon and since you can read about what there is to like in a lot of other places, I’ll tell you what is not to like! But first, let me offer a trigger warning for those who can’t bear to hear anything bad about Elon Musk. Second, a caveat: This is meant to provide those who want to test their optimism a few counterpoints, not to reflect my complete views on the company.
To start with the obvious, SpaceX is priced less like an actual company than like a precious object that should be in a museum. It is now valued at $2.1 trillion, which is about 36 times its annualized revenue and roughly 55 times its annualized adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization). That’s a premium that only a few other companies, like Palantir and Cloudflare, command. And as a source points out to me, that $2.1 trillion is almost twice the $1.28 trillion combined market value of the seven biggest defense giants—Lockheed Martin, General Dynamics, and so on. These are real companies, upon which the American economy depends. Does SpaceX’s premium reflect economic reality?
The profitability of both SpaceX overall and of the AI segment also merits a second look. The company’s accounting profit didn’t translate into free cash flow. Indeed, free cash flow burn was almost $10 billion, exceeding the expectations of a burn of $8.6 billion. In the AI business alone, SpaceX spent $16.4 billion in the quarter. There’s nothing wrong with spending cash in the expectation that the investments will turn into profits someday. But it does mean the company is dependent on the capital markets to supply the funding it needs to grow.
The larger issue is that SpaceX has effectively turned its AI business into a neocloud. Its own model, xAI, is badly lagging others, based on the reported token spend per model. XAI’s spend is a fraction of that of market leaders Anthropic and OpenAI. Whether because xAI was failing or because he spotted an opportunity, Musk pivoted to selling computing power to other players. That means that some of the same factors that affect a company like CoreWeave, which saw its stock plummet and the yield on its bonds soar last month because of its heavy cash burn and constant need to raise capital, also affect SpaceX’s AI business.
On the earnings call, Musk told analysts to expect 10 gigawatts of incremental compute to come online during 2027. According to a back-of-the-envelope calculation done by longtime skeptic Jim Chanos, for the most cutting-edge fully equipped data centers, that would require $500 billion-plus of capital expenditures, which is three times higher than the consensus view of what the entire company will spend in 2027. On the call, SpaceX management claimed they have debt lined up to finance the CapEx spree. “There are a number of available pools of debt capital that we are talking to at the moment relating to funding this incredible growth,” said CFO Brad Coffman. But it’s increasingly unclear if bond markets will absorb the sheer amount of debt all the AI companies plan to issue without charging higher rates—and, of course, higher rates make profitability more elusive.
