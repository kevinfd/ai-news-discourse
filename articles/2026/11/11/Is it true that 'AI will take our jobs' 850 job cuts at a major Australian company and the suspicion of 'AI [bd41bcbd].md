# Is it true that 'AI will take our jobs'? 850 job cuts at a major Australian company and the suspicion of 'AI washing'

note · 日本で報道しないニュース · 2026-11-11

Is it true that 'AI will take our jobs'? 850 job cuts at a major Australian company and the suspicion of 'AI washing'
Artificial intelligence (AI) will take away human jobs. This debate is no longer a prediction of the future. Companies around the world are announcing layoffs citing AI adoption, and the anxiety of workers is becoming a reality.
However, in Australia, that very explanation is now being called into question.
The trigger was the announcement of 850 job cuts by Qantas, the country's largest airline. The company explained that it would restructure its administrative departments—including finance, procurement, human resources, and marketing—as part of a multi-year partnership with consulting giant Accenture to adapt to technological innovations, including AI.
However, some of the tasks subject to the cuts are scheduled to be moved to overseas teams, primarily in India. Labor unions are pushing back, asking, 'Are jobs really being taken by AI, or are they just being moved overseas where labor costs are cheaper?'
Reuters reported on this issue as a phenomenon called 'AI washing.' Similar to 'greenwashing,' where companies promote themselves as more environmentally friendly than they actually are, this term refers to explaining layoffs as AI-driven reform when the actual primary purpose is cost-cutting or offshoring.
Is AI really replacing human jobs? Or has it become a convenient label for companies to justify staff reductions?
1. The reality behind Qantas' 850 job cuts
The job cuts announced by Qantas represent approximately 3% of its 28,000 employees in Australia. The affected areas are administrative departments such as finance, procurement, human resources, and marketing, rather than the actual operation of aircraft.
The cuts will not be implemented all at once. They will be carried out over the next few years, with the first changes expected to begin by mid-2027. Some operations will be transferred to Accenture, with plans for much of this work to be handled by overseas teams in places like India.
Qantas CEO Vanessa Hudson stated in an explanation to employees that the scale and speed of changes in technology and AI are unprecedented. The company explains that it needs to modernize old systems that have accumulated over many years, reduce operational duplication, and incorporate expertise in data and AI.
According to the company, there are over 1,000 legacy applications within the company built in different eras, some of which do not communicate with each other. Streamlining these systems is a rational decision for increasing a company's competitiveness.
However, what cannot be overlooked here is where the work is being moved. If AI is going to fully automate the tasks, why is it necessary to first move the work to other employees in India? This question is at the heart of the current 'AI washing' criticism.
2. What is 'AI washing'?
AI washing is an expression that refers to companies linking the reasons for layoffs or business restructuring to the introduction of AI, while obscuring the aspects of actual cost-cutting or offshoring.
Of course, it is true that AI is changing the nature of work in companies. Some tasks previously handled by humans, such as document creation, inquiry responses, data analysis, translation, and accounting, can be automated.
However, just because AI is introduced does not mean that all jobs immediately become unnecessary. There are many cases where, while some tasks are made more efficient by AI, humans still handle final decisions, customer service, and exception handling.
Professor Uri Gal of the University of Sydney Business School points out that companies should be cautious about bringing up AI as a reason for layoffs. While announcing them as simple cost-cutting measures would draw criticism, explaining them as 'AI-driven transformation' makes them look like progressive corporate reform.
In the case of Qantas, a vision has been presented where work is first moved to Accenture's overseas teams, and then AI and automation are utilized. Professor Gal raised the issue of whether there is a contradiction between the explanation that jobs are becoming unnecessary due to AI and the plan to move those jobs to other people.
However, at this stage, it cannot be concluded that Qantas is using AI as a mere pretext. Transitioning to new systems can sometimes require human labor and expertise, so it is not inherently unnatural for offshoring and AI adoption to proceed simultaneously. The important thing is to separately verify which tasks are being replaced by AI, which tasks are being moved overseas, and how many jobs are actually becoming unnecessary.
3. Not just Qantas: Restructuring of Australian companies
According to Reuters, this trend is not limited to Qantas. WiseTech, which handles accounting and logistics software, and business software company Atlassian are also cutting staff, citing AI as one of the reasons.
Telecom giant Telstra is also cutting staff in its AI-related business with Accenture and moving some operations to India. Furthermore, even companies that do not explicitly cite AI as a reason are moving to reduce domestic staff while expanding operations in India, the Philippines, Vietnam, and elsewhere.
In other words, what is happening at Australian companies is not just automation through AI. Outsourcing to overseas, streamlining organizations, and relocating to regions with lower labor costs are all progressing simultaneously.
For companies, this may be a rational management decision. If they can reduce labor costs, update old systems, and use AI to increase productivity, it will lead to improved international competitiveness.
On the other hand, for those working domestically, it becomes difficult to distinguish whether the introduction of AI or relocation overseas is the cause of job losses. Is retraining necessary to adapt to AI advancements, or will the workplace itself disappear due to relocation? Since the countermeasures are different, companies have a responsibility to explain.
4. Changes in employment as seen by the government and central bank
This issue cannot be ignored by the Australian government either. Amidst the continued rise in the cost of living, the unemployment rate has reached 4.7%, the highest level in five years. To curb inflation, the central bank has raised the policy interest rate five times since the beginning of 2026, bringing interest rates to an 18-year high.
When interest rates rise, companies face increased borrowing costs. Consumers also face heavier burdens such as mortgages, and if the economy slows down, companies find it easier to consider cutting labor costs.
The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) does not assess that large-scale job losses due to AI are already widespread. However, it has pointed out that job advertisements are weakening in occupations susceptible to AI, and changes in employment trends are beginning to appear.
The government's position is that companies should invest in upskilling employees so that workers can also benefit from new technologies. However, when companies move jobs overseas, there is no guarantee that employees can remain in the same workplace even if they receive retraining domestically.
5. If they blame AI, are companies exempt from accountability?
What is important in this issue is not a simple binary choice of whether AI will take jobs. It is a question of how specifically companies explain the reasons when they announce layoffs.
For example, by what percentage will work hours be reduced by AI? How many jobs will be moved due to overseas relocation? Are there opportunities for redeployment for the laid-off employees? Without such information, it is difficult for outsiders to verify the true reasons.
Qantas says it will continue to consult with labor unions and work on redeployment and support for affected employees. On the other hand, the union side is pushing back, arguing that workers should not unilaterally bear the costs of technological innovation.
If productivity improvements through AI are truly realized in the future, there is a possibility of positive effects on corporate profits and customer service. However, if those results are concentrated only on shareholders and management, and only layoffs remain for employees, social backlash will be inevitable.
Summary—Is AI taking jobs, or are companies moving them?
The 850-person layoff at Qantas has become a symbolic case for thinking about employment in the AI era. Companies are touting transformation through AI, but behind that, overseas relocation and cost-cutting are also progressing.
At this point, there is no evidence to conclude that Qantas has unfairly used AI to justify layoffs. However, it is a fact that if the introduction of AI and overseas relocation are explained together, it becomes harder to see what the true reason for the cuts is.
The 850-person reduction will be carried out over the next few years, with the first changes scheduled by mid-2027. It has not yet been determined to what extent the redeployment and support for affected employees will be realized.
The era in which AI replaces human work is certainly approaching. However, it does not mean that jobs are lost every time a company introduces AI. Moving jobs to countries with lower labor costs and AI automating work are issues that should be verified separately.
Behind the explanation that 'jobs were taken by AI,' companies were actually moving work overseas. To avoid overlooking such situations, we must look at the details of the cuts rather than accepting corporate announcements at face value.
Is AI really taking jobs? Or has it become a new excuse for companies to reduce their workforce? The answer should become clear through future corporate explanations and the actual fate of the workers.
