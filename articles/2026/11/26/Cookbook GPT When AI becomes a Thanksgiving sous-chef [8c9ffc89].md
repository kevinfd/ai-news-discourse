# Cookbook GPT: When AI becomes a Thanksgiving sous-chef

Axios · Avery Lotz · 2026-11-26

From cookbooks to YouTube to ChatGPT , the way Americans plan their holiday menus has evolved. The big picture: But there's something uniquely human about building a recipe, and chefs warn we risk fracturing personal connections and family history by reducing culinary traditions to chatbot prompts. "Recipes can have tradition and ritual," says Melanie Underwood, a culinary educator and founder of Nourish & Gather . She warns that taking the human conversation out of cooking could further erode our cultural oral histories. Context: Campbell's recent State of the Sides survey found that 27% of U.S. adults who celebrate Thanksgiving have used generative AI for holiday planning or cooking, up from 22% last year. And 65% of shoppers say they would like to use AI in some aspect of holiday meal planning, per holiday shopper trend research from FMI, the Food Industry Association. Among those celebrating at least one holiday this season, 34% say they'd use AI to find new recipes, 23% said they'd use it to plan the overall menu and 16% said they'd use it to make the holiday meal healthier. Friction point: Food bloggers and chefs have warned that traffic to their trusted websites is plummeting with the rise of AI recipe summaries, forcing some to quit the game altogether. Plus, AI recipes on the " slop "-pier side can be disastrous for the dinner table. The AI-generated recipes that pop up on Google's search results page "are often total nonsense," recipe blogger Eb Gargano told Axios in an email. "They've been generated by AI – and, at least last time I checked – AI cannot cook a recipe or taste a recipe," she said. Food bloggers have a name for those recipes, she added: "Frankenstein recipes." Underwood says she uses AI to help her with the operational side of her business. But when she recently asked a chatbot to scale her recipe for a large event and make a shopping list, it cut out ingredients — saying it was trying to improve her recipe. Our thought bubble: AI is not a chef — while it's accumulated years of IRL chefs' knowledge, it can still lead you astray. I asked ChatGPT to help me make my favorite Thanksgiving appetizers: stuffed mushrooms. I typically follow my mom's famous recipe (well, actually, Barefoot Contessa's , but shhh), but the AI-generated version differed in a few ways. It wanted me to preheat to 400 degrees and bake for 20 minutes or so, while my family's go-to calls for a cooler 325 for 50. Chat's recipe opts for butter, while the Contessa's does not. Some of the spices caught me off-guard as well: Red pepper flakes and thyme are not ingredients I usually throw in. To my untrained eye, the recipe still sounds tasty — but it's missing some of the key flavors in my dream stuffed mushroom, like sherry and scallions. The bottom line: AI can be an inventive appetizer, but human execution remains the main course. Underwood tells Axios: "AI has a place, but it doesn't replace human connection, and that involves even looking for recipes ... you can ask your friend as easily as you can ask AI."
