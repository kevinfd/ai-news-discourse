# AI may not be a bubble. But the way we’re financing it might be

Fast Company · Kolawole Samuel Adebayo · 2026-11-26

Jensen Huang is back at Nvidia. On November 23, the semiconductor giant reinstated its cofounder as president and CEO, less than two weeks after his abrupt resignation threw the company into a leadership crisis.
Investors may be relieved to have Huang back. But the events of the past two weeks have shown just how much Nvidia—and the wider AI industry—still depends on one man. The board said it had consulted senior U.S. government officials before reinstating Huang and concluded that his return posed no national security risk. Its investigation remains open, however, and Nvidia has announced additional measures to address national security and compliance concerns.
Huang’s departure also raised questions about Nvidia’s maturity as a corporate institution. In an analysis published shortly after Huang’s resignation, Andy Thurai, founder and principal analyst at The Field CTO, pointed to companies like Apple and IBM that spend years preparing their next generation of leaders. “That separates a mature corporate entity from a founder-led juggernaut,” he wrote. Huang is back, but Nvidia still needs to prepare for a future without him.
Meanwhile, another major artificial intelligence company was making history on Wall Street. Anthropic, the maker of Claude, went public on November 19, pricing its shares at $83 in one of the largest initial public offerings in history. At that price, every one of an estimated 96% of the company’s employees held equity worth more than $1 million, according to data from Hill.com reported by CNN.
The stock opened at $111.50 before closing its first trading day at $97.17. Investors who bought at the IPO price made money, but those who bought when trading opened were already sitting on losses.
Both developments come at a pivotal moment for the AI industry. Billions of dollars continue to pour into the sector, but how much money are the companies receiving those investments actually making? And how long will investors be willing to wait?
Judah Taub, managing partner at Hetz Ventures, thinks investors need to separate their excitement about AI from their assessment of the businesses behind it.
