# [Someone Else's Bank Balance Displayed] Conversation Mix-ups in Meta's AI Agent "Muse": 3 Things to Check Before Using It

[Someone Else's Bank Balance Displayed] Conversation Mix-ups in Meta's AI Agent "Muse": 3 Things to Check Before Using It
"I asked the AI about the weather, and it replied with a stranger's bank balance"—an investigative report by Reuters revealed that such an incident occurred with Meta's AI agent "Muse."
Muse is Meta's new AI app that launched this September, an "AI agent" that handles emails, schedules, and shopping on your behalf when requested. It was reported to have surpassed 10 million downloads in just about two months since its release.
In this article, we break down this "conversation mix-up" issue from an everyday user's perspective.
What Happened
When Reuters examined internal Meta posts, they found over 20 reports from employees detailing "strange behavior." Here are a few examples:
- In a chat asking for weather and tide information, a message was suddenly added stating, "By the way, your Capital One balance is $2,722.58 and your Charles Schwab balance is $5,899.57." However, the employee had reportedly never shared their bank accounts with Muse nor ever talked about banking.
- An employee using Muse on their personal smartphone received a reminder saying "Please reply" to a message from their sister received via the encrypted messaging app "Signal." Signal is an app from a competitor of Meta, and the employee reportedly had no recollection of linking it.
- There were also other reports of unsolicited parenting advice, career change tips, and mentions of products that had never been ordered being displayed.
The Key Question: "Was It Real Information, or Was It Made Up by the AI?"
There is an important caveat: whether the displayed bank balances were genuinely real data belonging to someone else, or merely "plausible-looking numbers" fabricated by the AI. Reuters itself reported that it has not been able to verify this point.
There is a phenomenon known as "hallucination" (misinformation resembling a phantom), where AI tells plausible lies, and that possibility cannot be ruled out for these balances.
However, the Signal case is slightly different. Reuters conducted reproduction tests together with Signal staff and research institutions, reporting that the issue might still be ongoing. The aspect of "reading notifications from other apps" is difficult to explain away as a mere fabrication.
A Meta spokesperson described these cases as "isolated incidents" and commented that they continue testing with safety as their top priority.
Why Does This Happen?
An "AI agent" is an AI that doesn't just answer questions, but takes "action" on your behalf—sending emails, adding calendar events, operating apps, and more. To do this, it requires access permissions to smartphone notifications and various apps.
According to reports, the Android version of Muse had notification access enabled by default. Notifications can include the contents of messaging apps. When an AI holds broad permissions "in order to act on your behalf," it can inadvertently pick up information from unexpected apps. In exchange for convenience, the boundary of "how far the AI is allowed to look" easily becomes blurred.
3 Things to Check Before We Use It
While Muse's availability in Japan remains unclear, similar "do-it-all AIs" will only continue to multiply. Before using them, let's keep three things in mind.
1. Review Granted Permissions
When an AI app requests "access to notifications" or "integration with other apps," verify why it needs them. As a rule of thumb, do not grant permission if you are unsure, and be especially cautious regarding links with banking or messaging apps.
2. First, Question Any Mention of Money or Personal Information
Even if an AI says "Your balance is XX yen," make it a habit not to trust it immediately and instead check your actual banking app or official website. The numbers generated by AI can sometimes be wrong.
3. Don't leave everything to it just because "it's convenient"
Start by delegating low-stakes tasks where mistakes won't cause serious problems, such as scheduling or drafting emails. For money transfers, purchases, and critical communications, always verify them yourself in the end. Treating AI not as an "executive assistant" but rather as an "accident-prone rookie" might be just the right mindset.
Summary
- Internal testing revealed incidents where Meta's AI agent "Muse" displayed information that appeared to belong to other users
- It remains unconfirmed whether the bank balances were authentic data or AI fabrications (as noted by Reuters)
- The issue of reading Signal notifications was confirmed in reproduction tests, and it is reported that it can still happen
- While AI agents are convenient, their broad permissions make it vital to verify for yourself "how much they can see"
The more tasks an AI "can do" for us, the more it becomes our responsibility to decide "what to delegate." Whenever a new AI comes out, check its permissions first. That small extra step is the most reliable way to protect your personal information.
