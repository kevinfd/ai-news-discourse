# Latin America has the copper and energy the AI build-out needs. Infrastructure is the test

CNBC · Natalie Feary · 2026-11-26

Latin America’s copper, energy and mineral resources could play a growing role in supplying the global artificial-intelligence build-out, as data centers and electrification drive demand for power and raw materials.
But for investors, it's less about whether Latin America has the resources than whether governments can turn them into viable projects.
"We are going through an acceleration in energy demand growth. We need every possible energy source available," Michele Della Vigna, head of natural resources research for Goldman Sachs, told CNBC. "Ultimately, what drives economic growth is energy," he added.
Ben Kumar, head of strategy for wealth, investment and public policy at 7IM, suggested Latin America could benefit, given the physical materials needed for AI equipment and data centers. "We are currently neutral in the short term, but if the materials and mining cycle continues, we could see this moving to positive in a few months," he said.
Where the investment case is strongest
The investment case varies across the region. Chile has copper; Argentina is expanding oil and gas exports; and Brazil has abundant renewable power and significant rare earth reserves.
Chile's copper sector illustrates both the supply potential and the constraints to expanding supply. Copper production fell 8.5% in the first nine months of 2026, according to the state copper commission Cochilco, as lower ore grades, maintenance, and operational disruptions constrained supply.
But demand for refined copper is set to grow 1.6% this year and 2% next year, according to the International Copper Study Group. Supply is struggling to keep pace with demand from expanding electricity grids.
Chile's copper commission has raised its 2027 price forecast to $5.95 a pound, from $5.10. Codelco, the state-owned copper producer, may also have scope to increase supply, including through proposed public-private partnerships.
Rafael Ch, partner and senior analyst for Latin America and emerging markets at Signum Global Advisors, sees demand continuing to grow. "As countries expand electricity grids, they need more copper regardless of which power source they choose," he said.
The scale of the opportunity will depend in part on how quickly energy demand grows. Goldman Sachs forecasts that global power demand for data centers will grow by 170% by 2030. The IEA projected in April that global data center electricity consumption would rise from 485 terawatt-hours in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030.
Turning resources into investment
The investment case also depends on whether the region can capture more value from the resources it produces. Latin America and the Caribbean refined only 26% of the copper they mined in 2025, according to the IEA.
"Cautious optimism is justified and there will be massive critical mineral demand," said Monica de Bolle, senior fellow at the Peterson Institute for International Economics.
"But improving refining capacity will be crucial to capture more of that value," she added.
Some projects have already secured financing. Chilean explorer Marimaca Copper Corp selected a preferred lender for a $400 million debt facility to build a mine on Oct. 28. The funding will go towards developing an open-pit mine in the north of Chile that will produce 50,000 metric tons of refined copper annually. Production is expected from 2029.
Argentina shows the other side of the opportunity: strong oil and gas resources, but insufficient infrastructure to get more production to global markets.
"I see an opportunity in the infrastructure that gets Argentine oil and gas to market. A producer in Vaca Muerta can have excellent wells and still struggle to expand exports without pipeline capacity and access to ports.
That gives investors a reason to look at transport and export infrastructure alongside production," Rafael Ch, partner and senior analyst for Latin America and emerging markets at Signum Global Advisors, added. Vaca Muerta is Argentina's vast shale basin.
Argentina is also attracting investment in drilling and export infrastructure. International capital markets have reopened to investment in drilling and pipelines in Argentina. That investment has already driven the build-out of a pipeline and port. Argentina will then be able to export far more crude oil. Chevron and American oil tycoon Harold Hamm have also increased their investments in Argentina.
Brazil's case rests partly on its renewable-heavy power mix. About 89% of the country's electricity was generated from renewable sources in 2025, according to the country's Ministry of Mines and Energy.
"That puts Brazil in a strong position to benefit as AI and data centers drive up demand," Stephanie Link, chief investment strategist and portfolio manager at Hightower Advisors, told CNBC.
But while Brazil has the world's second-largest reported rare-earth reserves, its estimated mine production is far smaller than China's. USGS estimates put Brazil's output at 2,000 metric tons in 2025, compared with China's 270,000 metric tons. Brazil's reserves are estimated at 11 million metric tons by the USGS, second only to China's 44 million metric tons.
For investors, the gap between Brazil's vast reserves and limited current production is both the opportunity and the execution risk.
The risks to watch
For investors, the policy question is whether governments can make projects viable and keep them on track.
Luis Costa, Citi's global head of emerging markets strategy, said governments need to offer incentives, particularly in the early stages of projects, because infrastructure lags the region's resource wealth. He also stressed that support must last: infrastructure projects can take several years to develop.
The World Bank's Latin America and the Caribbean Economic Update for 2026 projects growth for the region of 2.2%, and highlights the "restricted fiscal space, sticky inflation and high borrowing costs."
"The economic policy and fiscal policy needs to get in line as markets are globally becoming more sensitive to fiscal stories," said Costa.
Brazil will need to establish fiscal credibility to attract investment, particularly if higher public spending puts additional pressure on debt and borrowing costs. "If investors doubt the government can stabilize public debt, companies' long-term financing costs could stay high even as the central bank lowers its policy rate," Ch added.
With Argentina set for national elections in October 2027, political continuity will also matter for energy investment. "A return to tighter currency controls or persistent deficits would make companies more likely to delay projects, even in an oil field with attractive economics," said Ch.
Pro-business President Javier Milei faces a tight race for re-election, though a change in leadership would not necessarily undermine medium- to long-term investment.
"There is also the possibility of a pragmatic Peronist ticket emerging, which markets would be satisfied with," said Nicolas Jaquier, emerging-market fixed-income portfolio manager at Ninety One.
For those governing across Latin America, policies to unlock its wealth of resources will be key for it to emerge as a winner from the AI demand boom.
"Supply chain security is becoming more and more relevant, and if commodities take off Latin America can be a clear and obvious winner," said Kumar.
