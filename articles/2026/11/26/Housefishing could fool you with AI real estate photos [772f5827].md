# Housefishing could fool you with AI real estate photos

Fox News · Kurt Knutsson, CyberGuy Report · 2026-11-26

You find a beautiful house online. The driveway looks brand new, and the landscaping could be straight out of a home magazine. You get excited, schedule a showing and start thinking about where your furniture might go. Then you pull up to the address and wonder whether you're looking at the same house.
That's the growing concern behind housefishing, a term for real estate listings that use digitally altered or AI-generated photos to make properties look better than they actually are. A recent report highlighted complaints from homebuyers and renters who described misleading property pictures as "false advertising." With AI tools becoming more convincing, it's getting harder to know whether you're looking at a home's actual condition or a digital makeover. So how can you tell whether a listing photo shows the home you're actually getting, and what should you check before scheduling a tour?

What is housefishing in real estate?
Housefishing happens when property photos create an unrealistic impression of a home. AI can make an outdated property appear freshly renovated without anyone doing the actual work. For instance, two photos can show the same two-story house, but create very different impressions.
The original photo can show a home with a dark asphalt driveway and large shrubs covering much of the front porch. But an AI-enhanced version can show a clean concrete driveway and a newly styled entrance.
This example shows how digital alterations can make a property appear to have undergone an expensive makeover. If those improvements exist only in the edited photo, someone visiting the property could be in for an unpleasant surprise.
When do AI real estate photos become misleading?
Virtual staging has been around for years. Adding digital furniture to an empty living room can help buyers understand how they might use the space. AI simply makes those transformations easier and more realistic. But replacing a worn-out kitchen with an AI-generated renovation is another story. Laurent Cardinal, founder of real estate technology company Planpoint at planpoint.io/, believes agents need to be upfront about what they've changed. "The line is drawn where the intent is," Cardinal explains.
He supports using AI to show what a home could look like after renovations, provided agents clearly disclose the changes. His recommendation is straightforward: Show the original photo alongside the edited version so prospective buyers can compare them. However, digitally hiding property defects or replacing an unattractive view crosses that line. "That's cheating and it's hurtful to everyone in the market," Cardinal says. A misleading listing can waste someone's time and influence important financial decisions. For renters, the consequences can be especially serious if they send a deposit based on photos that fail to reflect the property.
Why AI-generated property photos are getting harder to spot
You might think you'd recognize an AI-generated photo immediately. Maybe the furniture looks strange or a window seems out of place. Unfortunately, today's tools can produce convincing results. Sometimes the changes are subtle enough to escape a close look. Cardinal warns that even people familiar with AI-generated content can struggle to identify it. "But there's no silver bullet here, and anyone can get caught," he says.
Unusually perfect rooms can raise questions, especially when architectural details appear inconsistent between photos. However, traditional real estate photography can also be misleading. Wide-angle lenses make rooms appear larger, while carefully chosen lighting can minimize visible imperfections. AI creates an additional concern because it can alter the features of the property itself. That makes it increasingly difficult to judge a home's condition from listing photos alone.

Are agents required to disclose AI-altered real estate photos?
The rules depend on where the property is located and which listing service an agent uses. California has already taken action. Under Assembly Bill 723, which took effect Jan. 1, 2026, real estate professionals must disclose covered digital alterations in advertisements for properties for sale. They must also provide access to the original, unaltered photos. The law covers changes made using traditional editing software or AI. Routine adjustments to lighting or color generally fall outside the requirements when they don't misrepresent the property. California has also approved Assembly Bill 2025. Starting Jan. 1, 2027, the state will require disclosures for digitally altered rental advertisements, with access to original photos when existing pictures have been changed.
Meanwhile, New York has warned agents about misleading AI property pictures. In November 2025, the New York Department of State reminded real estate professionals that advertisements must accurately represent properties for sale or rent. Agents who publish deceptive advertisements can face disciplinary action. Multiple listing services have their own restrictions. Stellar MLS, for example, requires disclosure of virtually staged photos and prohibits edits that conceal property defects or alter permanent features. There is currently no single nationwide law requiring every AI-edited real estate photo to carry a disclosure. Still, existing advertising laws and professional rules can apply when pictures mislead prospective buyers.
Can AI help detect housefishing?
Interestingly, the same technology creating misleading property photos may also help identify them. Cardinal's company, Planpoint, connects listing photos to interactive floor plans. That allows prospective buyers to see how rooms fit together instead of relying on individual pictures that may be difficult to interpret. Its Planpoint Visit platform also offers AI photo detection. The technology checks newly uploaded property photos for signs of AI generation and displays a warning when it finds something suspicious. Cardinal says the company achieved a 98% success rate in its internal testing.
How to protect yourself from housefishing
Before scheduling a showing or putting money down on a property, there are several ways to check whether the listing photos tell the whole story.
Ask for the original, unedited photos
If a home looks suspiciously polished, ask the agent whether any pictures have been digitally altered. Request the original photos so you can compare them. Pay particular attention to kitchens and bathrooms, where expensive renovations can significantly affect a home's value. If an agent cannot explain what was changed, ask more questions before moving forward.
Request a live video walkthrough
Cardinal recommends a simple way to verify a property's appearance. "The easiest way is to request a live video call with the agent or the owner," he says. Ask to see the areas you're most interested in, including anything that looks unusually perfect in the listing. A live walkthrough can reveal details that edited photos might hide. However, it cannot prove that the person showing the home has authority to rent or sell it.
Compare the listing photos with the floor plan
Look carefully at the room dimensions and window locations. Then compare those details with the photos. Cardinal suggests asking, "Do the rooms and angles add up?" If a small bedroom appears enormous in a listing, ask whether the pictures were digitally altered. Remember that wide-angle photography can also exaggerate the apparent size of a room.
Check the house on Google Street View
Search the property's address in Google Maps and look at the exterior using Street View. Compare the driveway and surrounding homes with what's shown in the listing. You might notice changes that deserve a closer look. However, Street View pictures can be outdated, so differences don't automatically mean an agent used AI.
Reverse-search the property photos
Use Google Lens or another reverse-image search tool to check whether the same property pictures appear elsewhere online. You may find older listings showing the home before it was digitally enhanced. You could also discover that someone copied photos from another property to create a fraudulent rental advertisement. Verify the property before sending money
Never rely solely on listing photos when making a financial commitment. If you're renting, independently verify the identity and authority of the landlord or agent before sending a deposit. For a home purchase, arrange an in-person showing and an independent inspection before committing to buy. A polished listing photo cannot tell you whether the property has hidden problems.

I'd like to see more transparency from real estate agents, with original photos available alongside any AI-enhanced versions.
