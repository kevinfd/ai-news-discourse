# How the Next Congress Will Tackle AI Copyright Questions

Newsweek · Lauren Giella · 2026-11-26

Artificial intelligence continues to be a top priority for federal lawmakers as the new Congressional session is set to begin in the new year.
Members of Congress have already put forward legislation to regulate rogue AI agents, data centers and privacy concerns. But one area of AI policy remains unclear: Copyright.
The bipartisan spirit of AI regulation in Congress and growing concerns over AI's rapid evolution might prompt lawmakers to take real action to clarify the rules, protecting creators while maintaining innovation in the tech world.

Current Copyright Law for Generative AI Models
Since copyright laws were written before generative AI was used, the question of whether AI models can be trained on copyrighted materials is lawful fair use remains unsettled as it is litigated in federal courts.
A federal court in 2023 held that "human authorship is an essential part of a valid copyright claim," meaning that only human authors need copyright as a creative incentive. That ruling was upheld in 2025, when the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit found that the Copyright Act "requires all eligible work to be authored in the first instance by a human being."
In 2025, the Senate Judiciary Committee's Subcommittee on Crime and Counterterrorism held a hearing on copyright law in the age of generative AI. One of the panelists, attorney Maxwell Pritt, said that AI companies like Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic are "running the largest domestic piracy of intellectual property in our nation's history" by training their AI models on books and other copyrighted works.
Pritt rejected the fair use claims and urged lawmakers to enact stronger copyright enforcement. Author David Baldacci said that discovering that an AI system could generate storylines that rip off his work felt like someone had "stolen" everything he'd ever created.
Proposed Federal Copyright Bills
The TRAIN Act, or the Transparency and Responsibility for Artificial Intelligence Networks Act, aims to help creators and artists access the courts to protect their copyrighted works if they are used to train generative AI models. It allows copyright holders to access the training records used for AI models to determine whether their work was used.
The bipartisan bill was introduced in the Senate in 2025 by Democrat Peter Welch of Vermont, Republican Marsha Blackburn of Tennessee, Democrat Adam Schiff of California and Republican Josh Hawley of Missouri. It was introduced in the House for the first time in January by Representative Madeleine Dean of Pennsylvania and Representative Nathaniel Moran of Texas.
"As AI rapidly evolves and becomes more present in our lives, our laws must catch up—that includes preserving the dignity of artists and the authenticity of their work," Dean said. "There is no path for creators to know if their work has been used—without their permission and without compensation—to train AI models."
Moran, a Republican, added that transparency is essential to ensuring innovation is built on integrity and respect for original work.
"AI is a rapidly evolving and promising frontier, and we must advance it responsibly by protecting American creators while encouraging technologies that reward creativity, collaboration, and proper attribution," he said.
The TRAIN Act is endorsed by several creative organizations, like SAG-AFTRA, the Recording Academy and the Authors Guild.
Other legislation introduced in the 119th Congress, such as the COPIED Media Act (S. 1396), the AI Accountability and Personal Data Protection Act (S. 2367), and the TRUMP AMERICA AI Act (discussion draft), would impose civil liability for training AI in some or all copyrighted material.
What Has Trump Said About AI Copyright?
The White House released its national AI legislative framework in March, outlining the administration's goals and roadmap for AI policy as the technology continues to evolve.
In the framework, the White House said creators, publishers and innovators should be protected from AI-generated outputs that infringe on their protected work without stifling lawful innovation and free expression.
"Although the Administration believes that training of AI models on copyrighted material does not violate copyright laws, it acknowledges arguments to the contrary exist and therefore supports allowing the Courts to resolve this issue," the framework said. "Similarly, Congress should not take any actions that would impact the judiciary’s resolution of whether training on copyrighted material constitutes fair use."
According to the White House, Congress should consider a voluntary licensing or collective rights system to allow rights holders to negotiate compensation from AI providers "without incurring antitrust liability."
It also suggests that Congress protect individuals from unauthorized use and distribution of their voice or likeness, "continue to carefully monitor the development of copyright precedents and enforcement in the courts," and consider additional protective actions needed as AI continues to evolve.
In September, the Department of Justice filed a brief supporting OpenAI's defense against copyright infringement, arguing that the U.S. has a strong interest in "rejecting any argument that training [large language models] on copyrighted texts violates copyright law."
What Is the Plan for Congress in 2027?
The Congressional Research Services released its Generative AI and Copyright Law report last week. The report concluded that federal copyright law is currently being applied to AI through existing legal doctrine, not through AI-specific legislation.
Congress, therefore, faces many regulatory options in the next year. It can either pass legislation to clarify the unresolved issues or it can continue to let the courts shape the law.
Policymakers would have to determine whether AI companies would need to obtain licenses from copyright holders, which could be difficult at scale. Other options include a voluntary licensing agreements, collective rights management organizations similar to those used in the music industry, compulsory licensing regimes and opt-out systems for creators.
