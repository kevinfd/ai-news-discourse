# FCC Green-Lights SpaceX Scheme for One Million Data Centers in Orbit

PCMag · Rob Pegoraro · 2026-11-26

Less than 10 months after requesting government approval for a plan to put one million data-center satellites into Earth orbit, SpaceX got an early Christmas present on Thanksgiving Eve: a green light for the vision that has become a singular focus of CEO Elon Musk.
The FCC announced the approval in a Wednesday press release, parts of which could have been written by an AI model trained on Musk's tweets. "At this pivotal time in America's development of super intelligence, the FCC is granting SpaceX authorization to deploy 1,000,000 orbital compute modules to process massive amounts of data to be used back here on Earth," it reads. "More fundamentally, it promises to bring the human race closer to a Kardashev II civilization—one that can harness the full power of the sun."
That refers to a metric of technological progress that Musk frequently invokes while leaving the connection between putting a lot of computers in space and exploiting the Sun's total output as an exercise for the reader.
A 14-page memorandum offers more details on the FCC's approval of SpaceX's "Starmind" plan for those million AI1 satellites. With an average 175kW of compute capacity powered by solar panels with a 246-foot wingspan–wider than that of a Boeing 777–they would require the company's giant, still-in-development Starship rocket.
These satellites would operate at altitudes ranging from 342 to 623 miles, with approval for orbits above about 373 miles pending until the FCC rules on SpaceX's plan to dispose of these satellites at the end of their useful lives in higher orbits.
The memo gives SpaceX some time to start this massive project, setting a deadline of Nov. 25, 2033, for the first orbital data center deployment and allowing two more years to have the first 100,000 working in orbit. It would then have to reach the half-million mark by Nov. 25, 2038, and cross the million-satellite mark by Nov. 25, 2040.
The FCC allowed SpaceX's request to use Ka-band frequencies–18.8 to 19.3GHz and 28.6 to 29.1GHz–for backup communications should its inter-satellite laser data links fail, on condition that they cause no interference to existing users of those bands. Existing and future Starlink satellites would handle relaying data to and from ground users.
The FCC memo brushes off other concerns as problems for other agencies to solve. The fears astronomers already have over interference with their observations? Not in the FCC's bailiwick because its "rules do not address the protection of optical astronomy." What about potential air pollution from all the rocket launches that would be required? Talk to the Federal Aviation Administration, "the agency responsible for conducting review of the environmental effects of rocket launches."
The FCC document entirely dismisses the possible pollution caused by so many spent data-center satellites getting reduced to metal dust during atmospheric reentry as "speculative and not supported in the record."
The environmentalist group Earthjustice lit into the memo in a statement on Wednesday, sharing objections from opponents of SpaceX's bid. "Allowing a million orbiting data centers with no environmental review isn't just irresponsible — it's reckless," says Tim Whitehouse, executive director of Public Employees for Environmental Responsibility.
"It is dismaying that the FCC has ignored the law so blatantly and tried to mask the fact with a holiday filing," added Jan Hasselman, senior attorney at Earthjustice.
The actual feasibility, both engineering and economic, of launching one million large satellites gets another backhand in the FCC memo. But that should not be overlooked either.
The Starlink constellation has grown enormously to 9,643 operational satellites, per astronomer Jonathan McDowell's count, but that is still far fewer than one million.
Ars Technica space reporter Eric Berger, author of two exceptionally well-sourced books on SpaceX, tried doing the math in July on the company's ability to get this launch job done with Starship. In his optimistic case, where a Starship can deliver 57 AI1 satellites at a cost of $1 million per flight and a launch cost of $20 million, SpaceX would need 17,500 launches over a five-year period (almost 10 a day), for a total expense of $1.45 trillion.
In his pessimistic case, where Starship only lofts 13 AI1 satellites at $2 million each, and every Starship launch costs $100 million, Starmind requires 77,000 launches over that five-year span (about 42 a day) and its all-in cost soars to $9.8 trillion.
It seems clear that Americans don't like data centers anywhere near their backyards. But the eagerness of SpaceX investors to bankroll an experiment to put them a few hundred miles above people's backyards is yet another issue the FCC is leaving for others to ponder.
