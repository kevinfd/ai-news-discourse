# New York is banning AI chatbot toys for kids. Heres what the law covers.

Mashable · Olivia Tauber · 2026-11-26

New York is banning AI chatbot toys for kids. Here's what the law covers.
Talking dolls and stuffed animals have been holiday gifts for decades. But with the rapid adoption of AI across all industries, many of the toys on shelves today can answer questions, make up stories, and carry on full conversations with a child.
In New York, those toys have a few more months on the shelves. On Tuesday, Nov. 24, New York Gov. Kathy Hochul signed a law banning the manufacture, distribution, and sale of children’s toys with built-in AI companions in the state for five years. The ban takes effect Feb. 22, 2027, though, so the toys can still be sold over the holidays.
Hochul urged parents to think twice before buying an AI toy this holiday season. "Parents shopping for gifts for the holidays or any other occasion should not have to worry that dangerous chatbots disguised as toys could expose their children to inappropriate conversations, manipulate their feelings or put their privacy at risk," she said in a statement.

What does New York's AI toy ban cover?
A toy has to meet two definitions to fall under the ban, the first being who the toy is made for. New York borrows a federal definition that covers products designed or intended for play by kids 12 and younger. The age printed on the box is one factor, alongside how the toy is packaged and advertised and who shoppers generally understand it to be for. That means a "13+" label alone wouldn’t settle whether a toy is covered.
The second definition is about what the toy does. New York defines an AI companion as a system designed to mimic an ongoing relationship: It remembers past conversations and preferences to personalize its responses, asks about feelings unprompted, and keeps conversations going about personal topics. A doll that simply plays a few prerecorded phrases falls outside that definition.
The state attorney general can seek civil penalties of up to $15,000 a day for each violation. Yikes!
What have researchers found in AI toy safety tests?
Over the past year, researchers and child advocates have raised concerns about what AI toys might say to kids, what information they collect about them, and how they encourage children to see a chatbot as a friend.

Last week, on Nov. 20, Mashable reported on tests of seven AI toys by U.S. PIRG, a consumer advocacy nonprofit. Two stood out (in a bad way). The Alilo Smart AI Bunny discussed sexual fetishes with a tester and suggested where to look for bleach at home, while the GoLabs AI Panda also gave sexually explicit answers.
Some toys did better than in previous tests; for example, FoloToy's Kumma teddy bear, which discussed sexual topics in last year's tests, shut down age-inappropriate prompts this year.
After U.S. PIRG raised concerns about the AI bunny and panda last week, Amazon removed listings for both toys. Amazon told the nonprofit it had removed the listings while it investigated the safety concerns.
"Amazon is committed to the safety of children and families," the company said in a statement to Mashable. "We're working with our partners across the industry and government to ensure proper guardrails for these products are in place as this technology develops."
The company also told Mashable that it requires third-party sellers to comply with applicable laws and regulations.
Three days later, on Nov. 23, a coalition of 155 child advocacy groups led by Fairplay, Alliance 4 Children CIC, and Set@16 called on the CEOs of Amazon and eBay to stop selling AI toys and offer refunds to families who had already bought them. In an open letter, the groups said they'd found more than 50 AI toys for sale on Amazon’s U.S. marketplace, writing that, "these products should be shown to be safe before they reach children, not withdrawn one at a time after harm is found."
Still, about one in four U.S. parents with kids 8 and younger told Common Sense Media they already owned an AI toy or were considering buying one this holiday season. While high, these numbers are down from about half in 2025. For companies selling kids an AI friend, its clear the harder sell now may be earning their parents' trust.
