# The hidden math behind this paradoxical Black Friday sale strategy

Scientific American · Jack Murtagh · 2026-11-26

“Found a lower price elsewhere? We’ll match it.” When big-box stores make these promises they sound like they’re on your side. Best Buy, Home Depot and Lowe’s will match prices from Amazon, Walmart and more than a dozen other retailers. Some go even further. Australian hardware giant Bunnings promises to beat any competitor’s lower price by a full 10 percent. In the U.S. Home Depot, Lowe’s and Sears once gave similar offers.
But game theory suggests these guarantees are less generous than they appear. In fact, they can paradoxically keep prices high. One telling clue is that many retailers switch off their competitor price matching around Black Friday and Cyber Monday, the most competitive shopping stretch of the year.
In 1883 French mathematician Joseph Bertrand published a scathing review of a book that offered one of the first mathematical models of economic competition. In that model, rival firms decide how much to produce, and the market settles on what the price is. Bertrand objected by saying that real sellers compete by setting and adapting prices, not production quantities. In just a few sentences, he sketched an argument that still informs how economists think about price competition.

Suppose a retailer called Store A sells a television that costs $500 to buy directly from the manufacturer. If it were the only store selling TVs, it would charge the price that earns it the most money, with no competition to incentivize a lower price." Perhaps $900 marks the store’s sweet spot: enough shoppers will pay that much, but if the cost were any higher, too many would decide the TV wasn’t worth it.
Now introduce a competitor, Store B, carrying the same TV, which also costs it $500. If both stores charge $900, customers might feel indifferent about where to purchase a TV, and the stores will roughly split the sales. But if Store A drops its price to $899, then every bargain hunter will go its way, and Store A will reap all of the profits. In the face of this, Store B drops its price to $898, and so on in a race to the bottom.
Left unchecked, this undercutting would stop only at $500, with neither store earning a dime on the sale. Real prices rarely fall quite that far. Stores differ in location and brand loyalty, shoppers don’t check every ad, and stores eventually learn that if they cut prices once, they’ll have to do so again and again to stay competitive.
Yet Bertrand’s core insight still holds: sellers compete via price setting, and the threat of being undercut prevents stores from charging whatever they want. Both stores in the hypothetical scenario would be better off holding at $900, but they don’t. That’s because each worries the other will lower the price, and any agreement to hold the line would amount to price-fixing collusion, which is illegal. It’s a version of the prisoner’s dilemma that happens to benefit consumers. Both sellers fare better when they collude, but they can’t trust each other, so instead they compete. That’s why economists worry about monopolies. A store with no rivals to fear can charge the profit-maximizing $900 and make shoppers either pay above their comfort zone or skip the product altogether.
Now suppose Store A hangs a sign saying, “We’ll match any competitor’s price.” When Store B considers cutting to $899, the calculation changes. Without Store A’s price-matching offer, Store B’s cut would poach every bargain hunter. Now those shoppers can show Store B’s price at Store A and pay $899 there, so Store B wins no new customers and earns less on the ones it already had. If Store A goes further and promises to beat lower prices from rivals, as some stores do, then Store B’s price cut would actually backfire, sending Store B’s customers to Store A for a better deal. When these guarantees are in place, cutting prices no longer pays, so both stores stay at $900. Without any backroom dealings, the two stores have landed on the price that a monopoly would choose—and the ploy that secured this reads like generosity.
Three things about price matching are worth noticing. First, only one store needs the guarantee—Store A’s promise to match competitors' prices protects Store B’s high price as much as its own. Second, a guarantee that functions as intended almost never gets used. In the idealized model nobody undercuts, so no shopper ever has a lower price to bring to the register. Third, the sign looks like it’s addressed to consumers, but it’s actually a message to rival stores.
Shoppers rarely see it this way. A 2003 paper in the Journal of Consumer Psychology tellingly entitled “Failing to Suspect Collusion in Price-Matching Guarantees” concluded that people tend to interpret price-matching offers as a signal that prices are already low, not as a way of keeping them high.
Whether real stores behave in accordance with Bertrand’s model is an empirical question, and the evidence says they often do, under the right conditions. A 1991 study tracked supermarket prices before and after stores adopted price-matching guarantees and found that price matching caused higher prices overall. More recently economist Ran Zhuo examined what happened when big-box chains began promising to match Amazon. Theory predicted that Amazon would stop undercutting—and that’s exactly what Zhuo’s 2017 paper reports. Amazon’s prices rose by roughly six percentage points after the guarantees took effect, according to the paper.
Of course, real markets get messy and rarely play out exactly as described in economics textbooks. The effect is strongest under particular conditions: a few big rivals selling identical products where price matches are easy for customers to claim. Economists Morten Hviid and Greg Shaffer showed in 1999 that price-matching offers lose their power when shoppers have to jump through hoops to claim them. Rivals know that few customers will bother, so the threat meant to deter undercutting loses credibility.
Retailers pitch price-matching guarantees as a promise to make sure you never overpay. But if that were their purpose, then the guarantees would matter most during sales events like Black Friday, when prices swing wildly. Instead that’s exactly when many retailers switch them off. On a normal day a price-matching guarantee discourages rivals from undercutting, so the offer costs stores very little. It works as a threat that rarely gets called. Doorbuster events such as Black Friday and Cyber Monday are all about steep cuts, however, and those cuts will come no matter what, so price matching won’t deter stores from making them. In this setting, price matching wouldn’t operate as a low-cost threat but rather a costly mistake, with stores beholden to every rival’s lowest price. Retailers tend not to explain why they suspend price matching during sales events, yet the timing is telling.
None of this means you should skip a price match when you find one. If a guarantee saves you money, use it. Just read the offer for what it is: a signal to the store down the street that undercutting won’t pay.
