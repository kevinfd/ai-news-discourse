# Thomson Reuters is using both American and Chinese AI for its next model

Business Insider · Charles Rollet, Charles Rollet (crollet@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-26

Thomson Reuters CEO Steve Hasker. John Lamparski/Getty Images for Advertising Week New York Forget the AI race: Thomson Reuters wants the best of both worlds. The company's next AI model is built off Google's Gemma and Z.ai's GLM, it tells Business Insider. The data and legal giant has spent $40 million so far building its own chatbots. As China and the US race to compete on AI, Thomson Reuters, the Canadian data and legal giant, is having it both ways. The company is launching a new AI model called Thomson 1.1 by the end of the year, it tells Business Insider exclusively — and it's built on open-source models from both countries: China's GLM, from Z.ai, and Google's Gemma. By using open-source models that anyone can download and host, Thomson Reuters hopes to reduce its AI costs and take more control over its intellectual property. There's a battle in tech over which AI corporations will use. It pits cheaper Chinese open-source models against closed ones from giants like Anthropic and OpenAI. US companies like Google, Meta, and Thinking Machines Lab have also released powerful open-source models in recent months. It's a shift for Thomson Reuters , which didn't use American AI for its previous in-house model, Thomson 1.0. Instead, it built its chatbot off Qwen, an AI from the Chinese tech giant Alibaba. "The US government, and many other governments around the world, prefer to have US and Western sovereign models at the foundation of what we do," Thomson Reuters' chief technology officer, Joel Hron, told Business Insider. "Where we can, we're going to use Western and US-based labs to ground Thomson." Hron said the company still uses Z.ai's GLM because it excels at using computers — a key feature of AI agents . It also chose Google's Gemma because of its small size and efficiency, he added. Thomson Reuters aims to build an AI that helps its clients with mid-tier work, such as tax advice. For the most difficult tasks, it still plans to use OpenAI and Anthropic. "There's a lot of work that happens in the middle, and that's where we get real economic benefit," Hron said. Open source has its limitations It takes a lot of money for a big company to build a custom AI chatbot, even if it uses free, open-source models. Hron told Business Insider that so far, Thomson Reuters has spent about $40 million on the effort to tweak and tune its own models. That money goes to things like chips, data, and salaries for AI researchers. Adoption is early. Less than 5% of all AI tokens used at Thomson Reuters come from its own AI, Hron said — although that is set to ramp up in the coming years as its focus areas expand. Chinese models also have built-in censorship features that can be tricky to unwind. Hirundo, a startup in Israel, sent Business Insider an analysis showing that one version of the current Thomson model parrots Chinese government talking points. For example, it refused to acknowledge the Tiananmen Square massacre and said that "Taiwan is an inalienable part of China." A Thomson Reuters spokesperson said that its AI handles such questions "substantially better" when it's used as intended — with a set of instructions to follow. The company's coming AI model further reduces bias, the spokesperson added. To large companies hit by high token bills — like Pinterest , AT&T, and others — the appeal of open source remains strong: it can reduce recurring AI costs and give companies technology they own and control.
