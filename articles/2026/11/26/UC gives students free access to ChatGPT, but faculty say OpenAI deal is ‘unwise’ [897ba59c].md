# UC gives students free access to ChatGPT, but faculty say OpenAI deal is ‘unwise’

The Mercury News · EdSource · 2026-11-26

By William Melhado, EdSource
The University of California is procuring full access to ChatGPT Edu for all students and faculty as part of a $1.2 million agreement with OpenAI, despite concerns from faculty leaders about the academic consequences associated with artificial intelligence.
The one-year partnership, which takes effect in December, is an extension of a 2024 agreement with OpenAI. The agreement surprised members of UC’s Academic Senate, who raised concerns late last month that administrators had not sufficiently consulted faculty on the partnership. UC students, staff and faculty will also have access to OpenAI’s coding agent Codex.
Administrators met with UC faculty after the Academic Senate raised concerns in an October letter related to data privacy, intellectual property protections and the impact on student learning.
“While procurement is typically handled by administrators, this case is far more serious than a routine software purchase,” Academic Senate Chair Susannah Scott said in a statement. “The Academic Senate believes it is unwise to provide students with universal access to a tool capable of performing the academic work required to earn a degree without first addressing the concerns of the faculty who are responsible for curriculum, teaching, and academic integrity.”
Scott said the faculty wants administrators to “engage in genuine consultation with faculty experts so that UC can provide the most appropriate tools to its students and instructors, as well as guidance for their use.”
A UC spokesperson said administrators have discussed the OpenAI partnership with Academic Senate leadership and other faculty members since this spring.
“Their feedback has been thoughtful and helpful,” the spokesperson wrote in a statement. “The University will continue working with the Academic Senate and other faculty groups on best AI practices to support teaching and learning.”
Faculty across California debate use of AI
UC is not the only public university system where faculty and administrators are at odds over the adoption of AI tools on campus. Earlier this month, California State University faculty adopted a resolution requesting university leaders scale back efforts to incorporate AI into curriculum, and instead limit use of the technology for administrative tasks.
Both university leaders and AI companies have argued that access to tools such as ChatGPT is necessary to prepare students for a rapidly evolving job market. UC announced additional training opportunities as part of the expanded partnership with OpenAI. University leaders also emphasized that students and faculty are not required to use the technology or incorporate it into classrooms.
“By signing this agreement and making the tools available to our faculty, staff, and students, it’s going to help us maintain a forward-looking position as far as AI adoption,” said Camille Crittenden, executive director of CITRIS, a technology research institute at UC Berkeley.
Crittenden acknowledged faculty’s concerns, but said that large universities, particularly highly regarded institutions such as UC, need to ensure students, staff and faculty “have the appropriate tools to do their work for the 21st century.”
UC San Diego Chancellor Pradeep Khosla said in an interview that he has seen AI tools used in novel ways by faculty and staff on his campus. He applauded the OpenAI partnership for expanding ChatGPT access to students, faculty and staff.
Khosla acknowledged that AI has the ability to create a significant amount of wealth and enhance people’s abilities, but that it also could lead to further inequities. “I think our job at universities is to figure out how do you balance that,” he said.
UC president says OpenAI deal benefits students
During the September board of regents meeting, UC administrators proposed adopting systemwide access to AI tools produced by OpenAI or Anthropic. The Academic Senate would be consulted before an agreement was signed, according to the update from UC’s Office of the President.
But Academic Senate leadership said they weren’t made aware of the purchase agreement with OpenAI until a month later, when they met with UC administrators.
Scott, the Academic Senate chair, told UC President James Milliken in her October letter that providing students with tools such as ChatGPT, which can do much of students’ work, would be perceived as endorsing students’ AI use in all courses. That, she said, would “sit in tension” with professors who may seek to restrict the use of AI in classrooms.
Scott requested that the Academic Senate be allowed to provide feedback before UC finalized an agreement with OpenAI. She also asked that ChatGPT not be deployed for academic purposes on campuses until faculty could weigh in on limitations over how AI would be used in instruction.
“Unfortunately, the core concerns raised in our October 30 letter remain unresolved,” Scott said.
Scott said the Academic Senate must establish ethical and pedagogical guidelines before UC widely distributes AI tools into the curriculum. She also urged faculty members to adopt data privacy protections for their research when using AI.
In an October email to Scott, UC Chief Information Officer Van Williams acknowledged faculty’s desire to give input on the partnership, but said the university needed to move quickly on the deal.
“The current negotiating window gives us a timely opportunity to secure terms that would lower costs, strengthen enterprise protections, and broaden access for faculty, staff, and others who may not have the resources to purchase licenses individually,” Williams wrote to Scott.
Milliken said in a letter last week that the terms of the partnership would benefit UC community members.
“This decision has the full support of all UC Chancellors,” the president wrote.
