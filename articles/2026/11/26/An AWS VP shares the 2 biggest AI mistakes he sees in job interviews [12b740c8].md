# An AWS VP shares the 2 biggest AI mistakes he sees in job interviews

Business Insider · Ana Altchek, Ana Altchek (aaltchek@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-26

Marc Brooker said he sees job seekers make two AI mistakes during interviews. AWS An AWS VP and distinguished engineer said he sees job seekers making two AI mistakes in interviews. The VP, Marc Brooker, said the first mistake is an under-reliance on AI. It's also an issue to over-rely on it, he said, because you offload your thinking. Job seekers want to show they're fluent in AI without suggesting they rely on it too heavily. Communicating that sweet spot can be tricky. Marc Brooker, a vice president and distinguished engineer at Amazon Web Services, said he commonly sees both over- and under-reliance in interviews — and it can be a costly mistake. Brooker said it's increasingly clear when candidates haven't kept pace with how quickly the field has changed over the past year. "It's very obvious when people haven't tried the tools, been building hands-on, been using this new technology, and spending time deeply understanding how it works," Brooker said. He said it can be challenging, even as a leader, to keep up. It can also be difficult to differentiate between a trend and a meaningful update. Identifying that difference requires good taste and a lot of communication with others in the field, Booker said. Personally, he said he dedicates eight hours a week to intentional learning, some of which he does in parallel with building. At least some of this time is set aside for experimenting with new techniques, new tools, papers, and keeping up with the long-term durable trends.

 The other mistake Booker says he sees is over-relying on the tech. That involves a mindset of, "'I'm just going to ask AI to do this for me,'" or, "'I'm going to hand this question to my agent and answer it.'" Those applicants aren't thinking through answers and design questions. "I want to hire people who can think," Brooker said. "I want to hire people who can deeply consider trade-offs. I want to hire people who can communicate and understand and engage with the technology." Brooker said that simply typing in a problem and then asking an LLM or AI agent to solve it, "isn't a particularly useful or valuable skill." However, the VP said that it's "very useful and valuable" to know when to use AI and when to think for yourself. His comments reflect a growing number of tech leaders who have said that judgment and taste are becoming the most important skills in the field, and that AI is increasingly automating work, leaving engineers with more time for higher-level decision-making.
