# Protecting kids from AI chatbot reliance, misuse

The Star · 2026-11-26

KUALA LUMPUR: Like many of her peers, college student Shanetha Simon uses artificial intelligence (AI) for school and life questions. While she acknowledges the benefits of the technology, she has fears about it too.
"My biggest fear as a woman is one day waking up seeing photos of myself being AI-generated and taken in an inappropriate sense,” she told a panel at United Nations Children's Fund (Unicef) Malaysia’s World Children’s Day festival here on Nov 20.
Her worry is not without cause. In January, the Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC) had to restrict access to the AI chatbot Grok after it was repeatedly misused to create sexually explicit and non-consensual images, including of women and minors. Access was restored 12 days later, subject to monitoring.
"We can see how Grok can easily follow the commands by its users, and most of the time these commands are quite inappropriate,” she told Bernama later.
Yet the technology that frightens her is also one that she and her peers find useful, available at any time and, most importantly, unlikely to judge them. This dilemma is at the heart of the struggle parents, schools and lawmakers face as they try to figure out how to protect children, from what others can make AI do, and from leaning too heavily on it themselves.
Children are adopting the new technology faster than adults. The 2026 Common Sense Media Census: Tech and Media Use by Tweens and Teens in the United States found that AI use starts as early as 9, and climbs quickly with age: 36 percent of 9-year-olds use it, and more than half (55 percent) of 11-year-olds. By 13, nearly three-quarters (73 percent) of kids have used these chatbots.
A Unicef analysis of data from 10 countries, released in June, estimated that at least 20 million children had used AI. About one in 10 said they had turned to it for advice on things that worried them.
Sensitive questions
Like many other conservative societies, there are some topics that Malaysian society is reluctant to discuss openly, such as sex. Sex education is taught in schools but only to a certain extent.
In the past, kids would get information from friends, or older siblings and failing that, their mothers. Now, there is AI.
Child rights advocate Nadiah Hanim Abdul Latif, an adviser to Unicef Young Persons Advisory Group, told Bernama she discovered that fact at a recent session with about 20 young people from all over Malaysia.
"Say you had a wet dream, for instance. Who would you turn to? Interestingly, half the people in the room said they would approach an older sibling, if they had one, while the other half said, ‘I’ll just ask AI,’” she said.
Parents, preferably mothers, were the last resort, she added. No one said they would ask their teacher.
Shanetha agreed, saying she and her friends would ask AI questions that they hesitate to ask their parents and teachers.
"Lots of people, I dare say, would turn to AI to answer these questions, especially due to its convenience and the lack of judgment,” she said, adding she has sought reassurance from AI chatbots from time to time.
She said there was comfort in talking to AI chatbots knowing it was not a real person that could judge her for asking the questions.
Is AI my friend?
AI chatbots don’t only get sensitive questions about sex and puberty, but also serve another purpose for many people. Research has found that kids and young adults also turn to AI to alleviate feelings of loneliness, and for comfort, support and companionship. People have reported growing more lonely and feeling more socially isolated with the World Health Organisation reporting loneliness affecting one in six people globally in 2025.
Studies over the years have found the same phenomenon affecting Malaysian youths. National Institute of Health 2025 research on 33,523 school children, ‘Loneliness Among Secondary School Students in Malaysia: The National Prevalence and Associated Factors’ by Mohamad Aznuddin Abd Razak et al, found that the national prevalence rate was 16.2 percent. The previous rate in 2012 was 8.1 percent and 9.3 percent in 2017.
Clinical psychologist Dr Chua Sook Ning, who was also a member of the Unicef panel, told Bernama via Google Meet that the temptation to seek companionship from AI was understandable but ultimately fruitless, and possibly dangerous.
"We know that one of the basic psychological needs is relatedness. It is not just about having a one-way connection, but a mutual one - feeling that we are liked and cared for, while also having affection for the other person. It is about having a reciprocal relationship,” she said.
"The thing about AI is AI is not reciprocating, or not really, right? It’s mimicking, it’s sycophantic, it gives you what you want, but there is no real connection because it’s not sentient and it’s not human,” she added.
The risk, she said, is that a young person with questions about the world and about themselves finds AI seemingly empathetic and helpful, then "confides more and more and more”, without knowing what data, biases and algorithms shape the replies.
The question extends beyond childhood. Dr Chua said people of any age are vulnerable when their need for meaningful human connection is not met in real life.
Researchers at US-based non-profit organisation Common Sense Media and Stanford University’s Brainstorm Lab for Mental Health found that chatbots would engage in harmful conversations with very little prompting. Neither did the bots intervene when the test users showed signs of mental distress or risky behaviour.
AI chatbots have played significant roles in real-life tragedies.
In April this year, a man was arrested for allegedly murdering his Bangladeshi roommate and the roommate's friend at the University of South Florida. According to prosecutors, the suspect had asked ChatGPT for ways to dispose a body in a dumpster before the two victims had disappeared. He had also made other inquiries relating to violence.
And in April 2025, a 20-year old student at Florida State University carried out a mass shooting on campus, killing two university staff and wounding several others. Phoenix Ikner had consulted ChatGPT about what gun and ammunition to use, and what time to perform the attack. Logs showed ChatGPT gave advice on how to do the shooting.
In the same month, 16-year-old Adam Raine from the United States committed suicide after allegedly confiding in the ChatGPT chatbot over seven months.
His parents subsequently filed a lawsuit against OpenAI, the creator of ChatGPT, alleging that the company had failed to intervene or issue a warning when Raine began discussing suicide and uploading images of himself engaging in self-harm.
The law catches up
In Malaysia, since June 1, major social media platforms have been required to verify users’ ages and prevent children under 16 from registering accounts under the Online Safety Act 2025.
Harris Zainul, director of research at the Institute of Strategic and International Studies (ISIS) Malaysia, said the Act’s licensing requirements, as currently drafted, do not extend to AI companies, although the potential harms are similar.
Parliament passed the Cybercrimes Bill in July, with offences covering malicious deepfakes and manipulated intimate images. The law is expected to take effect in early 2027.
A separate AI Governance Bill is expected in the first quarter of 2027. On Tuesday, Digital Minister Gobind Singh Deo told the Dewan Rakyat the government was examining shared liability for AI developers and platforms over harmful content reaching children, and considering mandatory crisis protocols for high-risk AI.
"We are concerned that many who use these applications do not actually know the risks involved,” he was quoted as saying of general-purpose chatbots.
Lawyer Devkumar Kumarendran, a member of the Bar Council’s AI committee, wants parents of children under 16 to be able to monitor their kids’ use of AI services, and require tech companies to give parents the access similar to Meta’s Instagram teen accounts.
"No one is regulating a 12-year-old, a 13-year-old,” he said.
Another is limiting the data that the chatbots would be able to retain and features available on the chatbots, given the age of the user.
Harris, who is also the co-founder of the Centre for Responsible Technology, told Bernama that Malaysia should not rely only on companies’ own safety reports.
"We need to be able to establish our own baselines and measurements (of how safe the technologies are)," he said, and tell companies to improve when they fall short.
Faster action, safer design
Protecting young people online is easier said than done, however. For Shanetha, feeling safe online depends heavily on how responsive platforms and authorities act. Although reporting features exist, she said the time the posts are taken down is too long.
"For example, if there were a post … spreading misinformation about a certain young person and we were to report that, it would take a long time before that post would actually be taken down. And news travels fast, especially when it's in a digital world,” she said.
She said this could have deleterious effects on people, especially girls and women, who face more social repercussions. The defamatory posts may also affect the young people’s future, including their employment prospects.
While she acknowledged some surveillance is needed, she said platforms should also maintain users’ privacy as much as possible.
Experts agree that restrictive and punitive rules should not be the only answer.
Dr Chua said parents should stay calm and listen when a child brings them a problem, or the child may work it out with AI instead.
"We got to make it quite attractive for them to talk to us by being trustworthy, calm, wise adults,” she said.
Nadiah advised parents to ask open questions rather than lecture, and to accept that their children may know more than they do. "You want to be the person that they go to when they’re in trouble,” she said.
All who spoke to Bernama say digital literacy was important, for adults and children alike, albeit with different approaches.
Shanetha would like to see lessons on digital literacy and ways to protect oneself online taught at a very young age. She also wants policymakers to listen to what young people have to say about regulating AI.
"If we are old enough to face the consequences of AI, we are young enough to be present to help write those policies,” she said.- Bernama
