# [Event Report] Education and Generative AI Grand Seminar 2026 in Nagoya | What to Leave to AI, What Humans Should Take On—Learning Assessment and AI Ethics

[Event Report] Education and Generative AI Grand Seminar 2026 in Nagoya | What to Leave to AI, What Humans Should Take On—Learning Assessment and AI Ethics
On September 6, 2026, the "Education and Generative AI Grand Seminar 2026 in Nagoya" was held at TKP Garden City PREMIUM Nagoya Station. The seminar was organized by the teacher community LEG Nagoya and sponsored by LoiLo Inc.
What should we leave to generative AI? And how can we create opportunities for children to think and judge for themselves? In this seminar, we explored changes in work and expression caused by AI, as well as learning assessment and how to interact with AI, through keynote speeches and workshops.
The Nagoya venue featured Noboru Ando, a lecturer at Aoyama Gakuin Junior High School and part-time lecturer at Aoyama Gakuin University who also serves as an instructor for Study Sapuri's "Information I" course, and Tamami Kondo, Representative Director of the Media Education Research Lab. Breakout sessions and workshops led by LEG teachers were also held.
This report introduces the day's content, focusing on the keynote speeches.
1. The Center of Gravity for Thinking Shifts from Creating to Judging—Mr. Noboru Ando
Mr. Ando's lecture theme was "Learning and Work in the Age of Generative AI Agents." While introducing examples of AI utilization, he spoke about changes in classes and school administration, and how to evaluate future learning.
How will education change in an era where AI advances tasks?
In the first half of the lecture, he introduced how far the latest generative AI can go, incorporating live demonstrations. Mr. Ando highlighted "AI agents," which not only create text and images but also receive human instructions to carry out tasks within software.
As a concrete example, a demo was conducted where an AI agent was made to operate LoiLoNote. When given voice instructions to summarize school lunch duty schedules and rotation rules, the AI proceeded to create cards. The work continued while Mr. Ando continued his lecture, and the completed cards were checked midway. It was shown that instead of performing operations one by one, one can convey what they want to do in words and leave the work to the AI.
In addition, examples were introduced such as using AI to turn what was learned in class into a song for reflection, and having AI respond in English to Japanese instructions during university lectures.
While such AI evolution supports teachers' work, it also relates to how we perceive the deliverables submitted by children. In the coming era, can we evaluate a person's understanding and thinking based solely on the finished product? In the second half of the lecture, this question served as the starting point for a discussion on how to change learning assessment.
Deliverables alone do not reveal children's learning
Until now, schools have tried to read what children have understood and thought from deliverables such as reports and slides. Mr. Ando pointed out that the premise of this evaluation is changing because AI can now create deliverables.
However, when AI can create deliverables, good appearance does not necessarily indicate the individual's understanding or thinking. It is difficult to detect the use of AI, and he says we need to consider how to evaluate based on the premise that AI can be used to create deliverables.
Therefore, the idea that the "center of gravity of thinking" shifts was presented. By having AI take on the task of generating ideas and giving them shape, the focus of human effort shifts from "creating ideas" to "examining and judging the ideas presented." Setting goals, conveying necessary backgrounds and conditions to the AI, and comparing multiple ideas and explaining the reasons for the choice become more important than ever.
Mr. Ando emphasized that knowledge is also required for such judgments. Is what the AI produced correct? Does it meet my purpose? How should it be corrected? He explained that knowledge and thinking are required to examine the output and arrange it in one's own way.
"100 ideas → 3 ideas → 1 idea": Making the selection process a learning experience
A concrete example introduced was the inquiry process of "100 ideas → 3 ideas → 1 idea."
1. Expand ideas (100 ideas): Research local issues and organize photos and notes in a shared LoiLoNote notebook. Based on that content, have the AI generate solution ideas and turn them into cards one by one. Distinguish between the AI's ideas and your own thoughts using colors.
2. Compare and narrow down to 3 options: Use thinking tools to compare ease of implementation, regional impact, and other factors. In addition to the reasons for selection, record the reasons for discarding other options, such as cost or time constraints.
3. Select 1 option and refine it: Finally, select one option to improve, and present it along with the changes from the initial draft to the final version and the reasons for those changes.
Professor Ando explained that thinking about why the remaining options were not chosen is also a learning experience. Learn not only from what went well, but also from failures and discarded ideas. This is a way of thinking that treats the AI's ability to generate many candidates as an opportunity to compare, question, and re-select.
What is important is not the number of ideas, but the criteria for selection and the process of revision. Professor Ando shared that in his own university classes, he requires students to submit not only the finished report but also a "log of thinking," such as what kind of Q&A they had with the AI. It is a way of thinking that treats the process leading to the final product as learning itself, including "why it was chosen" and "why it was not chosen."
At the end of the lecture, the phrase "What you leave to AI is tasks. What only humans can do is judgment and dialogue" was presented. Because the number of tasks that can be left to AI is increasing, teachers can spend their time observing children, thinking, and engaging in dialogue. The importance of this was demonstrated throughout the lecture.
2. Cultivating the ability to use AI and the judgment not to delegate everything — Professor Tamami Imado
The next speaker was Professor Tamami Imado, Representative Director of the Media Education Laboratory and Visiting Researcher at the Center for Global Communications (GLOCOM), International University of Japan. The lecture theme was "AI Ethics Education for Children Who Will Lead the Future."
The question posed at the beginning was, "What kind of society do you want to create using information technology?" It is not just about being able to use AI, but about understanding its mechanisms and risks, and deciding what to delegate and where to make decisions for oneself. She explained the skills necessary for children living in an information society.
Learning about AI before "using" it
How to cultivate the ability to understand the mechanisms and risks of AI and make decisions for oneself. These issues are also becoming important themes in the next Course of Study.
In the next Course of Study currently under consideration, discussions are underway to establish an "Information Domain (tentative name)" in elementary school integrated studies and a new "Information and Technology Subject (tentative name)" in junior high schools. The background to this is the response to rapidly changing information technology and the information society, including generative AI.
While introducing these movements, Professor Imado distinguished between learning how to operate AI and learning about AI itself. She compared learning only how to operate AI to driving a car without knowing traffic rules.
What is needed is not just how to ask AI questions and get answers. She explained that it is important to cultivate the ability to judge whether the answers are correct, whether there is bias, and how much should be left to AI, based on understanding what mechanisms generate the answers and what limitations and risks exist.
The four perspectives presented for considering AI ethics were "human rights," "fairness," "transparency," and "accountability." Whether the output violates someone's dignity, whether there is bias in the data or judgments, whether the basis for judgment can be questioned, and who is responsible for the results of its use. It is required not only to use AI as a convenient tool but also to think about its relationship with people and society.
An example of this way of thinking was the movement in New York City public schools. The city suspended the use of generative AI by students up to the 8th grade for one year in the 2026-27 school year.
Professor Imado interpreted this not as a simple "AI ban," but as a period to verify the impact on children and learn about AI first while establishing safe rules. It is a way of thinking that values the order of understanding mechanisms and risks before using it, rather than rushing to have children use AI.
How to accept AI's answers
Professor Imado cautioned against accepting AI's answers as they are. AI can show a tendency toward "sycophancy," where it excessively agrees with the user's opinion. An AI that returns affirmative words can be a comfortable presence, but just because the AI agrees with you does not mean that the idea is correct.
To verify this property, participants asked the AI questions about the educational use of smartphones from both positive and negative premises.
Comparing the answers shows that different premises lead to corresponding responses. It was an exercise that made it clear that not only what you ask, but also how you interpret the returned answers is important.
Mr. Kondo says that for children's learning, it is important to think for themselves before using AI, and to be able to explain the answer in their own words after using it. After researching and thinking for themselves, they can gain different perspectives or review their writing and presentation content. The idea is that AI should be used to expand one's learning, not to replace the act of thinking.
Also, plausible AI answers may contain incorrect information. To notice mistakes, one needs the knowledge to judge the content. It is required not only to be aware of "not taking things at face value," but also to acquire the knowledge to pause and verify.
Regarding home study, the idea of ensuring transparency through "declaration and verification" rather than a blanket ban on AI use was introduced. This means making it clear that AI was used and being able to explain what information was checked and how.
Supporting independence and social participation rather than just restricting
When thinking about how to interact with AI, it is essential not only to restrict its use but also to have a perspective that supports the child's learning. Mr. Kondo positioned AI and digital technology in special needs education as tools to support independence and social participation. For children who have difficulty with reading, writing, or expression, summarizing text, reading aloud, and voice input can be means to help them participate in learning.
According to Mr. Kondo, even in New York City, the use of AI for supporting children with disabilities or for multilingual support is exempt from the suspension.
What is important, he says, is not to decide uniformly whether to use AI or not, but to think about "what is necessary for that child's learning."
Introduced as the foundation for such learning is "Digital Citizenship." Digital Citizenship refers to civic education and human rights education that teaches how to actively engage in and fulfill responsibilities in a digital society.
In the group work, participants thought about a person's interests and feelings by using clues left on the internet, such as search terms and social media reactions. Through the experience of inferring a person's profile from fragmented information, they considered the issues of "profiling" by AI and privacy.
Furthermore, time was set aside for participants themselves to check the privacy and ad settings of the LINE app. This was a workshop to learn what information is being provided to familiar services and to choose for oneself how to interact with technology.
At the end of the lecture, the phrase "Knowledge makes us pause" was presented. It is not just about being able to use AI, but knowing its characteristics and risks, pausing to think, and making one's own judgments. Mr. Kondo conveyed that fostering such abilities is necessary for information education in the AI era.
