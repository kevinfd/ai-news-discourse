Writing a red line down is a start. These steps make it more likely you'll keep it. Do each one, or skip it, and the next one opens.
- In 138 trials, keeping track of a goal helped nearly three times as much when other people could see it. Where this comes from Send it to two people, and ask them where their red lines are.
- In those trials, people who were prompted to check on a goal did better at reaching it. Where this comes from
- In those trials, keeping track helped more than twice as much when people reported to someone else. Where this comes from A witness: someone who'll ask you about it if one of your lines is crossed. - In those trials, keeping track helped about one and a half times as much when people kept a written record. Where this comes from Add why you care, and the excuse you'd be tempted to accept (not shared; your copy and your witness email include it) See what you'll save
- The same review notes that people rarely check on a goal unless something prompts them. A date in your calendar is that prompt. Where this comes from The reminder doesn't include your red lines, so keep your saved copy.
Where the evidence comes from
From a 2016 review of 138 trials with 19,951 people (Harkin and others, Psychological Bulletin). In each trial, some people were prompted to keep track of a goal and others weren't. On average, the people who were prompted did better at reaching it.
- Keeping track helped more when other people could see it (0.55) or when it was reported to someone (0.47) than when it stayed private (0.19).
- It helped more when people kept a written record (0.43) than when they didn't (0.29).
The numbers are effect sizes: bigger means it helped more.
Nearly all the trials were about health goals, like weight or blood pressure. Nobody has tested red lines for AI, so read this as a guide, not a promise. Writing a line down doesn't mean you'll keep it. These steps make it more likely.

The one who left
Joseph Rotblat was a Polish physicist working in Liverpool when the war began. In 1944 he went to Los Alamos with the British team on the Manhattan Project. He had one reason for being there: the fear that Nazi Germany would build an atomic bomb first.
By the end of 1944 it was clear Germany would not. The reason he had come no longer held, so he resigned and went back to Liverpool. He is the only scientist known to have left the project on grounds of conscience.
Most of us never write down why we're somewhere. When things change slowly, nothing tells us to stop. Rotblat had that moment because he knew his reason. This page started as The Rotblat Line, to help you write down yours: the specific things that would make you act, and what you'd do, whether that's quitting, speaking up or finally getting involved. He was born on 4 November, so that's the day it suggests re-reading your red lines each year.
