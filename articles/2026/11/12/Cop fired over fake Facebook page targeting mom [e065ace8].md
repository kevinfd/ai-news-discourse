# Cop fired over fake Facebook page targeting mom

Fox News · Kurt Knutsson, CyberGuy Report · 2026-11-12

You come home with your kids after an afternoon at a trampoline park. A sheriff's deputy is waiting outside. He's investigating your adult son and later demands that everyone leave your house. You ask whether he has a search warrant. Minutes later, you're being arrested while your terrified children watch. That's how Georgia mom Natasha Lopez describes the police encounter that changed her family's life. But what happened afterward took an even more disturbing turn.
In my conversation with Natasha on The CyberGuy Report podcast, she described how a Flock license plate camera system allegedly helped a deputy track someone to her home. After Natasha publicly challenged her arrest, a fake Facebook page began attacking her family. The page allegedly shared sensitive records involving her children and AI-generated images designed to humiliate her.
The deputy who arrested her has since been fired. In a statement provided to CyberGuy, the Cherokee County Sheriff's Office confirmed that its investigation found him responsible for the Facebook page's content. However, the department strongly disputes Natasha's account of the original arrest and how authorities handled her complaints. Now, with a protective order in place and her criminal case still unresolved, Natasha says her family continues to live with the consequences.

How Flock cameras allegedly led police to Natasha's home
The trouble began on August 30, 2025, when Cherokee County Sheriff's Corporal Charles Mills was investigating Natasha's adult son. Authorities suspected him of fleeing police on a motorcycle during an earlier encounter. According to Natasha, Mills used Flock automated license plate readers to follow the movements of her son's girlfriend as she traveled to Natasha's house. "He was investigating my adult son, who he alleged was speeding on a motorcycle the night before," Natasha told me.
Flock cameras capture passing vehicles and record license plates along with their location and time. Police can search those records to identify where a vehicle appeared. Multiple camera sightings can reveal someone's movements across an area. Natasha believes those camera searches led Mills to her home. When Natasha returned from the trampoline park with her younger children, deputies were already at her home. Natasha questions how Mills connected her son to the motorcycle involved in the alleged incident. She says Mills relied on a Flock camera image captured approximately six hours before the reported encounter and at a different location. She believes that evidence did not establish that her son was the rider.
She asked for a warrant and ended up arrested
Mills had an arrest warrant for Natasha's adult son. However, authorities had not yet obtained a search warrant for Natasha's home. After taking her son into custody, the deputy demanded that everyone leave the house. Natasha wanted to know why he needed to enter. "Do you have a warrant?" she recalled asking. She says she repeatedly requested documentation authorizing a search. Instead, Mills walked past her and entered the house. "And I remember saying, like, my kids and my dogs are in there, like, what are you doing?" Natasha told me.
She followed him inside because her children were there. At the same time, she called 911 because she believed the deputy had entered without proper authorization. Her home surveillance cameras captured the confrontation. Natasha says she never expected an argument over a warrant to end with her being placed in handcuffs. "I felt very helpless, very scared, very scared for my children because they were taking the only parent that they had," she said.
What the sheriff's office says about the arrest
The Cherokee County Sheriff's Office disputes Natasha's characterization of the encounter. In a statement provided to CyberGuy, the department said Mills believed evidence connected to the felony traffic investigation was inside the home. According to the agency, he attempted to conduct a protective sweep to clear individuals from the residence and preserve potential evidence before obtaining a search warrant.
The department maintains that Mills believed his actions were permitted under established Fourth Amendment case law. It also says Natasha interfered with his efforts despite repeated lawful orders. The sheriff's office emphasized that Mills was wearing his full uniform, operating a marked patrol vehicle and had identified himself as a law enforcement officer. After securing the residence, Mills obtained a search warrant from a judge. Natasha was transported to the Cherokee County Adult Detention Center, processed and later released on bond. Authorities charged her with obstruction of a law enforcement officer under Georgia law.
The sheriff's office also told CyberGuy that prosecutors independently reviewed body-camera footage and determined there was probable cause to pursue the case. An additional obstruction charge was subsequently filed. Natasha maintains that she was exercising her rights and trying to protect her children. Her attorneys have challenged the legal basis for the deputy's actions. The court would later address those arguments, but the encounter had already set off another chain of events.
Why Natasha took her story to social media
After leaving jail, Natasha began asking questions about what happened. She told me she had asked supervising deputies to review her home security footage before taking her into custody. She believed the recording would show that the arresting deputy had misrepresented the encounter. "I told them the arresting officer wasn't telling the truth, and they would not," she said, referring to her requests that officials review the footage.
After her release, Natasha says she contacted the sheriff's office and prosecutors. She hoped someone would investigate her complaint. Instead, she felt that authorities had dismissed her concerns. She eventually began sharing her experience publicly through a social media page called Natasha Noble. She wanted people in her community to see what happened and decide for themselves.
The sheriff's office offered a different perspective on the social media activity that followed. In its response to CyberGuy, the department described Natasha's posts as an online campaign targeting Mills, other deputies, elected officials, prosecutors and their families. It also said employees and their families had received hateful messages and death threats from individuals across the country.
The department reported that its phone lines had been inundated with disturbing calls and said the attention surrounding the case had adversely affected employees and their families. Its statement did not establish that Natasha personally made those threats. Meanwhile, Natasha's public criticism of her arrest was followed by another problem.
A fake Facebook page starts attacking Natasha's family
Natasha noticed an anonymous Facebook page while checking reactions to one of her posts. The account was called Not So Noble, a play on the name of her own page. It used her branding and published material criticizing her. At first, she did not know who was behind it. Then the posts became increasingly personal. "It was a like, harassment page that somebody had made, that they were attacking me and my children on," she told me.
Natasha says the page accused her children of criminal behavior and published personal information about them. Some posts allegedly included unredacted school and incident reports involving minors. That frightened her because she could not understand how the person behind the page was obtaining those records.
She also noticed people connected to local law enforcement interacting with the content. However, she had no proof at that point of who controlled the page. As the posts continued, her concern shifted toward her children's safety. "I felt desperate, like, how, how how do I protect my children, you know?" she said. Natasha told me she filed a complaint with the sheriff's department because she feared for her family's safety. She says she wanted investigators to identify the person behind the page but felt authorities were unwilling to help.

What the sheriff's office says about Natasha's complaint
The Cherokee County Sheriff's Office disputes Natasha's description of how investigators handled her report. In its response to CyberGuy, the department said Natasha filed a complaint concerning Not So Noble in June 2026. According to the agency, she initially suggested that a woman known to her might have created the page and provided screenshots for investigators to review.
The department said detectives did not believe the screenshots established criminal conduct at that time. However, investigators identified reported telephone calls that could potentially violate Georgia's harassing telephone call statute. According to the sheriff's office, detectives asked Natasha to come in for a formal interview and possible additional evidence collection. The department says she did not communicate with investigators for approximately one week and later advised that she was unavailable to meet with the assigned detective.
The agency maintains that the investigation could not move forward without her participation. Natasha disputes how authorities handled her requests for assistance. During our podcast conversation, she described feeling abandoned while the online targeting escalated.
Then came the AI-generated intimate images
The online targeting eventually took an especially disturbing turn. According to Natasha, the Facebook page began posting personal photographs of her and digitally manipulated images designed to make her appear connected to an OnlyFans account. Some photographs were years old. Natasha says she still does not know how the original images were obtained. "I don't know, we still don't know," she told me when I asked where the photographs came from.
The images allegedly included AI-generated material created using her likeness. Natasha says the posts also directed people to folders on Google Drive, where copies of the images and other material could be viewed. She repeatedly reported the content to Facebook and Google. Although some images were removed, she says additional versions continued appearing online. What upset her most was the apparent effort to expose her children to the material. "They wanted, like, my children to see it," Natasha said. For Natasha, the situation became especially frightening because she still did not know who was responsible.
We reached out to Meta, Facebook's parent company, for comment on the page and Natasha's reports of harassment. A Meta representative told CyberGuy that the account appears to have already been removed.
The deputy who arrested her was behind the Facebook page
The biggest revelation came during a September 18, 2026 court hearing. Natasha says her attorney questioned Mills about records he had obtained involving her. Then the attorney asked whether he had created fake social media accounts. Mills acknowledged using fake Facebook profiles to monitor Natasha's posts after she blocked him.
That admission did not immediately establish that he operated Not So Noble. However, prosecutors subsequently disclosed that Mills was the page's creator and owner. When I asked Natasha how she reacted to learning that the arresting officer was connected to the page, she struggled to describe the shock. "Disgust. Anger. Sadness. Fear," she said. "I couldn't believe it." Natasha says she suspected someone connected to law enforcement might be involved because of the records appearing online. But she had never expected the person responsible to be the deputy who arrested her.
Mills and his wife fired as investigation continues
The Cherokee County Sheriff's Office told CyberGuy that prosecutors formally notified the department on September 21, 2026, that Mills was potentially involved in the Not So Noble Facebook page. The following day, September 22, Mills was placed on administrative leave while the department opened an internal investigation. On October 2, the department terminated his employment. The sheriff's office confirmed its investigation determined that Mills was responsible for the page's content and that his actions violated department policy.
In its statement to CyberGuy, the department emphasized, "The personal actions of Charles Aaron Mills do not reflect the values and ethical standards of the Cherokee Sheriff's Office." Meanwhile, Cherokee Judicial Circuit District Attorney Erle Newton confirmed to CyberGuy that his office was assigned to investigate Mills' actions alongside the Georgia Bureau of Investigation. Newton said that the investigation remains ongoing and that no criminal charges had been filed at the time of his response.
CyberGuy has also confirmed that Mills' wife was fired from her position at the Blue Ridge Judicial Circuit District Attorney's Office on September 30, 2026. The office said her termination was based on policy violations but declined to provide further details, citing its recusal from investigations involving social media content about Natasha Lopez. It remains unclear whether her firing was connected to the allegations involving her husband.
Despite those developments, important questions remain. The department's findings establish Corporal Mills' responsibility for the Facebook page's content, but they do not explain how sensitive records involving Natasha's children were obtained or who generated each allegedly manipulated image. Natasha also questioned whether other local government employees knew about the page. It's still unclear whether anyone else knew about it or helped Corporal Mills.
We reached out to Mills' attorney for comment on the allegations and the sheriff's office's findings, but did not hear back before our deadline.
Natasha wins a protective order, but her fight continues
The deputy's termination did not bring Natasha the closure she wanted. She sought legal protection from Mills after identifying him as the person behind the Facebook page. On October 20, 2026, a Cherokee County Superior Court granted her a 12-month stalking protective order against the former deputy.
Natasha told me the process had been extremely expensive. She estimated that one day of legal proceedings connected to obtaining protection cost her about $7,000. That financial burden added another source of frustration to an experience that had already taken an enormous emotional toll. During our conversation, she repeatedly questioned when the ordeal would finally end. "At what point do they stop, you know?" she asked.
Judge upholds the legal basis for the home search
Meanwhile, Natasha's original obstruction case remains unresolved. In an October 2026 ruling, a Cherokee County judge declined to dismiss the charges stemming from her August 2025 arrest. According to the sheriff's office, the court determined that the protective sweep and subsequent search of Natasha's residence were lawful and consistent with the Fourth Amendment.
The judge found that Mills had probable cause to enter the home and that it was reasonable for him to believe urgent circumstances justified securing the property before obtaining a search warrant. The ruling allows the obstruction prosecution to continue. Natasha maintains that her arrest was unjustified. Her attorneys have challenged the deputy's actions throughout the proceedings.
What happened to Natasha's children?
The original investigation also led to the arrest of Natasha's adult son, Joseph Christopher Helms. Natasha says Mills arrested him outside her home shortly before taking her into custody. She maintains that her son was wrongly identified as the motorcyclist suspected of fleeing police and says he continues to deny the allegation.
Cherokee County court records show he was indicted on November 10, 2025, under case number 25CR1207. District Attorney Erle Newton told CyberGuy that the Blue Ridge Judicial Circuit District Attorney's Office recused itself from Helms' case. Newton said the Prosecuting Attorneys' Council informed him that the case had been assigned to the Paulding Judicial Circuit District Attorney's Office.
Paulding Judicial Circuit District Attorney Robert S. Lane subsequently confirmed to CyberGuy that his office has been appointed to handle the case. Helms faces two counts of fleeing or attempting to elude a police officer. Lane said the case remains pending and no trial or hearing had been scheduled at the time of his response. Helms has not been convicted of those charges.
But what stayed with me most from our conversation was what Natasha shared about her youngest son. He was 9 when deputies entered their home and arrested his mother. She says he has needed therapy since the encounter and still fears police will take her away again. "You know, we go somewhere and he'll ask me like, mom, are they going to take you?" she told me.
Natasha says her son was hyperventilating after officers took her away. "Nobody even asked him if he was okay," she recalled. Meanwhile, she says some of the online posts targeted her teenage son and damaged his personal relationships. For this family, the consequences extend far beyond the courtroom.
Why Natasha wants Flock cameras removed
The Flock camera system remains central to Natasha's concerns about the case. She believes access to vehicle location records helped Mills follow her son's girlfriend, who Natasha says was not suspected of a crime. She also questions whether existing department policies provide enough oversight to prevent officers from abusing the system.
When I asked Natasha how the experience changed her opinion of Flock cameras, she told me, "We want them gone because policies aren't enough." She believes state lawmakers need to establish stronger restrictions on how police use license plate reader data.
Flock cameras can provide valuable leads in criminal investigations. However, officers may have access to large amounts of vehicle movement information. Flock Safety says its systems include search logs and auditing tools to help departments monitor access. Still, those safeguards cannot prevent every potential misuse.
The Cherokee County Sheriff's Office did not specifically address Mills' use of Flock cameras or whether investigators identified any violations involving the technology. We previously covered a Flock camera hit that helped send the wrong woman to jail. Prosecutors eventually dropped the charges in that case. Both cases raise questions about how police use license plate surveillance and who holds them accountable.
Natasha says she would speak up all over again
Toward the end of our conversation, I asked Natasha a question that had been on my mind. Knowing everything she has endured, would she have stayed quiet if she could go back and do it again? "No, it wouldn't be me," she said. Natasha told me she has never been someone who stays silent when she believes something wrong has happened. "I believe in accountability. Right is right and wrong is wrong," she said.
When I asked what justice would look like for her, she had a specific answer. "Accountability. I want Charles Mills to be charged. I want him to lose his post certification so he can not be law enforcement officer and harm somebody else like this again." Post certification refers to the professional credentials required for law enforcement officers in Georgia. Losing that certification could prevent Mills from serving as an officer in the state.
Natasha also wants investigators to examine whether anyone else played a role in the harassment or the release of personal records. Whether those demands lead to additional consequences remains to be seen. But Natasha says she has no intention of stopping her efforts.
What you can do if someone targets you online
Natasha's experience also highlights how difficult online harassment can become when someone uses fake accounts and manipulated images. If something similar happens to you, preserving the evidence early can make a difference.
- Save evidence before reporting posts: Take screenshots showing the account name and content. Keep copies of the URLs and record when the material appeared.
- Report impersonation accounts: Facebook and other platforms provide ways to report fake profiles, impersonation and harassment.
- Request removal of intimate images: Google allows people to report nonconsensual explicit imagery, including AI-generated images using their likeness. In Google Images, open the image, select the three-dot menu, choose Remove result and select It shows a sexual image of me. You can also use Google's image removal instructions.
- Review your family's privacy settings: Limit public access to photographs, locations and personal details, particularly those involving children.
- Seek legal guidance when harassment escalates: If someone publishes private records or threatens your family, an attorney can help explain your legal options.
Keep in mind that removing something from Google Search does not necessarily delete the original material from the website hosting it. For additional guidance, see our article on protecting your family from deepfakes and online abuse.
