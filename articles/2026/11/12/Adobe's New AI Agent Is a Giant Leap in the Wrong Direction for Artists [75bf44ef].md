# Adobe's New AI Agent Is a Giant Leap in the Wrong Direction for Artists

PCMag · Jim Fisher · 2026-11-12

Adobe went big with this year's Max conference, which serves as a showcase for its creative software and a launchpad for new features—it even got Brooklyn Nine-Nine star Terry Crews to emcee. The event, which Adobe calls "The Creativity Conference," obviously garners interest from photographers, graphic designers, videographers, and other creative professionals, but I'm more certain than ever that the artists themselves are no longer the target audience. Instead, Adobe seems intent on pitching lucrative AI tools to corporations.
The headline announcements at Max all focused on Adobe's Creative Agent, which can perform tasks within Creative Cloud programs on your behalf. Adobe essentially updated the AI tool to work with more of its creative apps, along with third-party software that supports the Model Context Protocol (MCP), including AI chatbots like ChatGPT and collaboration tools like Slack. If you think all of that sounds more relevant and useful to businesses than to creators, I agree. I'm extremely wary that Adobe's relentless adoption of AI will increasingly push artists out of the creative process.
Creative Agent Risks the McDonaldization of Art
At Max, Adobe tossed out a lot of potential use cases for Creative Agent, including performing tedious tasks like resizing images and organizing files. That's all well and good, but one new capability jumped out at me: Creative Agent in Firefly Boards. Firefly's AI Assistant, which is powered by Creative Agent, can create mood boards, something that creators use to collaborate on new ideas and set a tone for a project or campaign. That sure sounds like an attempt to replace the creative spark that professional artists bring to the table with computer intelligence.
To be fair, Adobe says Firefly Boards are "guided by human taste and direction," but I'd take that claim with a grain of salt. I feel that Firefly Boards are an unwelcome step beyond the tedious grunt work that AI has traditionally been pitched as handling. Like other AI solutions, Boards operate on prompts, remixing content from Firefly's database into something new. The problem? Over time, AI-driven creative concepts will tend to coalesce. You can call this the McDonaldization of art: It's (relatively) cheap and scratches an itch, but it's not as satisfying or unique as something handcrafted.
Adobe clearly doesn't plan to stop at idea boards, either. Firefly is poised to do a lot that's outside the bounds of busy work. Firefly Video will assemble raw footage into an edited timeline, complete with b-roll cutaways. Generative AI platforms can already create realistic video clips from a prompt, and now Adobe can give them the look of a polished edit.
The company also previewed "Always-on" agents at Max, which it says can do basic work like organizing files and reformatting content. It won't take much to put a workflow together in which Creative Agent takes AI-generated imagery, remixes it, and publishes it all by itself. This type of workflow would remove the human element from the equation, essentially tasking AI with creating content in a closed loop.
I'm sure that Adobe (and its shareholders) have fewer reservations. Creative Cloud is already a cash cow, but Adobe has found a way to squeeze more money out of its customers with Firefly. Like other AI tools, Firefly requires you to use credits to perform tasks. Subscribers get an allotment but have to pay more if they exceed their monthly limit.
The more credit-based AI tools Adobe adds, the higher the potential income. On the client side, it won't take long for corporate bean counters to recognize that they can trim headcounts in design departments and replace workers with Firefly credits. Will the work be as good? Probably not. A Big Mac sitting under a heat lamp is never up to snuff with a diner burger fresh off the grill. Will most people care? History says no; just ask the billions who've been served.
Adobe Hasn't Abandoned Artists Completely (Yet)
I'll be the first to say it—I'm not a fan of AI for creative work. Making art is fundamental to the human experience. Our ancestors painted their stories on cave walls. Prior to the printing press, hand-illuminated manuscripts preserved knowledge and stories. Renaissance artists helped redefine what art could be. Modern artists like Duchamp and Pollock broke new ground in the art world, making creative leaps that propelled the field forward. I doubt that an AI engine could be as revolutionary—generative models are better at remixing existing concepts.
In the digital era, Adobe is the preferred tool for creative types working in photography, graphic design, videography, and similar disciplines. So, I'm always going to be disappointed when the company introduces tools that remove the human touch.
The good news is that Adobe hasn't fully abandoned its core audience, at least not yet. At Max, it announced several new features for Photoshop, Lightroom, Premiere, and other Creative Cloud apps not built around generative content creation. For instance, photographers can now add a glow effect to photos in Lightroom and finally see the autofocus points their cameras used for an image. New Blemish Removal and Open Eyes tools can help clean up portraits, and camera tethering now extends to iPads. Adobe Illustrator and InDesign also get some new improvements.
Fed Up With Adobe? You Have Options
Creators can still make art with Adobe's apps, either as a hobby or a vocation, but I suspect the company will continue to see the AI dollar signs and increasingly shift its development efforts toward tools that appeal more to corporate managers than to digital artists.
If you feel we're already at that point, you have options. I just took a look at LightCraft and PhotoCraft, for example, two free apps that were ironically made with AI, but that aren't packed to the gills with AI tools. You can also take a look at our top picks for photo editing software. A few are available at a one-time price, and others aren't nearly as aggressive in their AI capabilities. All of this is to say that even if Adobe doesn't back off on AI, you can still do things the old-fashioned way—you know, like we did way back in the 2010s.
