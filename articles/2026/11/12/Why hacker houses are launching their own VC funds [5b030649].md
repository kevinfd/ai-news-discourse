# Why hacker houses are launching their own VC funds

Business Insider · Ben Bergman, Ben Bergman (bbergman@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-12

Surabhi Todi (left) and Lisa Shmulyan came out of stealth with Mission Street Capital. Hai Bui Hacker houses are raising their own capital to back early founders before traditional VCs find them. Former Mission Control roommates Surabhi Todi and Lisa Shmulyan are the latest to announce a fund. They recently emerged from stealth to announce Mission Street Capital, which raised under $2 million. San Francisco hacker houses have produced a long list of multibillion-dollar startups, from Perplexity to OpenRouter. Most of the riches have usually flowed to outside investors, but now more houses want to change that, raising their own capital to back very early founders before traditional VCs find them. Investing early has become imperative in the AI boom, as the hottest companies can reach staggering valuations just months after launch. Former Mission Control roommates Surabhi Todi and Lisa Shmulyan are the latest to announce a fund. They recently came out of stealth mode to announce Mission Street Capital. It raised less than $2 million for its "Fund 0," not a large pool of capital by today's venture standards, but enough to make very early bets and hopefully demonstrate a track record to raise larger vehicles. The fund's limited partners include researchers from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta, as well as investors from Andreessen Horowitz, CRV, and Emergence. The anchor investor is Fly.io, a cloud computing startup. Mission Street is part of a small but growing group of hacker house communities trying to become investors, not just temporary crash pads for founders. AGI House, a competing hacker house in Hillsborough that convenes AI founders and researchers for hackathons and other events, launched AGI House Ventures to invest in startups from its network. HF0, short for Hacker Fellowship Zero, began running its San Francisco live-in founder program in 2021, providing housing and capital for small groups of technical and repeat founders. Mission Control, founded by a group of Thiel fellows in 2013, bills itself as the city's oldest hacker house. Alums include the cofounders of Hightouch, an AI marketing company now valued at $2.75 billion. Marty Kausus, cofounder and CEO of a16z and General Catalyst-backed b2b support platform Pylon, also lived there, along with ScaleAI co-founder and Passes CEO Lucy Guo. Todi said the original insight for Mission Street Capital emerged during a late-night discussion outside one of the house's bathrooms, talking about how founders who had lived there had started building companies now worth billions of dollars. "We could have invested in that startup at a pre-seed valuation," Todi said in an interview with Business Insider. Todi said Mission Street scouts can spend up to 100 hours getting to know a prospective founder day and night, washing dishes with them or doing laundry. That compares to a few hours for a traditional VC over coffee when a founder will be on their best behavior. Todi pointed to a resident who replaced the house's couch cushions without being asked as someone with the traits of a successful founder. "If they're a leader in the house, we're going to invest in their company," she said. The firm's early portfolio of a dozen startups includes Smallest, a voice AI company; Aside, an AI browser that raised a $6 million seed round led by Y Combinator; and HydraDB, which is building knowledge graph infrastructure for AI agents. Founders do not have to live in Mission Control, as Todi says she wants to focus on the broader hacker house community. In fact, she moved out of the house after launching the fund.
