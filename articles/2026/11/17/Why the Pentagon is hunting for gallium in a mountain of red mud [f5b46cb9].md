# Why the Pentagon is hunting for gallium in a mountain of red mud

Scientific American · Sarah Scoles · 2026-11-17

About a decade ago geologist Amitava Roy received a sample of “red mud.” Red mud looks like clay with Instagram’s Lux feature cranked up, and it’s the residue left by the most common process for making alumina, the stuff that becomes aluminum. The sample had come from a production plant in Burnside, La., not far from Baton Rouge, where Roy works at Louisiana State University (LSU).
Roy was curious about what red mud was made of and how its elements were arranged, and he had the means to find out. LSU is home to one of the country’s few synchrotrons: a particle accelerator whose x-rays let scientists probe the makeup of materials. Roy could shine those rays at the red mud and discover both its contents and what chemical forms they took.
When he did, he found a lot of loosely bound gallium, a soft and silvery metal. He filed that away as part of his overall analysis of the substance. It was a scientific note but one nobody else paid much attention to—until recently. “Then I realized people are interested,” he says.
On supporting science journalism
Those people are, these days, from the government. Gallium is essential to the war machine; gallium-based semiconductors go into everything from radar and satellite solar panels to electronic-warfare systems and missile seekers. And the military’s appetite for it keeps growing now that the country is burning through munitions and the metals inside them thanks to, you know, war.
There’s just one problem: “It’s almost exclusively produced in China,” Roy says.
China accounts for nearly 99 percent of the world’s gallium. The U.S. hasn’t made any of its own since the 1980s and has no stockpile to fall back on—a vulnerability that Washington has only lately begun taking seriously.
In 2023 Beijing put export controls on certain minerals, including gallium. Anyone sending them abroad needed a special license and had to disclose whom they were selling to. At first most exporters got those licenses without much trouble. In December 2024 China banned the sale of gallium, germanium and antimony to the U.S. outright—then, in the fall of 2025, suspended that decision as part of a trade truce that runs out in January. But even with the ban on hold, according to a report from the Center for Strategic and International Studies, “there is growing evidence that Chinese exports of gallium to the United States have not resumed in a meaningful way.”
So the U.S. is trying, belatedly, to get its own supply. Gallium is now the subject of federal contracts worth hundreds of millions of dollars, part of a larger push by the Department of Defense to secure “critical minerals” at home—one that has spurred a more general mining revival.
Unlike some critical minerals, though, gallium doesn’t require spinning up a new mine or resurrecting an old one. It’s already lying around practically in Roy’s backyard. China has been pulling gallium out of its alumina refineries for years.
The U.S. is trying to do the same, and it could just work: the country doesn’t need much. It uses around 22 tons of gallium each year, and by one estimate, capturing just 1 percent of the gallium that’s being trashed at alumina and zinc refineries would cover that.
One of the best places to get it is Gramercy, La., where Atlantic Alumina, the only operating alumina refinery in the U.S., has red mud piles weighing more than 30 million tons. The Pentagon has awarded a company called ElementUSA $30 million for a demonstration plant meant to show it can strip the mud of gallium and of scandium, which strengthens the aluminum used in military aircraft and armor. The company also plans to build an $850-million extraction facility on-site. (Neither the DOD nor ElementUSA responded to requests for comment.)
And the refinery itself, backed by $400 million from the DOD, is coming at the problem from the other end by catching gallium before it ever reaches the pile. In August the refinery said it expected to produce its first gallium by the end of the year and, eventually, 50 tons a year—more than twice the amount the whole country uses.
Roy, who isn’t working with ElementUSA or the refinery, is also trying to use his scientific finding to interest the government. He recently submitted a proposal to the Department of Energy and is waiting to hear back. The department has already selected five other projects to recover gallium, including one with the alumina manufacturer in Gramercy.
Anyone trying to separate critical minerals from industrial waste needs detailed science like the kind Roy has done. “If you want to extract something, you need to know where it is and how it is there,” he says. The how is particularly important; extraction methods differ depending on the form an element or compound takes—whether it’s locked in a complicated chemical structure or easily separated out.
Waste-stream extraction is ElementUSA’s specialty. The company calls the approach “minerals without mines.” According to statements by Colorado School of Mines professor Elizabeth Holley, who is working with the company to extract rare earth elements from the same red mud, the work pays off twice: it squeezes more value out of things that were already mined, and it helps to reduce the caustic sludge left behind.
And there are plenty of other forgotten places where the same work could happen for gallium and other critical minerals. ElementUSA has already pinpointed more bauxite sites in Texas and elsewhere in Louisiana. “I was talking to somebody, and he goes birding in North Baton Rouge,” Roy says. One day this friend came across red mud from a long-closed facility. “All these piles are there,” he says.
They were just waiting for someone to want them for war.
