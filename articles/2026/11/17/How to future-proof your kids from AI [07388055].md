# How to future-proof your kids from AI

Business Insider · Dan Slater, Dan Slater (insider@insider.com) · 2026-11-17

Jon Stanley Austin for BI Books Insider is a regular feature from Dan Slater about the latest and best books in business and self improvement. Each month, through essays and interviews, Dan will introduce you to today's most fascinating authors. About 2,400 years ago, when the Spartan King Agesilaus was asked what boys should learn in order to prepare for their future, he is said to have replied, "that which they will use when they become men." Back then, education for boys amounted to a single-minded focus on soldiering and war to ensure Sparta remained the dominant power in Greece. But Agesilaus's advice was flexible, writes Daniel Susskind, which is why it has echoed down through the ages. For the last few centuries, the future has meant paid work, so the purpose of education has largely been to prepare kids for employment . Then came AI. Losing literacy, numeracy, and critical thinking would be "a disaster, leaving people unprepared for what lies ahead," Susskind writes. Jon Stanley Austin for BI An economist at King's College London and a senior research associate at Oxford's Institute for Ethics in AI, Susskind speaks to audiences all over the world about how AI is changing work. The most common question the father of three gets is: What should my children do? In his new book, he offers a thorough and layered response, mixing sober realism, pragmatic optimism, and disarming humility. His answer, in short: Stop trying to guess which jobs will survive and start building skills and character that will survive anything. In an AI debate that is awash with doomers and boosters , with books that are outdated before they are published, "What Should My Children Do: A Human's Guide to the Age of AI" should echo down for some time.

 Susskind's guide to the future opens with an admission that no one, including himself, can predict it. It's always been this way, he writes. At the dawn of the internet era, who could have guessed that "search engine optimizer" or "social media manager" would eventually be jobs? Thus most strategies for hedging against the future turn out to be fools' errands. Coding, long seen as the safe bet , is no longer a certainty. Given the world-historic uncertainty we now face with AI, attempts to future-proof our kids are particularly futile. Rather than try to predict what AI won't be able to do, Susskind advises parents to focus on helping our kids develop three basic skills, even if AI does them better: literacy, numeracy ( basic fluency with numbers and data ), and critical thinking. If smartphones and social media are addictive and distracting us from learning basic skills, the sheer power of AI removes the need to learn basic skills at all — and this is what Susskind says he worries about most. Why bother learning math if AI can solve every problem ? Why bother reading a book (or even an article about a book) if AI can answer any question about it? Because, Susskind writes, unlike losing memory, which we've been losing since the advent of writing, losing literacy, numeracy, and critical thinking would be "a disaster, leaving people unprepared for what lies ahead." Students should learn to use AI so they can "stand on the shoulders" of the technology and do tasks that would otherwise be "unthinkable," but they should also learn to "flourish without the technology" Susskind says. Jon Stanley Austin for BI Susskind's recommended education strategy runs on two tracks: AI and non-AI. Students should learn to use AI so they can "stand on the shoulders" of the technology and do tasks that would otherwise be "unthinkable," but they should also learn to "flourish without the technology," so basic skills don't die from disuse. Susskind argues that one third of a student's time at college and university should go to learning about AI and how to use it. "In making this change, there is a huge scope for creativity," he writes. "In English classes , for instance, you could simply ask students to make sense of a basic poem without AI and then a more demanding one with AI. Or you could go further. In the classes with AI you might also prompt it to provide a set of intentionally misleading readings of a poem and ask students to critique each of them." Susskind argues that AI can now offer everyone what Oxford's tutorial system once offered a privileged few — but better. The latest AI systems, he writes, are more effective at explaining ideas, questioning students, and guiding them through problems "than so many of the teachers I have watched in action over the years (and yes, I include myself in that group)." While Susskind readily admits AI is warping and ingesting white-collar work far faster than blue-collar work, he isn't advising you to tell your kids to become plumbers . In fact, his counterintuitive advice for early-career white-collar audiences is to stay the course. The future will likely see more "domain experts," experienced humans who know what problems need solving, paired with AI that excels at solving those problems. There are caveats. "It is crucial to move into a field because the underlying problems grip you, not the traditional job," he writes. Don't choose law for the image, the lifestyle, or even the work itself. Become a lawyer because you want to help people navigate the legal system . The problem lasts while the "job" changes in unpredictable ways. As AI progresses, Susskind suggests that education move away from building workplace competence and toward cultivating character. Jon Stanley Austin for BI And white-collar workers must prepare for a reshuffling of status. In the AI age, the roles that seem to be most at risk include some of the most historically high-valued ones, roles in which humans are merely "a means to achieve some end rather than an end in themselves." As Susskind puts it, we don't necessarily want a lawyer, an accountant , or a marketing expert. We want to be well-represented in legal disputes. We want to get our taxes done. We want to sell our products. Susskind's counsel is to look for work where people truly want a human involved, whether for aesthetic, empathetic, or moral reasons: artists and writers, athletes and musicians, nurses and carers, judges and politicians. As AI progresses, possibly toward the point where it could outperform humans at every economically useful task , Susskind suggests that education move away from building workplace competence and toward cultivating personal character. That would mean moving moral questions into classrooms "in a structured way": What is the right way to act? Which laws and institutions are just? What do fairness and community really mean? And which problems are worth spending all that time solving in the first place? My 11-year-old son is a math enthusiast . He works with tutors and participates in a competitive math team. He read "Big Math: The Hidden Codes That Run Our World," a new book by two mathematicians who wrestle with the implications of an awesome and terrifying future — one that arrived before their book even came out. In early October, OpenAI released 722 AI-generated math papers claiming solutions or progress on hundreds of open problems, some of them among the field's most famous and befuddling. Mathematicians are still checking the work, but the shock was immediate. In the wake of this existential moment, Steven Strogatz, a co-author of "Big Math," shed tears during an interview with The New York Times. He and his students wanted to know what the point of it all was, if they could no longer discover anything on their own. The latest AI systems are effective at guiding students through problems "than so many of the teachers I have watched in action over the years (and yes, I include myself in that group)," Susskind writes. Jon Stanley Austin for BI When I shared news of the mathpocalypse with my son, he shrugged: it didn't diminish his interest in math. He and I have both been leery of AI's ubiquity and its potential to replace human relationships . But while reading "What Should My Children Do?" I began experimenting more with AI. One night, I called my son over to the screen and asked if there was an advanced math problem he wanted to learn. Linear equations, he said. I prompted Claude to come up with a "story or a set of images that can help a fifth grader grasp and conceptualize this middle-school idea." In a minute or two, Claude delivered an illustrated, interactive, multi-chapter story called "The Great Piggy Bank Race," and by the next day my son was solving linear equations on his own. He said he preferred working with a human teacher. But he acknowledged that it was pretty amazing — and freaky — to know you can turn to this technology at any time, to be taught anything.
