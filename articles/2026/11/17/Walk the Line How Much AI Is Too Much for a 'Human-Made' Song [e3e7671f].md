# Walk the Line: How Much AI Is Too Much for a 'Human-Made' Song?

PCMag · James Peckham · 2026-11-17

How would you feel if you discovered that Paul McCartney used ChatGPT to help write "Eleanor Rigby"? That’s unlikely, given it came out 56 years before the release of AI chatbots, but it's a question affecting a lot of artists, record labels, distributors, and other industry professionals. When does using AI tip a song into being AI-generated?
Tracking tools are increasingly used by the music industry to identify AI-generated music, but it's impossible to know when generative tools were used sparingly in the process. For example, if someone uses a chatbot to discuss melody ideas for a song, and then records the results on a real-life instrument, tracking tools wouldn't detect AI use. So, is the song still human-made?
The music industry has yet to define artificial music; for now, labels are adjusting their artist policies, and streaming services are developing ways to notify users about AI-generated music. Globally, audiophiles are gauging their tolerance for tunes not produced by humans.
Earlier this year, the Australian Recording Industry Association (ARIA) banned AI-generated music from charting after a cover of Madonna’s "Like A Prayer" by DJ Josh Fawaz was found to have used generative AI. ARIA rolled out new rules requiring songs to be “substantially human-made” and “raise no stream or chart manipulation concerns" in order to be included in its charts.
The Grammys did something similar in 2023 when it banned generative AI works from winning altogether. Its policies do allow the use of AI, but with tight restrictions. “The human authorship component of the work submitted must be meaningful," the latest Grammys rulebook says. The work must also be human-authored to be eligible for the category in which it’s nominated; for example, if an artist used AI to write song lyrics, it wouldn’t be eligible for a songwriting award.
Who Decides the Limit?
The problem with definitions from ARIA, the Grammys, and many others is that the line is blurry and open to misinterpretation and even manipulation. What makes for "substantially human-made" art, and what level of "human authorship component" should a song have to qualify? Is the onus on the record label, the artist, or the distributor?
Ditto Music is a distribution platform that helps artists get their work onto over 150 third-party streaming services, such as Apple Music and Spotify, as well as stores. Previous customers include Chance the Rapper, Ed Sheeran, Sam Smith, Tems, and other big names. Ditto Music says 47% of the independent artists it works with now use AI at some point in their process.
“Many artists are using AI to write or polish their words, while the melody, the performance and the overall vision are still very much their own,” Shaun Little, Director of Operations at Ditto Music, told PCMag. “AI is also now built into the production software most artists use every day, from mastering assistants to stem separation and virtual session players. Plenty of artists use it without even thinking of it as AI.”
Ditto Music allows AI-generated tracks to be uploaded to streaming platforms through its systems, but it requires artists to note when they’ve used AI tools in the process. It has three levels: fully AI-generated, partially AI-generated, or not at all. It asks artists to identify the use of models but also employs tools to run detection in parallel.
"If they choose partial, they define the AI status of each contributor on the release," says Little. "That’s because an artist who wrote their own melody but used AI on the lyrics is in a very different position to someone who generated the whole track from a prompt."
Ditto Music shares this information with partners, and some platforms then declare it to users, with the information appearing differently depending on which streamer or storefront you use.
“Most of this relies on people declaring honestly," says Little. “But I don’t think it will stay voluntary for long. Under the EU’s AI Act, AI tools already on the market must mark what they generate by December. Once that marking is built in at the source, it becomes much easier for distributors and platforms to verify what’s declared, and much harder to get it wrong.”
This Streamer Is Monitoring AI Music Closely
Streaming platform Deezer has developed a toggle that lets users filter out all AI-generated music from the service. Deezer doesn't ask artists to self-identify AI tracks; instead, it uses in-house tools that detect artifacts left in audio files by AI generation. However, Deezer admits its technology can’t track if AI was used elsewhere in the process.
“Unfortunately, we're no better than the rest of the industry when it comes to having a clear definition,” says Deezer’s head of research and development, Manuel Moussallam.
“I think there’s still a lot of debate on what [AI-generated music] means," he adds. "Even when you talk about fully AI-generated, it used to be something very easy to understand: it was just a prompt, and then you generate everything. As time goes by, and as these tools get more complicated, the gray area expands.”
Deezer is battling a tidal wave of AI-generated tracks, with over 50% of its daily uploads now flagged as AI-produced. It is working to make it clear to listeners when something is AI-generated, even to the point of removing songs from editorial playlists. It also found that total AI music streams across its platform are below 3%. It estimates that 85% of AI music streams are fraudulent, meaning someone uploads a track, uses bot-like tools to listen to it, and then pockets the royalties.
Deezer has found that changes to the market leader in AI-generated music tools, Suno, are affecting the number of uploads it receives. Suno severely limited the number of songs you can download via its tools as of Sept. 3, meaning customers on its free plan can only download seven tracks without subscribing.
"Now we're trying to see whether it will be replaced by other models and services, or if it'll plateau at that level," Moussallam said in October. "We don’t know yet, but the dynamic has changed in the past few weeks. It’s an interesting moment.”
What About Big-Name Artists?
Your favorite artists may already experiment with AI tools; Dr. Dre, Timbaland, Diplo, Teddy Swims, and David Guetta have all expressed varying levels of interest in applying AI to their music or processes. But it’s unlikely many household names will release fully AI-produced tracks.
No matter how ubiquitous AI tools become in music production, many artists will continue to avoid AI tools, especially if they're established and have creative reputations to protect.
As British DJ and record producer Fred Again.. recently tweeted, "The process [of making music] is the magic, [which is why I've] felt no pull whatsoever to even try [AI].
"The clumsy dedicated tumbling towards an idea isss the thing we are most lucky to get to do. The hours and hours and years and years of endlessly trying to make the thing you wish could exist," he adds.
