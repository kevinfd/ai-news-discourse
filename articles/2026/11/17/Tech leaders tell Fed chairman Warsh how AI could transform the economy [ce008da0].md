# Tech leaders tell Fed chairman Warsh how AI could transform the economy

Axios · Courtenay Brown · 2026-11-17

Just maybe, both the extreme techno-optimists and AI doom-and-gloomers are wrong about the economic impact of the technology. That's the argument that venture capitalist Marc Andreessen — who is co-leading a Federal Reserve task force on the topic — made in a high-profile panel Monday. Why it matters: The argument could help shape how the Fed assesses AI's economic consequences, with major implications for its outlook on growth, inflation and interest rates. Andreessen's argument offered an early glimpse of the thinking that will underpin his recommendations to the central bank's leaders. What they're saying: "My line has been both utopians and the doomers are going to be disappointed, specifically disappointed by the scope and magnitude of the real-world impact, and specifically the economic impact," Andreessen said at the San Francisco Fed. Andreessen, a co-leader of Fed chairman Kevin Warsh's Jobs and Productivity Task Force, spoke alongside former Commerce Secretary Gina Raimondo at a hearing in the Bay Area, where Warsh studied at Stanford and later spent 15 years teaching and conducting research. (Fellow task force member Chad Jones, a Stanford economist on leave for Anthropic, was in the audience but did not speak because the company is preparing to go public, Andreessen said.) Zoom in: Andreessen previewed his contribution to the task force's report, arguing that the economy is divided into two groups: Fast-changing industries, such as technology and media, where innovation drives productivity gains and lower prices. Slower-changing industries, including health care, education and housing, where regulations, limited competition and other barriers make technological progress harder. The intrigue: These slower-changing sectors account for some of Americans' biggest expenses. Those sectors of the economy "are not legally allowed to have rapid change," Andreessen said. Andreessen argued that regulations and other barriers could prevent AI from transforming large parts of the economy, which he says would limit job losses and productivity gains. " You might interpret this as good news because it means the AI revolution is not going to destroy all the jobs or kill everybody," he said. "You can also view this as very negative. Productivity growth just simply may not grow very much. Economic growth just simply may not grow very much. We may just continue to stagnate," he added. The big picture: Warsh is turning to influential outsiders to challenge how the central bank thinks about the economy. Affirm CEO Max Levchin told Warsh that AI has made the company's engineers five times more productive, cutting the cost of developing new features by 80%. Stripe CEO Patrick Collison said new businesses launching on its platform each week have doubled from a year ago, an unprecedented surge in its 15-year history. Unlike established companies, startups are more willing to let AI independently develop products and fix bugs, potentially allowing them to grow faster with fewer employees. What to watch: Executives said AI is already delivering productivity gains in traditional industries — including sectors Andreessen said are more resistant to technological change. Erik Wexler, CEO of Providence, a large nonprofit health system, said AI tools that transcribe patient appointments are saving some physicians two to four hours a day on paperwork. Wexler said that one doctor called it "life-changing." What's next: Warsh has asked all five task forces to recommend ways to improve Fed policymaking by year-end, though he cautioned last month that some may miss the deadline.
