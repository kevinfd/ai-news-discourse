# Universal and Sony fight Suno’s bid to freeze second lawsuit, warning its latest AI models could escape scrutiny until at least 2028

Music Business Worldwide · Mandy Dalugdug · 2026-11-17

Universal Music Group and Sony Music Entertainment are fighting Suno’s attempt to put their second copyright lawsuit against the AI music company entirely on hold.
In papers filed in Boston federal court last Friday (November 13), the labels argued that a full pause could keep the case frozen into 2028.
Under Suno’s plan, they claim, its 2025 and 2026 models and business conduct – including v6, the model suite it launched in September – would be “examined nowhere until at least 2028.”
The 17-page filing can be read in full here.
Suno asked Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV on October 30 to pause the second case until he rules on summary judgment in the labels’ first lawsuit, filed in June 2024.
Suno’s main defense in that case is fair use: it argues that training its AI on copyrighted recordings is lawful without a license.
Suno also said that if Saylor rules in its favor on fair use, or on the labels’ separate claim that it stream-ripped recordings from YouTube, the first case “could foreclose” the labels’ theories in the second.
The labels agree that the fair use ruling will shape the second case, and have offered to postpone the work of proving they own the more than 60,000 recordings at issue until Saylor rules.
“What Plaintiffs oppose is Suno’s request that this action be frozen in its entirety, for a period that could realistically extend into 2028, while Suno continues to train, release, license, and monetize models that require independent scrutiny,” the filing states.
In the first case, fact discovery on the labels’ infringement claims has largely closed, while discovery on their stream-ripping claim runs until this Friday (November 20). Summary judgment motions are due by June 22, 2027.
The second lawsuit, filed on September 18, accuses Suno of copying 60,202 of the labels’ recordings without a license to train its AI models.
US copyright law caps statutory damages for willful infringement at USD $150,000 per work, which gives those recordings a theoretical maximum of just over USD $9 billion.
The labels are also asking the court to order Suno to stop any further use of their recordings.
The new filing is about timing: if the labels get their way, Suno would have to hand over internal records on its later models, licensing efforts, fundraising, and finances while the first case is still running.
The dispute began in June 2024, when Universal, Sony, and Warner Music Group sued Suno.
Warner settled and struck a licensing deal with Suno in November 2025, and the case now covers 560 of Universal and Sony’s recordings.
In May this year, Universal and Sony asked to add 61,026 more recordings to the original case, after tracing them inside Suno’s training data. The second suit asserts slightly fewer, 60,202.
Saylor refused on August 18, saying that adding them “will have obvious consequences of complexity and delay,” and steered the labels toward a separate lawsuit instead.
In that ruling, Saylor wrote that evidence-gathering in any new case “could proceed on a different schedule, or indeed be stayed pending resolution of the fair use issue.”
Suno says that means the court has already contemplated a stay. The labels counter that a stay was only one of several options Saylor listed, and point out that he also said the new case “could also be potentially consolidated, in whole or in part,” with the first.
Suno launched its v6 models on September 9, nine days before the second suit was filed, saying they were built in partnership with Warner, BMG, and Believe.
In the first case – referred to in the filing as “Suno I” – the labels say Suno’s document handover largely stops at February 2025, and no witness has been put forward to explain how v6 was trained.
“Suno has taken the position in Suno I that the full picture of its 2025 and 2026 models and licensing conduct are outside the scope of that case,” the filing states. “It now asks the Court to stay the only action in which that conduct can be examined.
“Either that conduct is part of the fair use record in Suno I, in which case Suno should produce the discovery there, or it is not, in which case the discovery must proceed here. The stay Suno seeks would ensure that discovery on key issues proceeds nowhere, and it would lengthen the life of both cases on the Court’s docket.”
“The danger to Plaintiffs is concrete,” the labels warn. “By the time discovery into v6 could begin under Suno’s proposal, the engineers who built the models may have moved on, their recollections will have faded and the training pipelines they used may have changed significantly or been superseded.”
“Delay would not merely postpone building the record, it would degrade it,” Universal and Sony add.
“This concern is already manifesting, as Plaintiffs recently learned that the engineer responsible for Suno’s data scraping and training data acquisition has left the company,” the filing says.
In its motion, Suno said it was “retaining relevant data in accordance with its legal preservation obligations,” and that the record in the first case “will preserve testimony from past years.”
The labels reply that this covers documents, not what the engineers know, and that the first case’s record preserves testimony about past models, not the one Suno is selling now.
Instead of a full pause, the labels have proposed what they call a “partial stay” with three parts.
The first would put off proving ownership of the 60,000-plus recordings.
The second would see evidence-gathering go ahead now on four subjects: Suno’s later models, including how v6 was trained; its efforts to license training data; its moves into new businesses and markets, including funding rounds and investor pitches; and its recent finances.
The third would let everything handed over in the first case count in the second, so that neither Suno nor the labels have to do the same work twice.
In return, Universal and Sony would hand over evidence on the market for AI training-data licenses, including their own licensing of recordings to AI developers, and on how AI-generated music affects the value of their recordings.
If Saylor opts for a broader pause, the labels want him to let the first case’s record be used in the second immediately, and to order Suno to preserve the training data, saved model versions, user-interaction and preference data, and engineering documents behind every model it has released or is developing.
They also want any pause to lift automatically once Saylor rules on summary judgment in the first case, rather than after any appeals are exhausted, with a joint status report due within 14 days.
“In the two years since Suno I was filed, Suno has grown from a start-up into a company valued at approximately $5.4 billion with annual revenue in the hundreds of millions of dollars … and every month of stay is another month in which Suno entrenches a market position built on the recordings at issue in this case,” the filing states.
Suno raised more than USD $400 million at a USD $5.4 billion valuation in June.
In a footnote, the labels take aim at a Justice Department filing in the OpenAI copyright litigation that Suno cited in its motion. That filing criticized the “market dilution” theory the labels are pursuing: the argument that a flood of AI-generated music competing with their recordings weighs against fair use.
The labels say that filing doesn’t apply to Suno: it was made in a case about large language models trained on newspaper text, and rests on the government’s interest in AI dominance and national security, considerations that have no bearing on a self-described “creative entertainment platform.”
The executive branch does not speak with one voice on the issue, the labels contend, pointing to a sentencing submission from the same Justice Department in the case of Michael Smith, who was sentenced to 18 months in prison in October for a streaming-fraud scheme built on AI-generated tracks. Prosecutors argued there that because streaming royalties are paid from a fixed pool, those tracks took money that would otherwise have gone to artists whose music listeners actually played.
“Though Smith involved fraudulent streams, the royalty-pool structure the government described does not depend on how the streams are generated,” Universal and Sony write. “Every stream of an AI-generated track reduces the pro rata share paid to every human artist in the same period, and the capacity to generate tracks at volume is what makes such dilution possible at scale.”
The labels also note that Suno faces a string of other copyright suits, several of them filed in recent months, including separate claims filed on November 6 by Elvis Presley Enterprises and Eminem co-publisher Eight Mile Style.
Because Suno is likely to have to hand over evidence in at least some of those cases anyway, the labels say, it cannot claim that the targeted evidence-gathering proposed here is a hardship.
In its October 30 motion, Suno argued that without a stay, the parties would face “expensive, duplicative litigation of the same issues currently presented in” the first case, and that Saylor’s rulings there could “obviate the need for certain discovery” in the second.
When the second suit was filed, Suno said in a statement that the claims “remain fundamentally flawed on both the facts and the law,” as MBW reported at the time.
The labels’ filing concludes: “Having forced the resulting claims into a second case, and having insisted in Suno I that delay was a prejudice it should not bear, Suno should not be permitted to insist that the second case wait for the first, or that the conduct it has kept out of the first may be examined in neither.”Music Business Worldwide
