# Infinitesimals are essential to calculus. But are they even real?

Scientific American · Manon Bischoff · 2026-11-17

This article is from Proof Positive, our friendly newsletter that explores the joys and peculiarities of math. Sign up today for a weekly math essay and puzzle in your email inbox.
Last week, I recounted how Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz laid the foundations for calculus—and how they relied on the curious concept of infinitesimal quantities to do so. But infinitesimals, understood to be immeasurably small—almost but not quite zero in value—have been a challenging concept for mathematicians to accept.
On supporting science journalism
Archimedes considered this concept centuries before either Newton or Leibniz. He defined infinitesimals as follows: |dt| < 1/1, |dt| < 1 / (1 + 1), |dt| < 1 / (1 + 1 + 1),... And even having defined them, he doubted these quantities could actually exist.
Some mathematicians, such as Guillaume de l’Hôpital, were convinced of the existence of infinitesimals. Leibniz and Newton, however, expressed themselves more cautiously, despite relying on the concept in their work. Leibniz did not claim that the unusual quantities actually existed but that correct conclusions could be drawn with their help. Newton went further. Although his calculations in his book Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica were based on infinitesimals, he replaced them with ordinary calculations or simply omitted them before publication.
Meanwhile, in 1734, philosopher George Berkeley critiqued infinitesimals, noting that even the tiniest values played a crucial role in mathematics. From Berkeley’s perspective, calculating velocity at a specific moment—a crucial step in Newton’s work—would never give us an exact velocity but instead an approximation. He wrote, “They are neither finite quantities, nor quantities infinitely small, nor yet nothing. May we not call them the ghosts of departed quantities?”
In the 19th century German mathematician Karl Weierstrass found a way around infinitesimals altogether. To define quantities such as velocity at fixed times, as Newton did, or to calculate the area under a curve, as Leibniz demonstrated, Weierstrass used limits. This approach circumvented the problem but led to stilted definitions, such as: v is the desired velocity if, for every ε > 0, the magnitude of Δs ⁄ Δt = – v is less than ε for all values of |Δt | < δ, where δ > 0. Such epsilon-delta definitions are typically taught today in undergraduate courses. They work well but are not particularly elegant.
Making Sense of the Minuscule
Yet infinitesimals continued to be used. In fact, numerous advances have been made in physics and applied mathematics as a result.
In the 1960s mathematician Abraham Robinson succeeded in developing a mathematical theory of infinitesimals. He built upon the abstract insights of mathematician Kurt Gödel, who had found a way to use the tools of mathematics to reflect on the discipline itself.
Gödel's basic idea follows: a specific area of the discipline, such as standard calculus, can be considered a “universe,” M; we use a language, L, to make statements about such universes, including formulas and formal statements, each of which can be true or false. For example: 1 + 1 > 1 is true. All false statements can be negated so that they, too, become true statements—at least theoretically. (In reality, we don’t know all the true or false statements.)
If you collect every true statement of L, including the negated false ones, you create a single set, K. This set is the complete theory of M, a universe in which everything in Kholds true. But Gödel’s work makes it clear that K can have other models, such as M*. These models may be built from entirely different objects than M but obey the same relationships such that every statement in K remains true.
Let’s use an example to think that through further. Suppose M consists of all the fish within a lake. You can make all sorts of true statements about these aquatic animals, such as that one of the fish can swim faster than another. You can then imagine M* as the entire lake, including not just the fish but the water as well. Every statement about M could still apply to M*, even though the latter contains more than M.
In the same way, standard calculus with real numbers, M, can be extended to a larger universe, M*, which contains new kinds of numbers. Robinson developed this line of thinking using two core statements of logic:
- Completeness: a set of logical statements is consistent—meaning free of contradiction—if and only if a universe exists in which they are all true. (This idea also comes from Gödel.)
- Compactness: if every finite subset of a collection of statements is consistent, the whole collection is consistent, however large it may be. If you can satisfy any finite set of statements at once, there must exist a universe where every single statement holds simultaneously.
That second tenet is essential to creating infinitesimals. If we go back to Archimedes, he essentially described an infinitely small quantity as: |dt| < 1/1, |dt| < 1 / (1 + 1), |dt| < 1 / (1 + 1 + 1).... In our ordinary universe of real numbers, no number |dt| satisfies every one of these at once—which is why Archimedes doubted the existence of infinitesimals. But any finite handful of these statements is comparatively easy to satisfy. By compactness, that makes the entire infinite collection consistent. That means there must be some universe where all of these statements are true simultaneously. In that universe, |dt| is a genuine infinitesimal: it is smaller than every ordinary positivenumber yet is not zero. (The same line of thinking, run in reverse, leads to a universe in which infinitely large numbers also exist.)
So is everyone convinced? As I’ve written before, there are mathematicians who reject the concept of infinity. But most see infinities and infinitesimals as valid mathematical structures, even if they don’t always map easily onto our day-to-day reality. Meanwhile Robinson’s nonstandard calculus, in which infinitesimals exist, has not yet gained widespread acceptance. Although the theory is certainly interesting, calculations in this area are often extremely complex. Standard calculus methods are usually more suitable for proving its existence.
