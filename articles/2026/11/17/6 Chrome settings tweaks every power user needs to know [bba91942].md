# 6 Chrome settings tweaks every power user needs to know

ZDNet · Jack Wallen · 2026-11-17

ZDNET’s key takeaways
- If you want to get the most out of Chrome, consider these changes.
- You’ll get better security and privacy, fewer interruptions, and more done.
- All of these changes are simple to make.
Whether you’re a longtime Chrome user, or just now switching to Google’s browser, you might assume that Chrome is perfectly fine right out of the box. That’s understandable, given that Chrome is the most widely used browser on the planet.
Also: Chrome running slow? 5 easy ways to speed it up fast
But Chrome’s default experience could use some improvement (as is the case with most software). Where do you start? I’m going to address what I believe are the most important changes to make in Chrome, so I won’t talk about appearance, tabs, or speed. This is mostly about privacy, security, and minimizing general annoyances, so you can get the best experience with Google’s browser.
More from ZDNET
1. Enhanced or Standard Protection
The single most important thing you can do in any browser is to enable the best protection it offers for your needs. In Chrome, this all starts with Enhanced Protection. Note: Enhanced Protection is typically the default, but this setting is so important that you should check to ensure it’s enabled.
Chrome offers three different protection settings:
- Enhanced Protection: Warns about dangerous sites, runs in-depth scans for suspicious downloads, protects across Google services, improves general security, and sends URLs of sites you visit to Google Safe Browsing.
- Standard Protection: Protects against sites, downloads, and extensions.
- No Protection: does not protect you.

There is one thing to keep in mind about Enhanced Protection: you lose some privacy because more data is sent to Google. With Enhanced Protection enabled, you share what you download and view with Google. If privacy is as important to you as important security, you could drop down to Standard Protection, because it’s still going to protect you against most threats.
This setting is configured in Chrome > Settings > Privacy and security > Security.
2. Block third-party cookies
Third-party cookies are there to create profiles of you that can be used for marketing and targeting. Cookies track you and send information to third parties — information that you might not otherwise want them to have.
Blocking third-party cookies helps to prevent cross-site tracking, reduce targeted ads, protect your anonymity and privacy, avoid security vulnerabilities, and prevent fingerprinting. I always recommend that users disable third-party cookies in Chrome, even if it can cause some sites to not load properly. The reason: You simply can’t trust those third parties. Who are they, and who is handling the information saved in those cookies?

If disabling third-party cookies breaks certain sites in Chrome, open them in a different browser (that also has third-party cookies disabled). If that browser doesn’t work, try another.
That’s how strongly I feel about blocking third-party cookies.
To block third-party cookies in Chrome, go to Chrome > Settings > Privacy and security > Third-party cookies and make sure they are blocked.
3. Block location access
With location access enabled, you could find yourself in a situation where your privacy and safety are compromised. Why is this an issue? Well, location access tracks your physical whereabouts. Consider the following two scenarios:
- There’s a ne’er-do-well wanting to keep tabs on where you are. With enough information and skill, location access could help that person track you down.
- You’ve gone on vacation and are using Chrome with location access turned on. The wrong person could pinpoint your location, know that you’re out of town, and break into your house.
To avoid becoming a victim of such situations, all you have to do is block location access, which is done in Chrome > Settings > Privacy and security > Site settings > Permissions > Location. On that page, make sure “Don’t allow sites to see your location” is selected.
4. Disable password saving
Yes, this one’s an inconvenience, but a web browser’s built-in password manager is simply not as safe as a dedicated password manager like KeePassXC. A dedicated password manager does one thing, and does it well; it helps you manage your passwords in a protected, encrypted vault.
When you save passwords to your web browser, it is possible that a malicious site or script could access those passwords and wreak havoc on your life. When you use a dedicated password manager, that browser isn’t going to have direct access to your credentials, which is exponentially safer.

To disable password saving, go to Chrome > Passwords and autofill > Google Password Manager > Settings and click the On/Off slider for “Offer to save passwords and passkeys.”
5. Disable notifications
If you’re like me, a constant barrage of notifications is not just annoying — it’s distracting. Every time a notification pops up, my attention is stolen from whatever it is that I’m doing, which means I have to spend extra time getting back into the groove. If that happens constantly, I struggle to get anything done.
To that end, I disable notifications in my web browsers, my desktops, and even on the lock screen of my Android phones. I then get to decide when I’m distracted (and it usually happens after I finish a task).

To disable notifications in Chrome, go to Settings > Privacy and security > Site settings > Notifications and check “Don’t allow sites to send notifications.”
6. Enable ‘Ask where to save downloads’
If you allow Chrome to dictate where downloads are saved, you could wind up with a directory full of random things. I like to be the conductor of those downloads and where they are saved for two reasons:
- It prevents a single directory from getting crammed full of unrelated items.
- It allows me to keep downloads organized.
For example, I download a lot of .deb and .AppImage files on Linux, and I certainly don’t want them all going into the Downloads directory. I also download a lot of documents and ISO images, neither of which should be saved to Downloads. If I left Chrome as is, all those files would be saved in Downloads, which would not only fill that directory but also make it harder for me to find what I’m looking for. Instead, I tell Chrome to ask me where to save every downloaded file.

To enable “Ask where to save downloads,” go to Settings > Downloads and make sure “Ask where to save each file before downloading” is enabled.
