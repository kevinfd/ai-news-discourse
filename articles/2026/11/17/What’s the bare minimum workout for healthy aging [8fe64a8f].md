# What’s the bare minimum workout for healthy aging?

Scientific American · Lori Youmshajekian · 2026-11-17

“Aim for the bare minimum” is rarely good advice for achieving anything. But when it comes to exercise, there is encouraging news for the less athletically inclined: even a little bit seems to help us stay healthy as we age.
The World Health Organization and guidelines for physical activity in the U.S. agree that adults should do at least 150 minutes per week of brisk walking or other moderate activity or spend 75 minutes doing more vigorous exercise, such as running or swimming laps. On top of this, they call for weight lifting, push-ups, or other strengthening exercises two or more days a week. Fewer than half of Americans hit that target, but the good news is that aiming to get even a fraction of the recommended exercise still lowers the risk of disease and early death. Studies differ on how long, hard and often we need to be exercising, but taken together, they suggest that minimal exercise can still be beneficial, experts say, especially for those who rarely incorporate activity into their lives.
The standard 150 minutes a week is a “pretty high bar for a lot of people to reach,” says Keith Diaz, who studies sedentary behavior at Columbia University, “and time is one of the biggest barriers.” Diaz was on a treadmill when we spoke, walking at two miles per hour. He prioritizes movement that is practical and easy, and for most people, that means walking. We evolved to get movement throughout the day, and “we need to give our bodies more of that,” he says.

Diaz’s laboratory experiments show just how little it takes. In a small 2023 trial, 11 middle-aged or older adults sat for eight hours with different schedules of walking breaks. Walking for five minutes every half hour reduced spikes in blood glucose after meals by more than 50 percent compared with levels during uninterrupted sitting. And even one minute of walking every hour lowered blood pressure, which, if sustained, could decrease long-term risk of cardiovascular events.
I-Min Lee, an epidemiologist at Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Harvard Medical School, came to the field having done very little sport, so she relates to the idea that exercise is a tough sell for many (and prefers the more palatable term “physical activity”). In 2015 Lee co-authored an analysis of data from more than 660,000 people. Those who did some activity but still less than the recommended minimum had a 20 percent lower risk of death compared with those who did nothing. In other words, those who didn’t do any activity were more likely to die over the 14-year follow-up period. “If you are at a very sedentary point, doing any amount is helpful,” Lee says.
Strength training seems to follow a similar pattern. A 2022 review found that 30 to 60 minutes a week of weight lifting, push-ups, squats, and other strength training was linked to up to a 20 percent reduction in risk of death from any cause during the studies’ time frames. There was no clear benefit to increasing the exercise time beyond an hour. A different study that followed people for three decades found that those who did 1.5 to two hours of resistance training per week had a 13 percent lower risk of all-cause mortality; more than two hours didn’t show additional benefits.
Working harder may deliver more benefits for the time spent. In nonexercisers, three bursts of vigorous activity every day—running up stairs or for the bus—was linked to an up to 40 percent lower risk of death over an average of seven years. As little as 15 minutes of high activity a week was correlated with an 18 percent lower risk of death from any cause and 16 percent lower risk of dying from cancer. At a national scale, these small amounts could add up to a lot. Researchers estimate that if middle-aged and older Americans added 10 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous activity, about 111,000 deaths a year could be prevented.
Lee is less certain that intensity is all that important. Most data suggest that the total amount of time you spend active matters most, she says. A 2022 study that followed more than 100,000 people found that those who met the guidelines with moderate activity had about the same drop in mortality risk as those meeting them with vigorous activity.
This is part of the defense of the “weekend warrior,” someone who makes up for a sedentary week with exercise on the weekend. In 2004 Lee and her colleagues found that men who crammed their activity into one or two sessions a week had lower death rates than sedentary men only if they had no major risk factors. Another study found male and female weekend warriors have a 30 percent lower risk of death from all causes compared with inactive people; regularly active people had a 35 percent lower risk. That analysis counted housework as well as walking and sports and concluded that even one or two sessions a week can improve mortality risk. Studies using wearables that measure movement have found similar drops in heart attack, stroke and death among active participants, whether that activity was bunched or spread out through the week. Still, Lee would rather that people just getting started spread out their physical activity, because they will be less likely to get injured as their bodies adapt to the increased workload.
Doing the bare minimum should be a starting point, not the goal, says Emmanuel Stamatakis, a physical activity researcher at Monash University in Australia. “Benefits continue to accrue up to about 300 minutes of moderate activity per week or its vigorous equivalent,” and most people can build up to this gradually, he says.
Achieving this doesn’t have to involve a gym. “You can also be healthy by just finding ways to move throughout your day,” Diaz says. The consensus is that something is better than nothing. If you like to go ballroom dancing, “hey, that counts,” Lee says. So does walking your dog or gardening. The best activity is the one you like to do and will do over the long term, she says. For people who are seriously pressed for time, Diaz says, “the bar doesn’t have to be that high.”
