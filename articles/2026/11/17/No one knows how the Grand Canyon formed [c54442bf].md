# No one knows how the Grand Canyon formed

Scientific American · Stephanie Pappas · 2026-11-17

One monsoonal day in August, a puffy white cloud wafted a meager curtain of rain onto the desert of northern Arizona. Between me and that arid expanse yawned the Grand Canyon, the muddy Colorado River twisting at its base.
I was standing at Desert View Point, the easternmost overlook in Grand Canyon National Park, above stately red-rock cliffs that dropped off into an abyss nearly a mile deep. The first Spaniards who came here, in 1540, couldn’t conceive that the river at the bottom was more than six feet across until they clambered down the cliffs and finally accepted what the Indigenous people living nearby had tried to tell them: the canyon is both deeper and wider than the mind can easily grasp.
But Karl Karlstrom, a geologist with a shock of white hair and a squint that betrays a lifetime of surveying harsh desert vistas, was bedeviled by other concerns. The informational signs in the national park don’t dwell on this, but the Colorado River’s path through its most famous creation doesn’t make a lot of sense. Before it arrives at Lees Ferry, the beginning of the Grand Canyon, the river takes a southerly path. But then, here at the eastern edge of the park, the Colorado suddenly veers west—right through the 9,200-foot Kaibab Plateau.

This is what Karlstrom and his wife, geochemist Laura Crossey, both recently retired from the University of New Mexico, had brought me there to see. At Desert View you can trace the curve of the land from the North Rim to the South Rim and understand that when you’re standing on the edge of the Grand Canyon, you’re also standing on the slope of a dome-shaped plateau. By all logic of water and gravity, the Colorado River should be somewhere else, having found a simpler path downhill that didn’t involve barreling its way through this bulwark of high ground. “Why does the crazy river come south and then all of a sudden take a hard right and go across an uplift?” Karlstrom had asked when we’d first spoken about this conundrum. He grew up near the Grand Canyon and worked as a river guide before he became a geologist; now he’s joined the scientific argument about this problem of the Grand Canyon—a dispute that’s been ongoing for more than 150 years.
A decade ago Karlstrom thought he had it solved. But new evidence has flared the debate once again, and it doesn’t center on the canyon at all. As we looked out, he gestured toward the empty landscape to the east, a vast desert basin that seemed desperate to absorb any water the puffy little rain cloud might deign to release.
“Bidahochi land,” Karlstrom said, “is all out there.”
The origin of the Grand Canyon might seem easy to figure out: rivers carve canyons, and the Colorado River carved this one. But that can’t be the full story. Not all rivers form canyons; you need not just water but upward movement, or uplift, of the surrounding land for the river to cut into. Hard rock and aridity help—without them, vegetation absorbs water, and soft sediment tends to crumble into gentle slopes rather than form dramatic cliffs. In that sense the story of the Grand Canyon is the story of the American West: its climate, its tectonic forces and the way the Colorado River gathered itself into a single ribbon from its headwaters in the Rocky Mountains to its terminus in the Pacific.
All of this happened millions of years before humans even evolved. The first of our species to encounter the canyon would have found it largely as it exists today. Many Indigenous tribes in and around the canyon have origin stories of its creation. The Hopi, for example, tell of two warrior brothers who built it with mud and lightning.
Since geologist and one-armed Civil War veteran John Wesley Powell made his famous river expedition through the canyon, in 1869, many geologists have hypothesized about the Grand Canyon’s origin. Powell thought the river got there first and the Kaibab Plateau rose around it, which turned out to be incorrect. Other geologists wondered whether the river first established itself west of the Kaibab and then ate eastward through the plateau before connecting with rivers on the other side, a process called headward erosion. One theory holds that the Colorado River initially dove under the plateau, flowing through cave networks and collapsing the formation from below.
“Why does the crazy river come south and then all of a sudden take a hard right and go across an uplift?” —Karl Karlstrom University of New Mexico
Regardless, the story starts about 70 million years ago, when a mountain-building event called the Laramide orogeny shoved up the Rocky Mountains and began uplifting the nearby Colorado Plateau, which makes up the modern-day Four Corners region. Today the Colorado River flows down the slope created by this orogeny, starting as streams that dance through Rocky Mountain National Park in Colorado at an elevation of around 12,000 feet before slicing through the roughly 8,000-foot-high Colorado Plateau via the Grand Canyon and ending—if agricultural withdrawals allow—at sea level in the Gulf of California.
Some researchers think that the modern-day canyon dates back 70 million years and that the Colorado River didn’t carve most of it at all. Instead, they propose, it would have been made by rivers flowing in the opposite direction back when the dinosaurs still roamed Earth. “At some later point the Colorado River opportunistically exploited this older canyon system,” says Rebecca Flowers, a geochronologist at the University of Colorado Boulder whose research on the cooling of minerals in canyon rocks supports this view.
Many geologists disagree. The topography of the Grand Canyon “screams young canyon,” says Kelin Whipple, a geomorphologist at Arizona State University, and the mineral-cooling methods that support the older date aren’t precise. We’ll let this argument lie because even old-canyon proponents such as Flowers agree that the eastern part of the Grand Canyon was carved mostly in the past six million years. And that eastern part, where the river slices through the Kaibab Plateau, is the subject of today’s most vociferous debates.
So: Most geologists pin the beginning of the Grand Canyon’s carving to about six million years ago, and the process has been ongoing ever since. Seasonal floods send boulders careening down the canyon, and the rocks chisel off pieces of the riverbed. But much of the geological evidence of this process has been lost to the desert Southwest’s incredibly erosive landscape. By one estimate, the Colorado River has flushed stone and sediment out of the canyon amounting to some 800 cubic miles of missing evidence. “A lot of what would inform this story has been removed by the incision that made the Grand Canyon so spectacular,” geologist Ryan Crow explained to me from his office at the U.S. Geological Survey in Flagstaff, Ariz.
Instead geologists look up- and downstream, chasing ghost rivers in grains of sand. Sediments in southwestern Colorado, near modern-day Grand Junction, reveal that the Colorado River was there 11 million years ago. Sediments from west of the Grand Canyon, near Lake Mead, reveal that the river had exited the canyon by 5.2 million years ago. Where it flowed for the intervening five million years, when the footprint of the canyon was established, was a mystery until just this year.
Two days after Karlstrom, Crossey and I beheld the Bidahochi basin from the Grand Canyon rim, I was deep in the basin itself, tromping up Balakai Mesa on the heels of John Douglass, a professor of geography at Paradise Valley Community College in Phoenix. Douglass, like Karlstrom, comes at his work with abiding love for the Grand Canyon. He also worked as a canyon guide in his youth, leading backpacking trips. He planned to become a snow scientist, but a class on the canyon’s formation derailed him entirely. He couldn’t believe there were so many mysteries about its origin. “I dreamt about it as I slept,” he says.
We were 100 miles as the crow flies from Desert View and more than a three-hour drive from Flagstaff. In his beat-up Toyota Tacoma, Douglass had maneuvered us carefully over rutted dirt roads through remote Navajo country. The landscape was sagebrush, greasewood and endless horizon. Occasionally—very occasionally—we passed an off-the-grid home or a Navajo dwelling called a hogan. Douglass had a permit to work on the reservation, but any resident could order him to leave at will, which had happened before. That day the only person we saw was a tiny old woman who mistook us for people who were supposed to be bringing her a ladder. When she found out Douglass was instead a geologist, she dismissed us with a scornful wave. Later we saw her, ladder acquired, reroofing her own house.
Now on foot, we skirted around delicate clumps of blue grama grass, its eyelashlike seed heads nodding as we passed. Most of the way up the mesa, the sand under my boots suddenly softened. “Feel the sand change?” Douglass said. “That’s Colorado River sand.”
I felt like I’d met a celebrity. The Colorado River? Here? But it was nothing compared with how geologists felt about this discovery, which was announced in two separate papers published earlier this year. For the first, a team led by Crow, Douglass and geologist John He of the University of California, Los Angeles, tracked the Colorado by sifting through the sand here and at other nearby mesas, looking for tiny minerals called zircons. Zircons are tough, eroding out of rocks but maintaining their own individual history, which means researchers can track their ages by measuring the radioactive decay of isotopes inside their crystals. In the study, published in April in the journal Science, the researchers found zircons known to be unique to Colorado River sands in the Bidahochi, much farther east than the river’s current path. The youngest zircons revealing the river’s presence date back 6.6 million years.
The path of the river during that crucial gap was coming into focus. Then a second study, led by Karlstrom and published in July in Nature Communications, used a different set of minuscule grains called sanidines to re-create the river’s first route. Sanidines are more abundant than zircons, but scientists only recently figured out how to easily pick them out from the many other clearish minerals found in sand. Karlstrom’s group realized that a subset of sanidines originated from volcanic fields in what is now Idaho and were carried downstream by the developing Colorado River. Where these grains appear, the Colorado River must have been. The researchers tracked the river’s development through land and time, finding that it entered Utah between 25 million and eight million years ago, then the Bidahochi basin upstream of the Grand Canyon seven million to six million years ago, finally flowing downstream of the canyon 4.8 million years ago.
The new data reveal that the river flowed through the Bidahochi, far from where it runs today, before it made it to the Grand Canyon and that it formed in a neat process from its headwaters to its terminus. Even better, the results essentially nail down the Colorado River’s path right up to the moment it hit the Grand Canyon. Now instead of five million missing years of river history, researchers are lacking only about a million or two—the time in which the river was within the canyon itself.
This brings me to the bigger reason Douglass brought me to Balakai Mesa: although it’s now agreed that the Colorado River got here between six million and seven million years ago, poised to start carving the Grand Canyon, how it cut across the Kaibab is still contentious.
Karlstrom, Crossey and their colleagues think that once it arrived, the Colorado turned the Bidahochi into a marshland. There were bogs, streams, rhinoceroses (which left fossils), small lakes, and—crucially—an ancient, preexisting canyon that ran right where the eastern part of the Grand Canyon does today. The evidence for this original canyon comes from a geological method called thermochronology. As rocks rise toward Earth’s surface, they cool, and minerals within them preserve evidence of that cooling. By measuring the radioactive decay of isotopes in these minerals, researchers can reconstruct this cooling history and estimate when processes such as uplift or erosion brought the rocks to the surface.
Geologists look up- and downstream, chasing ghost rivers in grains of sand.
Thermochronology suggests that a now vanished river carved a canyon across the Kaibab 25 million to 15 million years ago. At the Desert View Watchtower, Karlstrom had pointed above the horizon to show me where this East Kaibab paleocanyon would have been. Given how much ground has eroded away in those 25 million years, the canyon would have started above our heads and cut down more than 2,000 feet below today’s South Rim. The Colorado River could have found this canyon, its much needed low spot, and used it to traverse the Kaibab Plateau. As it did, it would have carved deeper, creating the Grand Canyon as we see it now. It’s an idea Karlstrom first proposed in 2014, and to his mind, the new data on the arrival of the Colorado River put everything in the right place at the right time for the paleocanyon to get the river across the Kaibab.
This explanation drives Douglass crazy. “There was a canyon already here,” he said while sitting on the tailgate of his truck in the Bidahochi. “Okay, well, great. What carved that canyon?”
As far as Douglass is concerned, Karlstrom’s paleocanyon solves nothing because whatever river carved that canyon would also have had a plateau in its way. “You kicked the entire problem that has fascinated everybody under the rug,” he says.
Thermochronology is an inexact science, and it’s possible to look at the data and conclude that there wasn’t a paleocanyon at all, which is what Douglass has done. Anyway, he told me, there doesn’t need to be a paleocanyon, because instead there was a really big lake.
At the top of Balakai Mesa, in the heart of the basin, the sand steepened into an outcrop that looks like rock from a distance but crumbles at the touch of a fingertip. Luckily, few fingertips make it out this way, and a cap of hard calcium carbonate rock protects the bed from rain and wind. An exposed surface shows linear deposits of sand converging at angles, a pattern known as cross-bedding. This happens at sandbars on lakeshores, Douglass explained, and the size of the cross-beds here indicates large waves. Large waves mean a long lake similar in size to today’s Great Salt Lake. The mesa may preserve a snippet of this long-lost lake’s shore.
Douglass argues that a large lake filling the Bidahochi basin could have climbed up the Kaibab Plateau’s eastern slope until it spilled over the top, eroding as it went. He and his colleagues have studied variations of oxygen here and at nearby outcrops that seem to suggest cycles of the lake shallowing and then refilling with fresh water. That, he argues, is an indication that the lake was spilling out over the Kaibab, which would cause the water level in the lake to drop until the flow of the river poured in new sediment and new water, pushing both lake bed and lake surface upward until the water spilled again. Over about 1.7 million years, he says, the water made its way through what is now the Grand Canyon, the first cut of a chasm.
A lake so large that it would have filled the Bidahochi basin is a controversial idea. Traditionally, the basin has been seen as capable of holding only a very shallow or ephemeral lake, says Whipple, the geomorphologist, though it is possible that the area’s intense erosion has wiped away strong evidence of a large body of water.
For their part, Karlstrom and Crossey are entirely unconvinced by the notion of a slow, cyclical spillover. Lake spillover does happen to connect lake basins together into rivers; in fact, spillovers are known to have happened below the Grand Canyon to connect the Colorado River to the Gulf of California. But those spillovers are often catastrophic, leaving rubble and debris as evidence. “The second that lake starts moving through, it cuts like a knife,” Crossey says.
Karlstrom and Crossey also object to the interpretation of the rocks here as beach sand, arguing that they could be younger stream or river deposits. It’s impossible to know exactly how old the cross-bedded sand formations are, and the mesas are so scattered that it’s hard to establish how their geological layers relate to one another. It’s also unclear whether these deposits really correspond to the height of the Kaibab six million years ago, given the region’s active tectonics, which can shift the landscape up and down.

Besides, can we wave away the thermochronological evidence of East Kaibab paleocanyon so easily? With an ancient canyon already carved, a giant lake couldn’t have pooled behind the Kaibab six million years ago; smaller lakes might have existed, but the canyon would have acted like a drain. “We just don’t think this lake could have been an immense lake like they think or that it was what caused the Grand Canyon to form,” Karlstrom says. To his mind, the important forces for explaining the first cut across the Kaibab lie under Earth’s crust. The ancient river he thinks cut the paleocanyon would have traversed a landscape dramatically different from today’s, and the Kaibab might not have been much of a barrier to its path. Only later, in the past eight million years, would tectonic forces have raised the Colorado River’s headwaters in the Rocky Mountains and caused other parts of the landscape—such as, perhaps, the Bidahochi basin—to sag. All these forces would have pulled the Colorado River into a single strand and directed it through ancient low spots such as the East Kaibab paleocanyon.
At one point or another in our hours of conversation, each member of Team Paleocanyon and Team Lake Bidahochi paused to ask me whether their differences seemed trivial. Douglass wryly told me about his college-age daughter, who often accompanies him during fieldwork but retains a healthy skepticism of her father’s obsession. “My daughter would be like, So there was a lake in both?” he said. “I’m like, yeah, but in one there was already this notch in the rim!”
“Is this all semantic? I even wonder this,” Karlstrom asked. Crow thinks there doesn’t need to be a debate; perhaps both lake and paleocanyon existed, and the paleocanyon provided the path for lake spillover. “I think we agree that multiple processes could have been involved,” Crow says of the two camps.
Maybe it’s a good sign that the differences are subtle. Maybe over 150 years’ worth of symposia and dueling research papers, scientists have been carving away at their hypotheses and getting closer to a consensus on the canyon’s origin. But the two competing visions also point to vastly different ancient landscapes and, ultimately, two distinct solutions to the mystery of how water made it over the barricade of the Kaibab. I left Douglass in the Bidahochi basin, where he was preparing to scout for more ancient shorelines amid the sagebrush. And when I said goodbye to Karlstrom and Crossey on the South Rim, it was to send them off to the same lonely desert on the hunt for the braids of ancient rivers.
Left on my own, I took a moment on the Grand Canyon’s rim. A family walked up to the overlook and peered down at the far-off Colorado. The father mused, to no one in particular, “That little river carved all this.”
Well, maybe. Is it less impressive if it was a giant lake first? Or if another river gave it a head start? Perhaps it’s just a testament to our good luck to be here in the geological moment when the Grand Canyon looks like this, awe-inspiring beyond imagination, dropping away into incomprehensibility.
