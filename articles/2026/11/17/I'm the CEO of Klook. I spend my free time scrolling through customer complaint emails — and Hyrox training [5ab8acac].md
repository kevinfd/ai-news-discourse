# I'm the CEO of Klook. I spend my free time scrolling through customer complaint emails — and Hyrox training.

Business Insider · Aditi Bharade, Aditi Bharade (abharade@businessinsider.com) · 2026-11-17

Klook This as-told-to essay is based on a conversation with Ethan Lin, the 40-year-old cofounder and CEO of travel platform Klook, who is based in Hong Kong. The following has been edited for length and clarity. In 2014, I left a stable banking job to cofound Klook, a platform where people can book travel experiences. I was 27, had no experience in tech or product, and bootstrapped the business with the savings my cofounders and I had built up. Now, the company is almost 2,000 employees-strong across 25 offices. As a 40-year-old CEO, I spend most of my time on work and fitness. Here's how I bring my A-game on busy work days. 
 
 Business Insider's Power Hours series gives readers an inside look at how powerful people structure their workday. Reach out to editor Lauryn Haas to share your daily routine. 
 
 An 8 a.m. dopamine boost Fitness is really important to me. It's a dopamine boost in the morning; it works better than coffee. Each week, I go to the gym five to six times, mostly in the morning before work, around 7 or 8 a.m. That helps me wake up. I have a light meal, and then I hit the gym for some strength training. I participated in Hyrox in Bangkok a few weeks ago. Klook is Hyrox's travel partner across Asia, and I like to show support. After my morning workout, I have a light meal that helps me start the day feeling very energetic. I reach the office around 9 a.m., then AI automations help me kick off I have several AI projects in the works that help me keep on top of my game. I have an AI automation set up that sends me a weekly Monday morning summary email, giving me a quick refresh on what's happening each week. Other AI automations work like think tanks for me, acting like a consultant, industry expert, communication strategist, analyst, and so on, brainstorming with me. One thing I enjoy about AI is that it feels like you're working with many experts every day. When it comes to drafting emails, I feed AI the skeletons of the content and structure and let it write a draft for me because English isn't my first language, but the critical thinking and judgment have to come from me. I need to remind myself to be mindful because they all sound right, and I need to be the one deciding what information is really suitable for me. Before AI, when you worked on a project or task or sent emails, you would get a few hours or days of break before you got a response, or the next piece of information. Right now, that part is pretty much instant, so we end up basically working nonstop. I feel like I'm being mentally challenged more and more every day. I scroll through customer complaint emails — and enjoy reading them I'm copied on all Klook customer inquiry or complaint email chains, and I enjoy reading them every day as they come in. In the last 12 years, that's one habit I haven't changed. The emails often look like: "I booked a day for this activity, but can I change to another day? The system doesn't support it," or "I wrote my name wrong. Can I get entry into this venue without the right name? Can you change it?" It's something AI can help summarize, but I like to get them randomly every day, even on weekends, because you cannot predict when a customer will submit a complaint. It's like TikTok — swipe, swipe, you never know what's coming next. It's quite addictive. I don't respond directly to them — that would be a bit awkward — but scrolling through complaints gives me enough frequent data points and real case studies to get a sense of our customers' problems. When we actually conduct analyses or prioritize which problems to solve, I have enough tangible examples. I'm in the office until at least 7 p.m. Most days, I'm in the office until 7 to 8 p.m., although other days can get quite late. That's also why I try to protect my morning workouts to kick-start the day, as my evening workouts aren't guaranteed. As a CEO, my time is very fragmented : I spend half an hour on one task, 15 minutes on another, and an hour on something else. I get sucked into a lot of projects and meetings but often don't have time to sit and think about how they're linked to see if anything's missing. Every week, I block off two to three hours from my calendar for personal reflection. I'll use that to finish a project or task, or consolidate information. My second workout, followed by bed at 1 a.m. After work, about twice a week at night, I hit cardio. I do a light zone-two run . That means a four- to eight-kilometer run at a not-too-crazy pace, depending on how busy my day has been. This helps me build endurance and sleep better, although I'm already a pretty deep sleeper. The last hour or so of my day is my quiet time. This involves some email clean-up, prepping for the next day, and reading things outside technology and travel to give myself a different perspective.
